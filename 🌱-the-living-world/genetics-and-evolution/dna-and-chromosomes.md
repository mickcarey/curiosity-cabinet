# DNA: How a Seed Gets the Sunflower Instructions 🧬🌻

Plant a sunflower seed and you might get a sunflower. You won't get a frog. Something inside that tiny parcel helps its cells build the right kind of living thing.

Part of the answer is **DNA**, short for **deoxyribonucleic acid**. That's a large name for a molecule that stores inherited information. You don't have to memorise the name. The interesting bit is how chemistry can carry a message.

## 🔤 Four letters, very long messages

A molecule is a group of joined atoms, the tiny pieces ordinary matter is made from. DNA is a long molecule with two strands, usually twisted together like a spiral ladder.

Its rungs contain pairs of chemical pieces we label **A, T, G and C**. These aren't actual printed letters. They are a handy way of recording which chemical piece occurs where.

The order matters. Just as the same alphabet can make different stories, different orders of DNA's pieces carry different information.

A always pairs with T, and G with C. That gives the molecule a wonderful copying trick. Separate the two strands and each can guide the construction of its missing partner. A–C–G–T needs T–G–C–A beside it.

## 📚 DNA, genes, chromosomes: who's who?

Your body is made of **cells**, tiny living units. Most contain a **nucleus**, a compartment holding most of their DNA.

A **gene** is a stretch of DNA that a cell can use to make a working molecule. Many genes help specify proteins: molecules that do jobs such as building structures and speeding up chemical reactions.

A **chromosome** is a long DNA molecule packaged with proteins. A **genome** is the complete set of genetic material.

Think of a library: DNA is the information-bearing material, chromosomes organise it into volumes, and genes are particular useful passages. The comparison has limits—cells need machinery to read the information, and a gene rarely explains a whole feature by itself.

Most human body cells have 23 pairs of chromosomes, with one set originally coming from the egg and one from the sperm. Other species have different numbers. More volumes doesn't automatically mean a more complicated creature.

## 🧵 The packing problem is ridiculous

The DNA in a typical human cell with two chromosome sets would stretch roughly two metres. Its nucleus is only a few millionths of a metre across.

It fits by wrapping around proteins and folding into carefully organised arrangements. The cell must also reach particular stretches when needed. Imagine storing a very long thread while still being able to find one precise bit without emptying the cupboard.

The familiar X-shaped chromosome is a special, tightly packed stage after copying. Chromosomes don't spend their whole lives as little floating Xs.

## 🛠️ Instructions need a reader

A cell copies selected information into **RNA**, or **ribonucleic acid**, a related molecule. Some RNA messages go to **ribosomes**, tiny structures that assemble proteins from smaller pieces called amino acids.

Different cells use different selections. A nerve cell and a muscle cell can hold largely the same DNA but make different tools. [Gene regulation](gene-expression-and-regulation.md) explains how that happens.

Some DNA helps control when genes are used. Some produces working RNA instead of protein. Other stretches have no demonstrated useful role. DNA is more varied than a wall-to-wall collection of protein recipes.

## ✏️ Copies aren't always exact

Cells check and repair DNA, but changes still happen. A change in its sequence is a **mutation**. Some matter greatly; many have little detectable effect. Changes that reach eggs or sperm can sometimes be passed to offspring.

Chromosome ends have protective structures called **telomeres**, which can shorten during repeated cell division. They matter to cell behaviour, but aren't a single timer counting down a person's life.

There is even a small extra collection of DNA inside mitochondria, the compartments that help cells use food energy. Their DNA is a clue to their [bacterial ancestry](../cells-and-chemistry/cells-and-organelles.md).

A seed needs water, energy and suitable conditions as well as inherited information. DNA helps make a sunflower possible; the seed still needs a world to grow in.
