# Soil: Structure, Chemistry and Living Communities 🪱

Soil contains mineral particles, organic material, water, air and organisms. Its usefulness to a plant depends on how these interact. A soil can contain abundant water that roots cannot access, or abundant phosphorus that remains chemically unavailable.

The spaces between particles are as important as the particles themselves. To a root, a compacted soil can be full of resources and still be a terrible address.

## ✋ Texture and structure are different

**Texture** describes the proportions of sand, silt and clay-sized mineral particles. Sand is relatively coarse; clay is very fine. Classification systems use slightly different size boundaries, so a number should be read with its system attached.

Coarse sandy soils usually have low water retention and drain quickly. Fine-textured soils hold more water, but some is held too tightly for plants to extract. Clay mineral type also affects behaviour.

**Structure** describes how particles form aggregates and how pores connect between and within them. Two soils with similar texture can behave differently because one has stable aggregates and the other is compacted or dispersed.

Large pores help drainage and aeration; smaller pores retain water. Root channels, animal activity, fungal hyphae and organic compounds all influence the arrangement. The practical field version is [reading soil](forests/reading-soil.md).

## 🌧️ Soil develops through interacting causes

Hans Jenny described soil formation in terms of **climate, organisms, relief, parent material and time**. These factors remain a useful way to think about a profile.

A young soil may strongly reflect its parent rock. Long weathering and leaching can remove readily soluble nutrients and leave different minerals behind. Slope position affects erosion, deposition and water movement. Organisms add carbon, alter chemistry and move material.

Horizons record some of this history. A dark surface layer may contain organic matter; a pale layer may have lost material through leaching; a deeper layer may accumulate clay or iron compounds. Not every profile contains the same sequence, and imported fill can interrupt it completely.

## ⚡ Chemical availability is not the same as total content

**pH** influences nutrient solubility, mineral reactions and biological activity. Strong acidity can increase aluminium toxicity in some soils. High pH can make iron and other nutrients less available to susceptible plants. Different communities are adapted to different ranges.

**Cation exchange capacity**, or CEC, describes the capacity of soil surfaces to retain exchangeable positively charged ions. Clay minerals and organic matter contribute, but their capacities differ. Calcium, magnesium, potassium and ammonium can participate in this exchange.

Nitrate is negatively charged and is often relatively mobile. Phosphate behaves differently again, binding strongly to some mineral surfaces or forming sparingly soluble compounds. “The soil holds nutrients” is therefore too broad unless we ask which nutrient and by what mechanism.

**Salinity** and **sodicity** also differ. Dissolved salts can make water harder for plants to obtain. Excess exchangeable sodium can disperse susceptible clays and damage structure. They can occur together, but are not interchangeable diagnoses.

## 🔁 Nitrogen moves through several forms

Atmospheric nitrogen gas is abundant but inaccessible to most organisms. Certain microbes fix it into ammonia using nitrogenase. Plants acquire nitrogen mainly through compounds such as ammonium and nitrate, not by extracting N₂ directly from air.

Decomposition releases ammonium from organic material through **mineralisation**. Microbes can also take up mineral nitrogen and incorporate it into their own biomass, called **immobilisation**.

During **nitrification**, microbes oxidise ammonia through nitrite towards nitrate. Under suitable low-oxygen conditions, **denitrification** reduces nitrate towards gaseous products, including nitrogen gas and sometimes nitrous oxide. Other pathways, including anammox, make the complete cycle more complex than a single loop.

Water movement can carry nitrate away before a root captures it. Nitrogen cycling therefore connects soil management to streams, estuaries and [coastal productivity](coasts/kelp-seagrass-mangroves-and-estuaries.md).

## 🪨 Phosphorus moves on a different timetable

Weathering of phosphorus-containing minerals supplies phosphate. Biological recycling returns it from dead cells and organic molecules. Unlike nitrogen, phosphorus has no comparably large atmospheric gas reservoir supporting rapid exchange.

Phosphate can bind to iron and aluminium compounds in acidic soils, or form calcium-associated compounds under other conditions. Old, highly weathered landscapes can therefore be phosphorus-limited despite containing phosphorus in total.

Roots and microbes release enzymes and compounds that mobilise some of these pools. The cost of obtaining nutrients influences plant strategies: [banksias and other woodland plants](forests/woodland-and-rainforest-plants.md) provide visible examples.

## 🍄 Fungal partnerships: substantial, but specific

In **arbuscular mycorrhizas**, fungi form branched exchange structures inside root cells while remaining separated from the plant cytoplasm by a specialised plant membrane. In **ectomycorrhizas**, fungi typically form a sheath around roots and a network between root cells.

These associations can improve nutrient access while receiving carbon from the plant. Their effects depend on the partners and conditions; a partnership can be more beneficial in one environment than another.

A fungus can associate with more than one plant, and transfers through shared networks are possible. That does not establish that forests routinely redistribute resources according to kinship or collective need. [Karst, Jones and Hoeksema's review](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-023-01986-1) distinguishes evidence for common mycorrhizal networks from stronger popular claims about their prevalence and benefits.

The cabinet's [fungal-network article](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-fungal-network-beneath-our-feet.md) explores the attraction of that idea; the evidence needs the qualifications here.

## 🍂 Carbon storage is a process

Organic matter includes fresh residues, living organisms and material transformed through decomposition. Persistence depends on climate, accessibility, aggregation and association with minerals, as well as molecular chemistry.

Draining peat can expose previously waterlogged organic matter to oxygen and accelerate decomposition. Disturbing mineral soil can alter aggregation and exposure, although outcomes depend on conditions. There is no single universal lifetime for “soil carbon”.

Many soil organisms remain poorly characterised. Detecting a gene suggests a capability; it does not show how fast the process is running in the field. That gap between inventory and activity is one reason soils remain a major research frontier.
