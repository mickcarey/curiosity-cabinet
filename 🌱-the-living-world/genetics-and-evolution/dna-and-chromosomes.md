# DNA and Chromosomes 🧬

How does a tiny seed grow into a sunflower rather than a frog? Part of the answer is that living things inherit chemical information from their parents. **DNA** is the material that carries that information.

DNA stands for **deoxyribonucleic acid**. It is a **molecule**: atoms joined together by chemical bonds. Atoms are the tiny building blocks of ordinary matter. A DNA molecule can be enormously long by molecular standards, containing millions of repeating units. Its full name describes its chemistry; you do not need to memorise it to understand what DNA does.

Sunflowers, frogs and humans are made of **cells**, the small living units that build and maintain their bodies. DNA inside those cells helps them make the molecules they need, including many of the tools used to grow, repair damage and reproduce.

## 📍 Where is DNA, and what is a chromosome?

Most human DNA is inside the **nucleus**, a compartment within a cell. It is divided into long molecules packaged with proteins. Each package is a **chromosome**. A chromosome is not a different substance from DNA: it is a way of organising it.

A **gene** is a region of DNA whose information a cell can use to make a working molecule. Many genes help specify proteins, the molecules that build structures and perform much of a cell's chemical work. Others produce working RNA molecules instead; we will meet RNA below.

A **genome** is a complete set of genetic material. These names can sound like four different mysterious substances, but they describe different things: DNA is the material, genes are useful regions within it, chromosomes organise it, and a genome is the whole set.

Genes do not operate in isolation. Cells also need water, energy, other molecules and suitable surroundings. DNA helps specify how a sunflower develops; it cannot grow a sunflower in an empty cupboard.

## 🔤 How can a molecule contain information?

DNA is built from repeating units called **nucleotides**. Each has a sugar, a phosphate group and one of four chemical parts called **bases**. The bases are adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine: **A, T, G and C**.

The order of these bases is the **sequence**. Just as changing the order of letters changes a word, changing a DNA sequence can change the information a cell reads. The letters are our shorthand for actual chemicals; there is no microscopic alphabet printed inside you.

Two DNA strands usually wind around each other to form a **double helix**, rather like a twisted ladder. Sugar and phosphate form its sides; paired bases form its rungs. A pairs with T, and G pairs with C.

Those pairing rules make copying possible. If a stretch of one strand reads A–C–G–T, the matching stretch reads T–G–C–A. Each strand supplies the information needed to build its partner.

## 📖 How does a cell use that information?

A cell makes an RNA copy of a selected DNA region. **RNA**, short for **ribonucleic acid**, is another chain molecule. It uses a slightly different sugar and usually has the base uracil, U, where DNA has T.

For a protein-making gene, the RNA copy carries information to a **ribosome**, a small molecular assembly that joins amino acids into a protein. Amino acids are the building units of proteins. Other RNAs do jobs directly, including helping the ribosome work.

The cell does not use every gene at once. A muscle cell and a nerve cell can contain largely the same DNA while making different combinations of proteins. That selective use is [gene expression and regulation](gene-expression-and-regulation.md).

This is why DNA is often compared with instructions, but is not a complete construction plan acting on its own. Existing cellular machinery reads it, and conditions inside and outside the cell influence the result.

## 🧵 Two metres of DNA. One microscopic space.

Stretch out the DNA from a typical human cell with two chromosome sets and it would total roughly two metres. The nucleus holding it is only a few millionths of a metre across. That is an outrageous packing problem. It gets harder: the cell must still find and use particular stretches without tangling the lot.

In animals, plants and other organisms with nuclei, DNA wraps around proteins called **histones**. A short length of DNA wrapped around a histone group forms a **nucleosome**. DNA and its associated proteins together make **chromatin**.

This packaging lets long molecules fit while allowing particular regions to be reached. Cells alter the packaging as they copy or use DNA. Tighter packing often reduces access, although access also depends on other controls.

The familiar X-shaped chromosome is a special appearance seen after copying and strong condensation before cell division. Most of the time, chromosomes are less compact. The textbook photograph catches them dressed for a particular occasion.

## 👯 Why do humans have pairs?

Most human body cells have **23 pairs of nuclear chromosomes**: one set originally supplied by the egg and one by the sperm. Having two sets is called **diploid**. Eggs and sperm usually contain one set, called **haploid**.

Twenty-two pairs are **autosomes**, the chromosomes other than sex chromosomes. The remaining pair is commonly XX or XY, although other patterns occur. Chromosomes contribute to sexual development alongside the genes they carry and the way those genes function.

The corresponding chromosomes inherited from the two parents are **homologous chromosomes**. They generally carry the same genes in corresponding places, but their versions of those genes can differ. The X and Y chromosomes share only limited corresponding regions.

Now distinguish a different kind of pair. Before division, a cell copies each chromosome. The two attached copies are **sister chromatids**. A pair inherited from two parents is not the same thing as two copies made inside one cell.

Not every human cell fits the usual count: mature red blood cells have no nucleus, for example. Other species also have different chromosome numbers. More chromosomes do not automatically mean a more complex organism.

## 🔄 What happens when DNA is copied?

Before a cell divides, it usually needs to copy its DNA so that both resulting cells receive genetic information. This copying is **replication**.

Proteins called **helicases** separate the two strands. **DNA polymerases** build new strands by adding matching nucleotides. Each completed double helix contains one original strand and one new strand. This is called **semiconservative replication**: each copy preserves half of the original pair.

There is a chemical complication. DNA strands have direction, and the two strands point opposite ways. Their ends are called **5-prime** and **3-prime**, written 5′ and 3′, after numbered positions in the sugar. Polymerases can add new units only at the 3′ end.

As the strands separate, one new strand can be extended relatively continuously. The other must be built in short sections, called **Okazaki fragments**, which are joined afterwards. Short starting pieces called **primers** let copying begin; other proteins manage twisting, check the new sequence and repair many errors.

An alteration in DNA sequence is a **mutation**. Some mutations change how cells work; others have little detectable effect. [Inheritance](inheritance.md) explains which changes can reach offspring, and [evolution](evolution.md) follows inherited changes through populations.

## 🧷 Chromosomes need attachment points and protected ends

During division, protein fibres move chromosomes into the resulting cells. They attach through a protein structure called the **kinetochore**, assembled at a chromosome region called the **centromere**.

**Telomeres** are specialised DNA-and-protein structures at the ends of linear chromosomes. They help stop a normal chromosome end from being mistaken for broken DNA. Human telomeres contain many repetitions of the sequence TTAGGG.

Telomeres often shorten through repeated divisions. An **enzyme**, a molecule that speeds up a reaction, called **telomerase** can extend them. This affects cell behaviour, ageing and cancer, but telomeres are not a single countdown timer telling an entire person how long to live.

## 🔋 Some DNA lives outside the nucleus

**Mitochondria** are cell compartments that help convert energy from food into forms cells can use. They retain their own small DNA genome, reflecting their origin from bacteria that became permanent partners inside ancestral cells.

Human mitochondrial DNA contains 37 genes. Most proteins needed by mitochondria are nevertheless specified by nuclear DNA. The two systems depend on one another.

Mitochondrial DNA is normally inherited through the egg in humans. Cells can contain many copies, sometimes with differences between them. [Cells and their compartments](../cells-and-chemistry/cells-and-organelles.md) explains how these structures work together.

## 🧩 Is all DNA a set of protein instructions?

No. Only a small fraction of human DNA directly specifies protein sequences. Other regions help control gene use, produce working RNAs, support chromosome organisation or contain repeated and mobile sequences. Some DNA has no demonstrated useful role for the organism.

“Noncoding” means that a sequence does not encode protein; it does not mean useless. Equally, finding that something happens at a DNA sequence does not automatically show that the activity benefits the organism.

The fascinating part is how chemical order becomes living activity: a sequence influences a molecule, that molecule changes what a cell can do, and cells together build a body. None of those steps is magic, but each deserves an explanation.
