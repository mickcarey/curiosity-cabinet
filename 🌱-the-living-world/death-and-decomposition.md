# Death and Decomposition: Life After the Organism 🍂

A fallen tree has stopped living as a tree. As a **habitat**, a place for other organisms to live, it may be entering a particularly busy phase. The tree has retired; the tenants have plans.

Fungi spread through its wood, beetle larvae feed beneath its bark, and the decaying trunk holds water and shelters other organisms. Its carbon and nutrients move into new bodies, soil and the atmosphere. **Decomposition** is the breakdown of dead material into simpler substances. It feeds an **ecosystem**, a community of living things and the physical surroundings they interact with.

## 🧬 Cell death and organismal death are different

**Cells**, the tiny living units making up an organism, die throughout the life of a many-celled body. Controlled cell death helps shape developing tissues, remove damaged cells and regulate populations of immune cells.

In **apoptosis**, a cell activates mechanisms that dismantle it in a relatively contained way, allowing its remains to be cleared. Other forms of cell death can rupture membranes and provoke **inflammation**, a response to damage that brings immune cells and fluid into the area. The details matter to [development](bodies-and-energy/embryonic-development.md) and [immunity](bodies-and-energy/immunity.md).

Organismal death concerns the loss of the coordinated processes that sustain the organism. It does not require every cell or associated microbe to cease activity simultaneously. Tissues differ in their dependence on oxygen, nutrients and ongoing signals.

This biological distinction is useful without turning the note into a clinical definition of death. A tree, a bacterium and a mammal organise their living parts differently; the death of the whole cannot always be described by watching one component.

## ⏳ Why maintenance eventually fails

Organisms continuously repair damage, replace components and manage errors. Ageing involves changes across many systems, including repair of **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid, the molecule carrying inherited information), maintenance of proteins that perform cellular work, energy use, immune defences and communication between cells.

Evolutionary explanations ask why **natural selection**, in which inherited differences affect reproductive success over generations, has not produced unlimited maintenance. Selection against a harmful effect often weakens when that effect occurs late in life. Some inherited features that are beneficial earlier can impose costs later. **Disposable soma theory**—“soma” means the body apart from its reproductive cells—emphasises trade-offs between investment in bodily maintenance and other demands, including reproduction.

These are related explanations, not evidence that bodies contain a universal scheduled-death switch. Ageing patterns differ greatly among species. [Open questions in biology](open-questions-in-biology.md) considers the mechanisms and remaining uncertainty; the [immortal jellyfish](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-immortal-jellyfish.md) is a particularly unusual example of life-cycle reversal, rather than invulnerability.

## 🪱 Decomposition is a food web

Dead organic matter is **detritus**. It includes leaves, wood, bodies, shed tissue and faecal material. The organisms eating it, and those eating them, form a **food web**: the connected paths by which food passes between living things.

Scavengers remove pieces. Animals without backbones, such as worms and insects, break material into smaller pieces and mix it with soil. Fungi and bacteria release **enzymes**, molecules that speed up reactions, to break large molecules into smaller ones they can absorb. Predators and grazers then consume the organisms doing that work.

Different resources favour different communities. A carcass provides concentrated protein and other readily available compounds. Wood contains **cellulose**, strong bundles of sugar chains, embedded in other material including the tough substance **lignin**. Its decomposition often requires specialised chemistry.

White-rot fungi can substantially degrade lignin as well as other wood components. Brown-rot fungi preferentially remove the sugar chains providing much of the wood's structure while leaving chemically modified lignin-rich material. These processes produce different textures and residues. A crumbling log is a record of what its occupants have been able to digest.

## 🌡️ Why some things rot slowly

Decomposition depends on the material, the organisms present and the physical environment.

Water is essential for microbial activity, but saturation can restrict oxygen supply. Warm conditions often accelerate biological reactions within suitable ranges, while drought, cold or extreme heat can limit activity. The chemical composition and physical accessibility of the material matter too.

A standing dead trunk may remain relatively dry; a fallen trunk in contact with moist soil experiences different conditions. Bark, diameter, tree species and fungal colonisation alter the rate further. There is no universal expiry date for a log.

Fire is another route by which organic matter is transformed. It can rapidly release carbon, redistribute nutrients and leave charcoal and ash. It does not reproduce the same ecological effects as gradual decay. [Fire, water and succession](forests/fire-water-and-succession.md) looks at that difference in Australian landscapes.

## 🔁 Nutrients cycle; energy flows

As decomposers break down fuel to release energy—a process called **respiration**—some carbon in dead material returns to the environment as carbon dioxide. Some becomes the bodies of microscopic organisms or remains in partially processed material. Under oxygen-free conditions, further microbial transformations can also produce methane.

Organic nitrogen can be released as **ammonium**, a nitrogen-containing compound plants and microbes can take up, a process called **mineralisation**. But decomposers also need nitrogen for their own growth. When feeding on carbon-rich, nitrogen-poor material, they may take up available nitrogen and temporarily reduce what nearby plants can access. That is **immobilisation**.

Phosphorus is released from organic compounds through enzyme-driven reactions and may be taken up, retained on mineral surfaces or lost from the site.

The atoms are reused. The energy is not recycled in the same way: as organisms use it, usable energy is progressively dissipated as heat. Ecosystems require continuing energy inputs, mostly sunlight. Decomposition is recycling with a power bill.

## 🪵 Dead wood is also structure

A log holds moisture, creates shaded spaces and changes how water and sediment move. Standing dead trees can provide cavities and perches; fallen wood supports organisms specialised for particular decay stages.

A gap in the overhead layer of leaves, the **canopy**, also changes light and temperature. Seedlings may establish or accelerate growth while the fallen trunk decomposes nearby. The vegetation response and the decay process have different timescales.

This is why removing every fallen branch does more than change appearances. It removes habitat and alters the movement of carbon and nutrients. The effect depends on context, but a visually tidy woodland is not automatically an ecologically complete one.

[Soil ecology](soil-ecology.md) explains what happens to some of the resulting organic matter. Persistence often depends on association with minerals and protection inside soil structures, not simply on material being intrinsically indigestible.

## 🌊 The same processes feed the deep sea

**Marine snow** consists of sinking particles such as dead **plankton** (organisms carried by currents), animal droppings and clumps of other biological material. Microbes and animals consume much of it before it reaches the seabed. The fraction that sinks deeper transfers carbon and energy away from surface waters. See [NOAA's explanation](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/marinesnow.html) and the cabinet's [marine snow note](../🌙-bedtime-reading/marine-snow-the-oceans-slow-blizzard.md).

A whale fall is a much larger, concentrated resource. Mobile scavengers consume soft tissue. Other animals exploit enriched sediments and remaining material. Microbes breaking down fats in bones can help generate **sulfide**, a sulfur-containing chemical. Other microbes obtain energy by reacting sulfide with substances such as oxygen, supporting animals that feed on or house them. Even the bones can keep dinner going.

These stages overlap and vary with carcass size, depth and local conditions. They illustrate **succession**, a change in the community over time, driven here by a changing resource: what can live there changes as the material is consumed.

A dead organism therefore has several ecological futures at once: food, physical shelter, a nutrient source and a changing patch of habitat. Watching a log decay is watching those futures unfold slowly enough to notice.
