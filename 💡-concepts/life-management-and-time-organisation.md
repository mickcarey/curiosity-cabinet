# Life Management And Time Organisation 🗓️🧺

## The External Brain Problem

Life management is not really about becoming a perfectly disciplined productivity statue. Thank goodness, because that sounds unbearable.

It is about building an **external brain** good enough that your actual brain can do human things:

- think,
- play,
- notice,
- rest,
- learn,
- have ideas in the shower,
- occasionally remember that vegetables exist.

The point of organisation is not to worship the calendar. The point is to reduce the amount of life you are carrying in anxious fog.

## Why "Just Remember It" Fails 🧠

Working memory is small, fragile, and easily mugged by notifications. If your system is "keep everything in my head," then your head becomes:

- calendar,
- inbox,
- warehouse,
- alarm clock,
- guilt aquarium,
- emotional junk drawer.

No wonder it gets loud in there.

A better system captures commitments outside your skull, then gives you regular moments to review and choose.

## The Four Buckets 🪣

A simple life-management system needs four places:

- **Calendar:** things that happen at a specific time.
- **Task list:** actions you can actually do.
- **Notes/reference:** information you may need later.
- **Someday list:** good ideas that are not promises yet.

Most chaos comes from mixing these. A dentist appointment in a notes app is a trap. A vague dream on today's task list is a guilt seed. A task called "sort life out" is not a task; it is a fog machine.

## Turn Fog Into Actions ✂️

Bad task:

> Get better at piano.

Better task:

> Practice left-hand bars 9-12 for 15 minutes at 60 bpm.

Bad task:

> Be more organized.

Better task:

> Spend 10 minutes clearing inbox and choosing tomorrow's top 3.

Bad task:

> Get fit.

Better task:

> Put football boots by the door and leave for training at 5:40.

The rule: if you cannot picture the physical next action, the task is still a cloud.

## Implementation Intentions: Tiny Plans With Teeth 🦷

An implementation intention is an if-then plan:

> If situation X happens, then I will do Y.

Examples:

- If I finish dinner, then I put tomorrow's practice book on the piano stand.
- If I open YouTube to "learn something," then I first write the question I am trying to answer.
- If it is Sunday at 4 p.m., then I review the week and choose three priorities.
- If I miss a workout, then I do the smallest version the next day instead of holding a guilt festival.

These work because they move decisions out of the heated moment. Future-you gets rails.

## Weekly Review: The Tiny Lighthouse 🔦

Once a week, ask:

- What is on my calendar?
- What promises did I make?
- What am I avoiding?
- What matters this week?
- What can be deleted, delayed, or made smaller?
- What would make next week less silly?

This is not corporate theatre. It is cleaning the windshield.

## The "Top Three" Trick

Each day, choose three meaningful targets:

- one must-do,
- one should-do,
- one would-feel-good-to-do.

That gives the day a spine without pretending you control every rib.

For example:

- Must: submit the form.
- Should: 25 minutes piano practice.
- Feel-good: tidy bedside table before sleep.

If everything is priority one, nothing is priority one. It is just a crowd shouting.

## Energy Is Part Of Time ⚡

A calendar that ignores energy is fiction.

Some work needs sharp attention:

- writing,
- learning,
- hard practice,
- planning,
- difficult conversations.

Some work fits lower energy:

- admin,
- tidying,
- errands,
- formatting notes,
- gentle review.

Do not schedule your hardest thinking where your brain usually becomes soup. Unless soup is the project, in which case proceed.

## A Bedtime Shutdown 🌙

Try a five-minute shutdown before sleep:

1. Capture loose tasks.
2. Check tomorrow's calendar.
3. Pick the first useful action.
4. Put one object in place to make it easier.
5. Stop negotiating with the day.

This lets sleep do sleep instead of running an unpaid night shift as project manager.

## Why It Matters

Good organisation is not about squeezing every minute. It is about making the important things easier to return to.

The system should feel like a set of handrails, not a cage.
