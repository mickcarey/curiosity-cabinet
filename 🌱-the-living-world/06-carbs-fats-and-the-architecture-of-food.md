# Carbs, Fats, And The Architecture Of Food 🧱

People talk about food as if it were a moral ranking of fuels. Carbs are a vibe. Fat is a villain or a religion. Protein, from [the last chapter](05-proteins-the-machines-that-fold.md), at least gets to be a gym. This is a category error wearing a supermarket label.

Sugars and fats are energy, yes. They are also **scaffolding, waterproofing, membranes, hormones, and name-tags**. Treating them as calories with a personality is like judging a piano by how well it burns. You can get heat out of a Steinway. That is not what it is for.

I am the AI narrator. Mick is the human who asked for a deep dive and is not, here, being handed a diet. This chapter is not medical advice and not a meal plan. Macros are architecture. If someone on the internet is shouting a ratio at you, they are in a different building.

A calorie, properly, waits for the next arc. Here the question is cruder and more interesting: what *are* these molecules doing in a body that is trying to stay a body?

## Four Jobs, One Word: Carbohydrate 🍬

Carbohydrate is a terrible umbrella. It covers glucose in your blood, glycogen in your liver, the cellulose in a tree, the chitin in a beetle, and the sugar trees stuck to the outside of a cell so the immune system can read a nametag. Calling all of that "carbs" is like calling a guitar, a stadium, and a setlist "wood."

Chemically, the simple ones are hydrates of carbon in the old formula sense: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, rings and chains that water understands. **Monosaccharides** (glucose, fructose, galactose) are letters. **Disaccharides** (sucrose, lactose, maltose) are two-letter words — sucrose is how a plant ships sugar through phloem; lactose is how a mammal ships sugar through milk; maltose is a starch fragment mid-demolition. **Polysaccharides** are novels, or brick walls, depending on the linkage.

Four jobs, stacked:

1. **Fuel.** Glucose is the cell's most boringly reliable substrate. Glycolysis, later, will take it to pieces. Brain and red cells are famously greedy for it, which is a physiology fact, not a command to live on lollies.
2. **Store.** When you have more glucose than you need right now, you do not pile it as syrup. You polymerise it. Plants make **starch**. Animals make **glycogen**. Same monomer, different filing system.
3. **Scaffolding.** **Cellulose** and **chitin** are structural carbohydrates. They are what a forest and a crab are doing with sugar when they are not thinking about snacks.
4. **Name-tags.** Short, branched sugar trees — **glycans** — stuck onto proteins and lipids. Information, not calories. The genome does not write them the way it writes a protein sequence, which is why this job is still a frontier.

The linkage is the whole plot. Glucose connected α-1,4 is food. Glucose connected β-1,4 is wood. Same brick. Different mortar. Enzymes are snobs about this. Yours include amylase and a suite of glycogen tools. They do not include a cellulase worth the name. A cow outsources that job to microbes. You outsource it to "fibre," which is a polite word for carbohydrates that leave the party still dressed.

Glucose itself is already a small origami problem. In water it spends most of its time as a ring, not the open chain the textbooks draw first, and the ring has two faces (α and β) depending on how the first carbon's hydroxyl sits. That anomeric carbon is the hitching post. Life's entire carbohydrate architecture is "which carbon, which face, which neighbour." Miss that and starch and cellulose look like the same molecule, which they almost are, which is the joke.

## Starch Versus Glycogen: Warehouse And Pit Crew 🏁

Plants and animals both needed a way to put glucose in a drawer without turning the cytoplasm into jam. They picked related polymers and then specialised like two codes of football.

**Starch** is the plant store, packed into granules in amyloplasts. It is a mixture:

- **Amylose**, mostly a linear chain of glucose with α-1,4 links, coiled into a helix. Roughly a fifth to a third of typical starch.
- **Amylopectin**, branched: α-1,4 chains with α-1,6 branch points about every **24–30** glucose units. The rest of the granule, and the reason starch can be semi-crystalline.

The branching is sparse enough that amylopectin packs, partly crystallises, and sits quietly in a seed or a tuber through winter. Slow-release by design. A warehouse, not a pit stop. When you eat potato, you are raiding a plant's long-term storage, then using **your** enzymes to turn it back into glucose. Cooking helps, because a granule that has gelatinised is easier to rob. That fact will matter when calories get honest. Here it is just architecture meeting kitchen.

**Glycogen** is the animal (and fungal, and many bacterial) version. Same α-1,4 chains, same α-1,6 branches, but the branches come every **8–12** residues, and the whole thing is built as a sphere around a protein core (glycogenin), often in tiers.

Liver packs glycogen as a buffer for *the whole animal's* blood glucose. Muscle packs it as a local tank for the muscle that earned it, and muscle is selfish: it keeps that glucose for contraction, which is why a long run and a thinking brain are not drawing on the same jar. More ends means more places for glycogen phosphorylase to nibble at once. Rapid mobilisation. A pit crew.

Why polymerise at all? Free glucose is osmotically loud. A cell stuffed with millimolar sugar would suck water like a badly behaved sponge. A glycogen granule is thousands of glucose units behaving as a few particles. Dense, quiet, legal. Starch granules in a seed are the same trick on a plant's schedule. The architecture is a way to store carbon without drowning in it.

If starch is a warehouse with a few loading docks, glycogen is a Formula One stop with a hose on every side of the car. Plants can afford to wait for spring. A startled mammal cannot. The chemistry is almost the same. The branching frequency is the policy.

Humans also have a little glycogen in other tissues, and some mutant plants make a glycogen-like **phytoglycogen** when starch-branching goes sideways. Nature keeps remixing the same album. The hit single is always: polymerise glucose so it is dense, osmotically polite, and available at the speed your lifestyle requires.

## Cellulose And Chitin: Same Bricks, Different Glue 🪵

**Cellulose** is the most abundant organic polymer on the surface of the Earth, which is a statistic that should make every "carbs are just fuel" sermon sit down. It is glucose in **β-1,4** links: each residue flipped 180 degrees relative to the last, so the chain is a flat ribbon, not a digestible helix. Ribbons hydrogen-bond into microfibrils. Microfibrils are wood, cotton, paper, the cell wall that lets a tree be a tree and not a puddle of photosynthesis.

Plants are not furniture, as a later chapter will insist. Cellulose is why they can *afford* to stand still.

You cannot break those β-links with the enzymes that handle starch. Termites, cows, and fungi that rot logs either make cellulases or keep microbes that do. When fibre shows up in a human diet discussion, this is the molecule underneath: carbohydrate that was never trying to be your fuel. It still does work in a gut — bulk, water, microbial food — which is ecology, not a smoothie slogan.

**Chitin** is cellulose's cousin with a costume change. Same β-1,4 backbone, but each sugar is **N-acetylglucosamine**: glucose wearing an acetylated nitrogen at carbon 2. The extra hydrogen-bonding makes it tougher. Arthropod exoskeletons. Fungal cell walls, alongside glucans. Crab shell, beetle wing, mushroom, the reason a lobster is a tank. Chitin is often called the second most abundant structural polysaccharide. Whether that ranking is exactly right depends on how you count the ocean. It is abundant enough that the biosphere is, to a first approximation, cellulose and chitin wearing other molecules as trim.

Bacteria, never content to be left out, build **peptidoglycan**: sugars cross-linked with peptides, a bag that holds a cell against its own osmotic swagger. Penicillin is a weapon against that bag. The point, for this chapter, is not microbiology class. It is that structural carbohydrate keeps turning up wherever life needs a wall that water cannot just shrug off.

One carbon-carbon geometry. Two kingdoms of architecture. A third, glycogen/starch, for the pantry. A fourth, in bacteria, that antibiotics noticed. Carbohydrate is not a food group. It is a construction company that also runs a petrol station.

## Glycans: Name-Tags The Genome Does Not Write 🏷️

Then there is the job that still does not fit on a cereal box.

A **glycan** is a sugar chain, often branched, stuck to a protein or a lipid. **N-linked** glycans attach to the nitrogen of asparagine, usually at an Asn-X-Ser/Thr motif. **O-linked** glycans attach to the oxygen of serine or threonine. The attachment is a post-translational modification, which is a bureaucratic way of saying: the DNA wrote the protein, and then the Golgi did jazz on top.

This is not decoration. Glycans are how cells display identity.

The celebrity example is **ABO blood groups**. They are not proteins with opinions. They are glycan endings on red-cell surfaces (and other tissues, and secretions, depending on your FUT2 status). The ABO gene encodes a glycosyltransferase. Type A adds N-acetylgalactosamine. Type B adds galactose. Type O's enzyme is a dud, so you keep the H antigen and call it a blood type. Transfusion is, among other things, a lesson in not mixing nametags. The immune system reads sugars. It has been reading them longer than it has been reading our textbooks.

Elsewhere:

- **Selectins** grab specific glycans to park white cells on vessel walls. Inflammation is a carbohydrate conversation.
- Influenza haemagglutinin binds sialic acids on your airway cells. A virus is a lock-picker of glycan doors.
- Hormones and antibodies are often glycoproteins; the sugar trees affect folding, half-life, and whether something looks "self."
- Sperm and egg, host and microbe, tumour and the immune cells trying to notice it: glycans at the border.

Ajit Varki and the *Essentials of Glycobiology* crowd have a statistic that should be illegal in a first-year lecture: three amino acids make six tripeptides; three hexoses, once you allow branching, anomers, and linkage positions, can make **thousands** of trisaccharides. A hexasaccharide's theoretical combinatorial space is cartoonishly large.

Biology does not use all of it. It uses enough that we still cannot routinely sequence a glycome the way we sequence a genome.

The genome is a linear code. Glycans are a branching code written by a committee of enzymes whose activity depends on cell type, nutrient status, and mood. That is why two copies of "the same" protein can wear different sugar trees. Microheterogeneity is not sloppiness. It is the point of a nametag you can update without rewriting the passport.

Two more pieces of kit, because nametags need readers and slime needs a recipe:

- **Lectins** are proteins that bind specific glycans. They are the eyes. A selectin is a lectin with a day job in blood vessels. Plant lectins, bacterial adhesins, your own immune lectins: same idea, different loyalties.
- **Mucins** are proteins so heavily O-glycosylated they are basically sugar with a protein afterthought. Mucus is a glycan material. A gut lining, an airway, a slug: hydrated nametags at industrial scale. If you have ever wondered why "mucus" and "information" feel like they should not share a chapter, this is the sentence that marries them.

Glycolipids do the same nametag work on a fat rather than a protein: a sugar tree on a ceramide, sitting in the outer leaflet of the membrane, waving at the world. Blood-group antigens live on both proteins and lipids. The cell does not care which flagpole you use. It cares that the flag is readable.

## Fats Are Not Just Fuel 🛢️

Lipids are worse than carbohydrates at being one word. The category is "doesn't like water," which is a personality, not a structure.

A few families that actually matter here:

- **Fatty acids**: hydrocarbon tails, usually even-numbered, with or without double bonds. Palmitate, oleate, and friends. Saturated tails are straight and pack. *Cis* double bonds kink. Kinks are fluidity.
- **Triglycerides**: three fatty acids on a glycerol. Adipose tissue. Insulation, padding, and the densest common fuel store. This is the "fat" on a nutrition label.
- **Phospholipids**: two tails, a phosphate head, often with choline or another personality stuck on. **Amphipathic**: wet head, greasy tails. The molecule that makes membranes possible.
- **Waxes**: long esters. Cuticle on a leaf. Waterproofing on feathers and fur. Sebum. The reason a duck is not a sponge.
- **Sphingolipids**: a different backbone (sphingosine, not glycerol), including sphingomyelin and the glycolipids just mentioned. Myelin loves these. So do lipid rafts, if that phrase still means anything by the time you finish the paper you opened.
- **Sterols**: four fused rings. **Cholesterol** in animals. Related sterols in plants and fungi. Not a fatty acid. Still a lipid. Still architecture.

Triglycerides are the calorie people mean. Phospholipids and sterols are the city people live in. Confusing them is how a nutrition slogan starts giving urban-planning advice.

Fuel is a real job. Fat stores more energy per gram than carbohydrate or protein, which is why migration, hibernation, and human adipose tissue exist. Waterproofing is a real job. The job that diet culture keeps forgetting is the membrane.

## Membranes: The Wall That Assembles Itself 🧱

[Chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) already spoiled this. Water hydrogen-bonds with itself and will not babysit grease. Put enough amphiphiles in that argument and they form bilayers: heads out, tails in, a two-molecule-thick truce. Membranes are not a miracle invention. They are what phospholipids **do** in water, the way a crowd forms a terrace.

A cell without a membrane is a rumour. Every organelle's district line, every mitochondrion's inner folding, every synapse's gap, is a lipid bilayer with proteins floating in it like boats in a harbour. The **fluid mosaic** is the textbook name. Fluid because the tails are not a crystal. Mosaic because proteins and glycolipids interrupt the sea.

The recipe is not one phospholipid. Tail length, saturation, and head-group mix set the texture:

- More **unsaturated** tails, more kinks, more fluid. A cold-water fish is a liquidity strategy.
- More **saturated** tails, tighter packing, more rigid.
- **Cholesterol**, in animal plasma membranes, inserts among the tails, hydroxyl toward the heads, rigid rings damping motion near the surface. It is a fluidity buffer: not too melt-y, not too brittle. Eukaryotic plasma membranes can run **20–50%** sterol by the lipid count. That is not a rounding error. That is a structural beam.

**Myelin**, the sheath that lets axons send fast mail, is a membrane showing off. Dry myelin is something like **70–85% lipid**, versus a more typical membrane's roughly even protein/lipid split. Cholesterol, galactolipids, plasmalogens: insulation as a fat story. A brain is, among other things, an organised lipid machine. "Fat is optional" is an interesting claim to make at an organ that is so rude about being made of it.

Membranes are also patchy. Lipids do not mix like paint. Cholesterol and sphingolipids can cluster into more ordered patches — **rafts**, in the literature — that corral some proteins and ignore others. How literal a "raft" is, in a living cell rather than a model bilayer, is still a polite fight. The useful takeaway does not depend on winning it: composition is local. A membrane is not one liquid. It is a neighbourhood with postcodes.

Later, when we get to the cell as a city, this bilayer is border control. Here, just hold the load-bearing fact: **if you refused to build with lipids because lipids contain energy, you would have no rooms.**

## Steroids, Hormones, And The Cholesterol Slander 💍

Cholesterol's public relations problem is so successful that people forget it is a precursor, a membrane component, and not a toxin that snuck into the pantry.

From cholesterol, cells make:

- **Steroid hormones.** Cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, oestrogens, progesterone. Four rings and a few decorations, and suddenly you have puberty, stress, salt balance, pregnancy. These are lipids as **letters**, not as lard. They slip through membranes because they are greasy, then bind receptors and rewrite transcription. A hormone that is a protein has to knock. A steroid can wander in.
- **Bile acids.** Liver chemistry that turns cholesterol into soap, so dietary fat can be emulsified and absorbed. You cannot run a fat-absorption system on a dare. You need detergent. Biology makes it from the same ring system.
- **Vitamin D.** Sunlight photochemistry on a cholesterol derivative in skin, then a couple of activations. Call it a vitamin or a hormone; it is a sterol either way.

Fat-soluble vitamins **A, D, E, and K** all need lipid neighbourhoods to move and to be absorbed. That is not a wellness blog. That is solubility. Drop dietary fat to nothing and you do not just lose calories. You lose a transport system.

Humans can make many fatty acids. We cannot make **linoleic acid** (an omega-6) or **α-linolenic acid** (an omega-3), because we lack the desaturases that put double bonds that far along the chain. Those two are **essential**: they have to come in through the mouth, after which we can elongate and desaturate toward longer cousins such as arachidonic acid and, less efficiently, DHA. Essential, here, means "the architecture requires a part we cannot fabricate," not "buy this brand." Neuronal membranes are greedy for polyunsaturated tails. DHA in particular turns up in grey-matter phospholipids like a specialist timber: kinky, fluid, not the main beam of myelin, important in the membranes that actually do the thinking. The exact supplement argument can wait in a bin labelled *not this chapter*. The architectural claim cannot: some lipids are structural components you have to import, the way a builder imports glass.

Waterproofing deserves one more sentence because it is the unglamorous twin of membranes. A leaf cuticle is wax. Seabird feathers are waxed. Your skin is a lipid barrier trying its best. Lose that architecture and you do not gain a cleaner fuel policy. You gain evaporation, infection, and a duck that sinks.

## Why "Just Don't Eat Fat" Wrecks The Building 🚫

The slogan is simple, which is how slogans get into trouble.

Fat contains a lot of energy, so cutting it looks like a shortcut. It is the same logic as refusing bricks because bricks could, in a pinch, be thrown on a fire. Yes. And then you have no wall.

A body that is "just not eating fat" still has to:

- Maintain **bilayers** in every cell, with a mix of saturated, unsaturated, and (in animals) sterol
- Wrap axons in **myelin**
- Make **steroid hormones**, **bile**, and **vitamin D**
- Absorb **A, D, E, K**
- Supply **essential fatty acids** it cannot desaturate into existence
- Store energy for the gaps between meals, because glycogen is a short tank and a liver is not infinite

None of that is an instruction to eat a stick of butter and call it wisdom. Energy surplus, atherosclerosis, and the politics of LDL are real, and they live in a different conversation with a GP, who is not a chapter. The claim here is narrower: **lipids are load-bearing.** "Fat" on a packet is mostly triglyceride. "Fat" in a cell is a construction kit. Confusing the two is how a calorie slogan knocks a wall down and then acts surprised about the weather.

Carbohydrate slogans fail the same exam from the other side. You can live on weird macronutrient ratios; cultures have. You cannot live without the *jobs* — fuel buffering, glycan nametags, cellulose in the biosphere if not in your stomach. A ketogenic experiment, a high-carb tradition, a medical prescription: those are tactics. They do not repeal the architecture.

The next arc will talk about what a calorie actually measures, and why a bomb calorimeter is a sociopath. This chapter's job is to stop you walking into that argument thinking food is only a number. A number can be true in a flask and still miss the wall, the nametag, and the hormone. Architecture first. Accounting later.

## The Glycobiology Frontier 🔭

Proteins got Anfinsen, then AlphaFold, then a database of two hundred million statues. Glycans got a beige lanyard and a reputation for being too hard.

The word **glycobiology** was coined in the late 1980s, when carbohydrate chemistry finally collided with cell biology and stopped being the course people dropped. The lag was not laziness. Glycans are branched, not templated directly from a codon table, hard to sequence, and heterogeneous on purpose. DNA is a railway. A glycan is a shrubbery.

What is still early:

- **The glycome** of a cell is not one structure. It is a distribution that shifts with development, diet, inflammation, cancer. We do not have a glycan equivalent of a routine genome sequence.
- **Congenital disorders of glycosylation** showed that missing a single glycosyltransferase can wreck a life, which is the grim proof that the nametags were load-bearing all along. Most of the subtler, common-disease genetics of glycan genes is still being unpicked.
- **Host–microbe glycan warfare** is endless. Pathogens wear our sugars, eat our sugars, or hide behind sugars. Your blood type is a footnote in that war.
- **Therapeutic antibodies** succeed or fail in part on their Fc glycans. Biotech learned glycobiology because money did.

Carolyn Bertozzi's way in was to stop trying to sequence the shrubbery with 1950s tools and instead **tag it in living cells**. Bioorthogonal chemistry — click reactions that do not disturb the cell's own chemistry — let researchers put a handle on glycans as they were being made. She shared the 2022 Nobel with Morten Meldal and K. Barry Sharpless, who had made click chemistry a sport. The prize citation is chemistry. The motive, in her case, was the sugar coat we could not see.

If proteins were the machines that fold, glycans are the livery the machines wear to be recognised. We can now photograph a lot of the machines. We are still squinting at the livery. That is not a side quest. A cell that cannot be recognised is a cell that cannot join a tissue, cannot tell an immune system to leave it alone, and cannot stop a virus from using the doorbell.

Mystery, as promised: **glycobiology is a frontier wearing a beige lanyard.** The lanyard is a glycan. Of course it is.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Why these storage polymers, exactly?** Starch versus glycogen is a beautiful just-so story about branching and lifestyle. How much of that is constraint, and how much is one evolutionary roll that stuck, is not fully closed.
- **How much of a glycan's function is the exact tree versus "there is a tree here"?** Some nametags are precise. Some are bulk and charge. We are better at the first in textbooks than in living tissue.
- **The full human glycome**, even of one cell type, in one state. Not done. Not close to the way proteomics is close.
- **How diet, microbiome, and glycosylation talk to each other**, beyond the obvious "fibre feeds bugs." Real, messy, easy to overclaim. Easy to underclaim too.
- **How to think about lipids without a morality play.** Membrane composition, essential fatty acids, and circulating lipoproteins are different questions that share a word. The word is the problem.
- **Whether "carbohydrate" and "lipid" remain useful categories** once you have seen cellulose, cholesterol, and a blood-group antigen in the same chapter. They remain useful the way "weather" is useful. You still need a forecast.
