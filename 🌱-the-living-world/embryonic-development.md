# How an Embryo Builds a Body 🥚

A human body develops through cell division, movement, signalling and changes in gene activity. No cell holds a miniature anatomical diagram. Cells respond to local conditions, and their responses change the conditions experienced by their neighbours.

The remarkable part is how reliably this produces organised tissues from one starting cell. The complications are equally informative: development involves timing, mechanical forces and inherited cellular material as well as DNA.

## 🧬 What egg and sperm contribute

Human eggs and sperm normally contain one set of 23 nuclear chromosomes. Their production involves **meiosis**, which reduces chromosome number and reshuffles inherited variants through recombination and chromosome assortment.

Fertilisation brings the two sets into one cell, the **zygote**. The egg also supplies nearly all the cytoplasm, organelles and stored molecules supporting early development. Nuclear DNA from two parents is therefore only part of the starting condition.

Sperm and egg production distribute resources differently. Sperm development produces small specialised cells; oocyte development preserves most cytoplasm in one large cell, with small polar bodies receiving other chromosome products. Human oocytes also undergo long periods of meiotic arrest.

Errors in chromosome separation can produce missing or additional chromosomes. Errors after fertilisation can produce **mosaicism**, where different cell lineages have different genetic compositions. These describe mechanisms, not a complete prediction of an individual's development.

For the chromosome machinery itself, see [DNA and chromosomes](dna-and-chromosomes.md) and [inheritance](inheritance.md).

## 🫧 From one cell to distinct cell populations

Early **cleavage divisions** partition the zygote into smaller cells without comparable growth in overall size. The cells compact into a **morula**, and then a fluid-filled cavity develops within a **blastocyst**.

The blastocyst contains an outer cell population contributing to extraembryonic structures, including the fetal component of the placenta, and an inner cell mass contributing to the embryo and additional supporting tissues. These cells have begun to differ in position, polarity and gene expression.

In typical human development, blastocyst formation occurs during the first week after fertilisation, followed by the beginning of implantation in the uterine lining. The stages are biological processes with variation, not appointments at which every embryo checks its watch.

Stored maternal molecules support the earliest stages, while expression from the embryo's own genome becomes increasingly important. Development depends on this transition as well as on cell number.

## 🧭 Gastrulation establishes the basic arrangement

During **gastrulation**, cells move and reorganise into three primary germ layers. In humans this begins around the third week after fertilisation.

| Germ layer | Examples of major derivatives |
| --- | --- |
| Ectoderm | Epidermis and nervous-system tissues |
| Mesoderm | Muscle, connective tissue, blood, heart and much of the urinary and reproductive systems |
| Endoderm | Epithelial lining of much of the digestive and respiratory systems, with associated organs |

These are starting lineages, not exclusive labels for entire organs. A lung includes tissues derived from more than one layer.

Body axes emerge through interacting signals and cell movements. **Morphogens** are signals whose concentration or exposure history can influence cell fate. Gene-regulatory networks interpret those signals in the context of what a cell has already experienced.

Mechanical effects matter too. Cells adhere, pull, fold and change shape. A sheet becoming a tube is simultaneously a genetic, biochemical and physical event.

## 🏗️ Building organs uses addition and subtraction

Organ formation involves local proliferation, migration, differentiation and programmed cell death. More cells alone would produce a larger mass, not a functional heart or a hand with separated digits.

Developmental signals are reused in different settings. Their effect depends on location, timing and the cells receiving them. This helps explain how a finite set of signalling pathways can contribute to many structures without each organ requiring completely unrelated molecular machinery.

The standard human **embryonic period** extends through the end of eight weeks after fertilisation. The **fetal period** follows, with substantial growth and continued differentiation. Clinical gestational age is usually counted from the last menstrual period, approximately two weeks earlier than fertilisation-based age. The two clocks should not be mixed.

## 🌿 The placenta is an active organ

The placenta supports gas exchange, nutrient transfer, waste removal and hormonal signalling. Maternal blood and fetal blood normally remain in separate circulatory compartments while substances cross the intervening tissues.

Oxygen crosses by diffusion; glucose and amino acids use transport mechanisms. Fetal haemoglobin has properties that favour oxygen uptake under fetal conditions. The umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood towards the fetus, while the arteries return blood towards the placenta. Vessel names describe direction relative to the heart, not oxygen content.

The immune relationship is also active. Local regulation permits contact between genetically different maternal and fetal tissues while maintaining defence. It is more accurate than saying pregnancy switches immunity off. The broader mechanisms of tolerance appear in [immunity](immunity.md).

## 👯 Twins and developmental flexibility

Dizygotic twins develop from two fertilised eggs. Monozygotic twins originate from one fertilised egg, but subsequent mutations, regulation and developmental conditions can produce differences between them.

Monozygotic twinning shows that early development has a capacity for reorganisation. The familiar relationship between developmental timing and shared membranes is a useful descriptive model, but it is not a complete explanation of the cellular causes of twinning.

More generally, researchers can explain many local developmental mechanisms without yet predicting an entire human body's formation from first principles. Work in other animals, human embryonic material and stem-cell models illuminates different parts of the process; a model reproducing one feature does not automatically reproduce the whole embryo.

[Gene regulation](gene-expression-and-regulation.md) explains how cell identities persist, and [animal body plans](animal-body-plans.md) shows how related developmental tools have produced very different anatomies.
