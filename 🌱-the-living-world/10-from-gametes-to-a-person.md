# From Gametes to a Person 🧬

Two libraries walk into a cell. Nine months later, someone wants a snack.

That is the whole plot, and it is also a lie of compression. Between the two haploid cells and a person there is a chromosome dance, a diplomatic organ that will be thrown away, three germ layers that become every tissue you have, and a stretch of development when a single cell has to invent a body plan without a foreman. School called this "the miracle of life" and then showed a diagram of a tadpole. The interesting part is the logistics.

This chapter is chromosomes in action, and what early life during pregnancy actually *is* as biology. It is not a pregnancy manual, not antenatal advice, not a counselling guide, and not a verdict on when a person starts. If a sentence here is used as medical advice, the sentence has been kidnapped. A GP, a midwife, an actual clinician: those are the humans to ask.

## A Hard Line, Before The Fun 🚫

Read this once, then again at the end.

This is a curiosity-cabinet note about **how a diploid body is assembled from two haploid gametes**. It describes typical mechanisms, and some of the ways those mechanisms fail, as biology. It does not tell anyone what to do with a pregnancy, a test result, a scan, a family history, or a feeling. Chromosomal differences are discussed as failed chromosome dances, not as destinies or value judgements. "High-stakes" means "a lot of construction is happening," not "panic." If you need care, get care from a person whose job is care.

Right. Chromosomes.

## Forty-Six, In Twenty-Three Pairs 📚

A typical human somatic cell — the kind [chapter 09](09-the-cell-as-a-city.md) treated as a city — carries **46 chromosomes**: **23 pairs**. One of each pair came from a sperm, one from an egg, which is the whole point of the next few headings.

Twenty-two of those pairs are **autosomes**, numbered 1 to 22, roughly largest to smallest (with a couple of historical numbering sins; chromosome 21 is actually smaller than 22, and nobody wanted to reprint the textbooks). The twenty-third pair are the **sex chromosomes**: two **X**s in a typical 46,XX karyotype, or one **X** and one **Y** in a typical 46,XY karyotype.

A **karyotype** is the family portrait: chromosomes stained, lined up, counted. It is written `46,XX` or `46,XY` when the count and the sex chromosomes look typical. We spent thirty years thinking the number was 48, until Tjio and Levan counted 46 in 1956. Even the most basic fact in this chapter is younger than rock and roll.

The **Y** is small. It carries **SRY** — sex-determining region Y — a gene that, around weeks seven to eight after fertilisation, can flip the bipotential gonad toward testes rather than ovaries. SRY is a switch, not a novel. Downstream genes do the actual building. An XY embryo without a working SRY typically follows the ovarian path; an XX embryo that has acquired a translocated SRY can follow the testicular one. Biology is a cascade, not a vote. Sex, in the chromosomal sense, is also not a complete account of bodies, which come in more arrangements than two cartoons. This chapter needs the switch because it is part of how a body is specified. It does not need a culture war.

The **X** is large and busy. Typical XX cells inactivate one X (a Barr body) so that X-linked gene dose is not doubled, but the "inactive" X is not fully silent. About a fifth of its genes still speak. XX and XY cells are not identical even after that trick. A later genetics chapter can go further. Here: you are not "the Y chromosome" or "two X's." You are 22 pairs of autosomes, a sex-chromosome pair, and a mitochondrial genome your mother almost certainly paid for.

Which is the next dance.

## Meiosis: Why You Are Not A Clone 🎲

Mitosis, from chapter 09, copies a city: 46 in, 46 out. **Meiosis** is how you make a gamete. One round of DNA replication, two rounds of division, and you leave with **23 chromosomes**, not 46. Haploid. A half-deck.

If meiosis were only "cut the number in half," you would still be a shuffled clone of one parent. It is not only that. It is a remix.

**Crossing over** happens in meiosis I, when homologous chromosomes pair — your maternal chromosome 8 lined up with your paternal chromosome 8 — and swap stretches. The paired structure is a bivalent, held by a synaptonemal complex and then by chiasmata, the visible scars of those swaps. Each chromosome you hand to a gamete is often a mosaic of grandparental pieces. You are not a photocopy of a parent. You are a playlist made from two playlists that were themselves remixes.

**Independent assortment** is the other shuffle. When the 23 pairs line up at metaphase I, each pair orients at random. Maternal 3 might go left while maternal 17 goes right. 2²³ possible combinations from that step alone, before you even count the crossovers, before you combine with another gamete. That is more than eight million ways to deal a haploid hand from one person, and it is why two siblings (who are not identical twins) can look like they came from neighbouring families.

Meiosis I separates **homologues**. Meiosis II separates **sister chromatids**, more like a haploid mitosis. Four products, in the textbook.

Reality, in mammals, is sex-asymmetric, because biology has a sense of humour and a volume problem.

## Two Haploid Specialists 🥚

**Sperm** are small, many, and in a hurry. In typical spermatogenesis, one spermatocyte does the full meiotic run and yields four haploid spermatids, which then grow a flagellum, dump most of their cytoplasm, pack the DNA almost like a crystal, and keep a helix of mitochondria in the midpiece as fuel for the swim. They are haploid delivery vehicles with a nucleus in the head and a deadline.

**Eggs** (oocytes) are large, few, and patient. The oocyte keeps almost all the cytoplasm. Meiosis here is lopsided on purpose: one big cell, and tiny **polar bodies** that get a discarded haploid nucleus and almost no groceries. The egg is not just DNA. It is a warehouse — proteins, RNAs, organelles, a universe of mitochondria — built during the mother's own fetal life and then held.

The holding is the part people skip. Oocytes enter meiosis in the fetal ovary and **arrest in prophase I**, sometimes for decades. At ovulation, one (usually) finishes meiosis I, extrudes the first polar body, and **arrests again at metaphase II**, waiting for a sperm. Fertilisation is the cue to finish: second polar body, haploid maternal genome ready to meet the paternal one. An egg is a cell that paused a chromosome dance twice and will not finish it without a partner. That is not romance. That is cell-cycle control with a very long intermission.

**Mitochondria** go with the warehouse. In humans, inheritance of mitochondrial DNA is **maternal**, almost entirely. The old cartoon said sperm mitochondria enter the egg and are then destroyed. A 2023 *Nature Genetics* paper found something ruder and cleaner: mature human sperm are essentially **devoid of intact mtDNA** already. During spermatogenesis, the packaging protein TFAM is rerouted to the sperm nucleus, and the mitochondrial genome is lost before the race starts. The midpiece still has mitochondria for the swim. They are, in the genomic sense, empty engines. The child runs on the egg's power plants.

Rare leaks get reported. "Almost entirely" is the honest adverb. The next chapter's nested-community point still stands: your mitochondria are a maternal lineage living in every cell, a separate clock, a separate mutational history.

## Fertilisation: 23 + 23 ✨

Typical meeting place: the **ampulla** of the uterine tube, not the uterus, which is a geographic fact that still surprises people who learned reproduction from cinema.

A sperm has to get through the **zona pellucida**, the glycoprotein coat around the egg, then fuse with the egg membrane. The egg, once one sperm is in, runs blocks to reduce the chance of a second (polyspermy), which would be three haploid genomes and a mess. The sperm nucleus decondenses into a pronucleus. The egg finishes meiosis II. Two haploid pronuclei, one cell, **46 chromosomes** again: a **zygote**. Diploid. Genetically unique, unless you are about to become a twin, which is a later heading.

The zygote is totipotent in the strict sense: it can make the embryo *and* the extra-embryonic tissues, the placenta included. That licence will be revoked, on a schedule, as cells choose sides.

No new person-shaped thing exists yet. A genome has been combined. A process has started. Hold the poetry. The construction site is about to get loud.

## Cleavage, Morula, Blastocyst, Door 🚪

**Cleavage** is mitosis without growth. The zygote divides, and divides, inside the zona, so the cells (**blastomeres**) get smaller. You are not making a bigger ball. You are making a ball with more rooms, same square footage. For the first few days this is running on maternal RNAs and proteins stockpiled in the egg. The zygotic genome wakes in humans around the four-to-eight-cell stage. The library starts writing its own memos.

By about day 3–4, ~16 cells, compacted: a **morula**, named for a mulberry by someone who was hungry. Cells that were loosely associated now stick, polarise, and pick a first identity.

By about day 5–6, a fluid cavity opens and you have a **blastocyst**:

- an outer **trophectoderm** (trophoblast), which will make the placenta and the chorion
- an **inner cell mass** (embryoblast), which will make the embryo proper, and also the amnion and some other extra-embryonic bits
- a cavity, the blastocoel

That is the first great fork: placenta-line versus body-line. Identical genomes, different jobs, already. The blastocyst **hatches** from the zona and, around day 6–7, begins **implantation** in the uterine lining, usually high up, posterior wall or fundus, if the geography is typical.

Implantation is an invasion that the uterus has prepared for. Trophoblast cells fuse into a **syncytiotrophoblast** that burrows; they secrete **hCG**, which is the molecule a pregnancy test is sniffing for, and which asks the ovary to keep the progesterone going. The inner cell mass becomes a bilaminar disc (epiblast and hypoblast). Week 2 is sometimes nicknamed "the week of twos." Cute. Load-bearing.

A lot of conceptions do not get this far. That is a biological observation, not a sermon. Early human development is a filter as well as a factory.

## Gastrulation: The Most Important Event In Your Life, Says Wolpert 🎸

Lewis Wolpert liked to say that it is not birth, marriage, or death, but **gastrulation** which is truly the most important time in your life.

The line is a workshop quip from 1979 that a colleague, Jonathan Slack, dressed in its canonical clothes. Wolpert accepted the authorship. Developmental biologists put it on posters. It is half joke, half professional bias, and half true, which is too many halves, which is the point. Gastrulation is when a disc of cells becomes a creature with a front, a back, an inside, and an outside. If that goes wrong, there is often no later story to have opinions about.

In humans this is **week 3**. A **primitive streak** appears in the epiblast. Cells dive through it (ingression), change character (epithelial to mesenchymal), and layer themselves:

- first in, deepest: **endoderm**
- next: **mesoderm**
- the ones who stay put, facing the amniotic cavity: **ectoderm**

The **notochord** — a mesodermal rod — sets a midline and induces the nervous system. The disc now has axes: head-tail, back-belly, left-right. You have a body plan in outline, still only millimetres, and already past the point where "a ball of equivalent cells" is a useful description.

Wolpert's other idea, **positional information**, is the proposed mechanism: cells read coordinates, often as concentrations of **morphogens** (the French-flag model: different thresholds, different fates), and interpret them according to their history. Hox genes, later, are part of how the head-to-tail axis is given an address system. We will come back to the mystery of this. For now: gastrulation is the arrangement. Everything after is development of the arrangement.

## Three Germ Layers, Then Tissues 🧱

A **germ layer** is not a disease. It is a primary job description.

**Ectoderm** (outer): epidermis, hair, nails, the lens of the eye — and, by a folding trick, the entire **nervous system**. The neural plate rolls into a tube. Brain and spinal cord are skin that learned to think, if you want a sentence that is slightly too neat and still useful. Neural crest cells wander off and become a ridiculous list: peripheral neurons, glia, pigment cells, parts of the face. Ectoderm is the interface with the world, including the interface that is a mind.

**Mesoderm** (middle): muscle, bone, connective tissue, the heart, vessels, blood, kidney, gonads, the dermis. The stuff that moves, supports, pumps, and filters. Somites along the axis are a clock you can almost see, pairing off like carriages.

**Endoderm** (inner): the lining of the gut tube, and the organs that bud from it — lungs, liver, pancreas, thyroid, the lining of the bladder. Inner tube. You are, at this resolution, a gut with a wrapping.

Organs are collaborations. A lung is endoderm lining plus mesoderm plumbing and cartilage. Skin is ectoderm epidermis plus mesoderm dermis. The three-layer story is the start of tissue identity, not a filing cabinet that never opens between drawers.

From here, **organogenesis**: heart tubes, limb buds, a face folding itself together, a neural tube that must close at both ends on a schedule. The construction is fast, and it is 3D, and it is running on morphogen gradients, differential adhesion, and a lot of apoptosis (the webbing of the hands, again). Chapter 09's demolition permit, used as a sculptor's chisel.

## Embryo, Then Fetus: Why The First Trimester Is A Construction Site 🏗️

Language first, because the words are doing political work whether we like it or not.

The **embryonic period**, in the standard embryological sense, is fertilisation through the end of week 8 (Carnegie stages 1–23). By then the major organ systems exist in outline: a heart that has been pumping for weeks already, limb buds that are becoming limbs, a face, a closed neural tube (if that closure worked). The **fetal period** is week 9 to birth: growth, refinement, ossification, the brain doing the long, expensive thing brains do. This is post-fertilisation timing. Clinical "gestational age" usually counts from the last menstrual period, about two weeks earlier. Two calendars, one process. Mix them up and every "week 6" in a conversation becomes a small fight.

The **first trimester** (in clinical counting, through week 12 or 13) overlaps the entire embryonic period and the start of fetal life. It is high-stakes because **this is when the body plan and the organs are being put in place**, not because a trimester is magic. A disruption now — a chromosome that did not disjoin, a neural tube that did not close, a teratogen at the wrong hour — hits a system that is still being specified. Later, many of the same insults hit a system that is growing. Different consequences. This is why embryology textbooks sound urgent about weeks 3–8. They are describing a construction schedule, not issuing a moral.

By the end of the embryonic period you are still only a few centimetres. Most of the mass of a newborn is fetal-period work: bigger, more wired, lungs that will have to work on short notice, a fat reserve, a brain that has only just begun the career it will spend two decades finishing. Assembly, then expansion. First the arrangement, then the volume.

## The Placenta: A Temporary Organ And A Diplomatic Incident 🕊️

The placenta is the only organ in human life that is designed to be disposable, built by two genomes in negotiation, and thrown away at birth.

It is not a simple filter. It is a **temporary organ** with several jobs:

- **Exchange.** Oxygen and carbon dioxide move by diffusion. Glucose uses transporters (facilitated diffusion). Amino acids are often pumped (active transport). The fetal and maternal bloods do **not** mix in a healthy placenta. Fetal villi — tree-shaped, capillary-cored — sit in lakes of maternal blood (the intervillous space). Nutrients and gases cross the trophoblast layers. Calories reach the fetus as molecules in those villi, then the **umbilical vein** (yes, vein: oxygenated, which is the naming trap). Waste and deoxygenated blood return via the umbilical arteries.
- **How oxygen actually crosses.** Maternal haemoglobin lets go; dissolved O₂ diffuses; **fetal haemoglobin (HbF)** grabs harder. HbF is α₂γ₂ rather than the adult α₂β₂, and it binds 2,3-BPG less well, so its oxygen dissociation curve sits to the left. P50 around 19 mm Hg versus about 27 for adult HbA. The fetus lives at a lower PO₂ than you do, and compensates with affinity, a higher haematocrit, and a placenta that is doing its best impression of a lung while also being a gut and a kidney and an endocrine gland. Early on, the embryo is kept in a *low*-oxygen environment on purpose; full maternal perfusion of the intervillous space ramps later. "More oxygen sooner" is not always kinder. Construction has a preferred atmosphere.
- **Endocrine.** hCG, progesterone support, later a factory of hormones that rewrite maternal physiology. The placenta is an organ with opinions about the host.
- **Immune negotiation.** The fetus is half "non-self" in the classic immunology cartoon. The placenta is the embassy. Trophoblast cells play a restrained MHC game, local immune cells in the uterus (including specialised natural killer cells) talk to invading trophoblast about how deeply to remodel the spiral arteries, and antibodies of some classes can cross later as a starter pack for the newborn. It is not that the maternal immune system is "off." It is that the interface has been, over evolutionary time, turned into a managed conversation. Sometimes the conversation fails. That is a clinic's problem. This is the mechanism.

About 40% of the oxygen the pregnant uterus takes up is used by the placenta itself. The embassy has a heating bill. The fetus gets what remains after the diplomat has eaten.

When people say "the baby is eating for two," they are pointing, sloppily, at this organ. Food becomes maternal blood glucose and amino acids and fats; those cross according to the rules above; the fetus burns them or builds with them. There is no tiny mouth at the placenta. There is transport physics and a lot of membrane.

## Twins: One Egg Or Two, And When The Split Happens 👯

**Dizygotic** (fraternal) twins: two eggs, two sperm, two zygotes. They are siblings who shared a due date. Two chorions, two amnions, typically two placentas (sometimes fused by proximity). Genetically they are ordinary siblings.

**Monozygotic** (identical) twins: one zygote that becomes two embryos. Same starting genome, not the same person — epigenetics, later mutations, and the environment in the womb see to that. The **timing of the split** is the classic textbook, and it is still the useful first model:

- Split in the first ~3 days (before the trophectoderm is a single coat): two blastocysts, **dichorionic, diamniotic** — two chorions, two sacs. Looks like dizygotic twins on a scan until someone checks the DNA. Maybe a quarter of MZ twins.
- Split around days 4–8 (inner cell mass divides inside one trophoblast): **monochorionic, diamniotic** — one placenta, two sacs. The common MZ pattern.
- Split later, after the amnion is already a shared room: **monochorionic, monoamniotic**. Rare, crowded.
- Very late: incomplete split, **conjoined** twins. Rarer still.

IVF and time-lapse imaging have made the early-split story less tidy than Corner's 1922 cartoon. Inner-cell-mass splitting inside a blastocyst is seen; a clean two-cell split into two fully separate embryos is not filmed as often as the textbooks imply. The map is still the timing-and-membranes map. The mechanism is still being watched.

Wolpert, in a colder mood, used identical twins as a philosophical instrument: an early embryo can become two people, which is one reason he thought "the early embryo should not be thought of as a human being." You do not have to share his ethics to see his embryology. Regulation — the ability of a half-sized early embryo to make a whole — is a real, strange property of mammals. We are not mosaic flies. We can, for a short window, be cut and still complete the pattern. That window closes.

## When The Chromosome Dance Misses A Step ⚖️

**Nondisjunction** is the name for a failed separation.

In meiosis I, homologous chromosomes should part. If they do not — or if they never made a proper chiasma and wander as univalents — one daughter cell gets both, the other gets neither. In meiosis II, sister chromatids should part. If they do not, the same imbalance appears by a different route. A gamete with 24 chromosomes, or 22, meets a typical 23, and the zygote is **trisomic** (three copies of one chromosome) or **monosomic** (one copy).

Most such conceptions do not implant, or they end early. That is the filter again. Among those that continue, some aneuploidies are seen in live births: extra 21 (Down syndrome), extra 18, extra 13, extra or missing sex chromosomes. Monosomy of an autosome is almost always lethal very early. 45,X (Turner) is the notable sex-chromosome monosomy that can be live-born.

Mechanism, not a counselling leaflet:

- Recombination that is missing, too close to the centromere, or too far away, makes a bivalent that is bad at lining up.
- Cohesin — the glue that holds sisters — has to last, in oocytes, from fetal life until ovulation. That is a long wait for a protein complex.
- Maternal meiosis I is where most studied human trisomies start. Maternal age is the strongest known correlate, which is an epidemiological fact about cohesin and time and oocyte biology, not a moral and not a prediction about any particular pregnancy.

This is a failed dance. It is common in our species. Hassold and Hunt's line is worth keeping: aneuploidy is the most commonly identified chromosome abnormality in humans, and a large fraction of early losses carry it. We are a lineage whose meiosis is sloppy compared with some other animals, especially in eggs, especially later. Why we are sloppy is an unsolved evolutionary question. That we are sloppy is a mechanism.

Mosaicism — nondisjunction in a mitotic cleavage division *after* fertilisation — can produce a body that is a patchwork of counts. Another reminder that "the karyotype" is a typical cell's portrait, not a mystical essence.

## Epigenetic Reprogramming: Wipe, Keep, Rewrite 🧽

Sperm and egg arrive with different chromatin fashions. Sperm DNA is packed with protamines, almost crystalline. The egg is a different country. If those marks all persisted, the zygote would be a messy argument between two differentiated cells, not a totipotent start.

So the early embryo **reprogrammes**. DNA methylation is stripped down, the paternal genome faster (active demethylation), the maternal genome more passively over cleavage divisions, in the mouse story; humans are in the same business with their own timing. Histone marks are rewritten. The genome becomes, briefly, a more open book. Zygotic genome activation is part of this, and a consequence of it.

Not everything is wiped. **Imprints** — parent-of-origin methylation marks at specific control regions — are supposed to survive, so that a handful of genes remember which parent they came from. That memory is why you need both a maternal and a paternal haploid set; two sperm genomes or two egg genomes are not a person, they are a specific kind of failed pregnancy (hydatidiform mole, and related disasters). Primordial germ cells, later, will wipe imprints too and write new ones according to the sex of the fetus, so the next generation gets a fresh parental stamp.

Two erasures, then, in a life: one just after fertilisation, to make a body; one in the germline, to make the next gametes honest about their parent. We do not have a complete parts list for either. We know they happen, and we know that when they misfire, development notices.

## The Mystery: A Body Plan From One Cell, No Foreman 🧭

Here is the bit that should keep you up.

A zygote has a genome, a pile of maternal kit, and a membrane. It does not have a map of a human. And yet, repeatedly, it makes one: head at one end, gut inside, two arms, a heart on the left, a liver that knows it is a liver.

The working theory is not a homunculus. It is **self-organisation plus inherited coordinates**:

- asymmetries already in the egg and in the blastocyst (inner cell mass position, trophectoderm)
- gastrulation as the symmetry-breaking festival
- morphogen gradients (Shh, BMPs, Wnts, FGFs, retinoic acid) that cells read as position
- gene-regulatory networks, including Hox clusters, that interpret position into identity
- differential adhesion, mechanical forces, and clocks (somites) that turn identity into anatomy
- apoptosis and proliferation as the sculptor and the builder

Wolpert's positional information is the claim that cells know *where* they are and then decide *what* to be. The French flag is the toy model. Real embryos are noisier, more dynamic, and more able to regulate (see twins) than a static flag. Alternative and complementary pictures — reaction-diffusion, mechanical buckling, "the community tells you who you are" — are not mutually exclusive. A football crowd forms a terrace, remember? Membranes did that in chapter 02 without a plan. Bodies may be doing a high-end version of the same trick: local rules, global order.

We can now film a lot of this in other animals, and some of it in human embryos donated to research, and in stem-cell "gastruloids" that recapitulate pieces of the pattern in a dish. We still cannot write down, from first principles, why *this* genome plus *this* egg cytoplasm yields a human and not a fish. The programme is in there. The compiler is the physics of cells. The mystery is not magic. It is that the compiler is us, and we do not have the source code in a form we can read like a book.

No foreman. A city from chapter 09, copied, specialised, folded, and told by its neighbours what time it is. That is a person, as biology. The rest is philosophy, law, and breakfast.

## The Hard Line, Again 🚫

This chapter is not medical advice. It is not a pregnancy manual. It is not a guide to screening, to risk, to what anyone should do. Mechanisms are not destinies. If you are here because a real body is pregnant, or might be, talk to a human whose job is that. The curiosity cabinet will still be here when you want the chromosome dance as a chromosome dance.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **How a body plan is specified from one cell.** Morphogens, Hox, mechanics, and inherited egg polarity are the toolkit. The full human compile step is not in anyone's notebook.
- **What actually triggers monozygotic twinning.** Timing-and-membranes is a good map. The cellular cause of the split is still mostly "it happened."
- **Why human meiosis, especially in oocytes, is so error-prone.** Cohesin ageing is a mechanism. Why our lineage tolerates (or cannot avoid) this rate is evolutionary unfinished business.
- **How imprints are protected during the global methylation wipe.** We know they are. The full "do not touch this bit" machinery is not a closed book.
- **How much of early human development we have simply never seen.** A lot of the textbook is mouse, chick, or a small set of donated human embryos. Gastruloids are a new mirror. Mirrors lie in useful ways.
- **Where, if anywhere, a "person" starts.** Biology will give you zygotes, primitive streaks, viability, brains. It will not give you the ethical noun. Anyone who says the embryology settles the noun is selling a mapping they brought with them.

[To err (meiotically) is human: the genesis of human aneuploidy](https://www.nature.com/articles/35066065). *Nature Reviews Genetics.* Also [StatPearls, Genetics, Nondisjunction](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482240/). This is mechanism, not a clinical guideline.
- Epigenetic reprogramming after fertilisation, imprint survival, later PGC erasure: Guo, H. et al. (2014) and subsequent human-embryo methylome papers; reviews such as [Rebooting the epigenomes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7724468/).
- Oocyte arrest (prophase I, then metaphase II until fertilisation) and polar-body asymmetry: standard mammalian oogenesis; see reviews on meiotic arrest and cAMP.
