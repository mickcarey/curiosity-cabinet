# Reading The Water 🌊

*An Australian sea companion: rockpool → tide → salt → substrate → what could actually live here*

This is the wet voice of [The Living World](../🌱-the-living-world/README.md).

Land has trees that stand still long enough for you to have an opinion. Water has the nerve to move twice a day, hide the evidence, and then pretend it was always like this. That is why it needs its own shelf.

I am the AI narrator again. Mick wanted to walk into a forest and read it. Then he remembered that most of the planet is not a forest, it is a rock with a wet habit, and Port Phillip is right there looking like a map that forgot to include the interesting bits. So: coasts, rockpools, estuaries, kelp, mangroves. The water you can stand next to without a spaceship.

The science lives next door. Chapter 17 of the living-world series is the global seascape method. These notes are the local application. The [bush companion](../🌲-reading-the-bush/README.md) is the dry twin. Same game, different medium.

## How To Use This 🧭

Look. Infer. Then check. Same as the bush, except the exam paper is tidal.

1. **Notice the energy** — swell, chop, a bay that behaves, a headland that does not.
2. **Notice the salt and the fresh** — river mouth, hypersaline puddle, the weird in-between of an estuary.
3. **Notice the floor** — rock, sand, mud, seagrass, the argument between them.
4. **Read the zonation** — the barnacle line is a tide table. The kelp is a light-and-nutrient speech. The mangrove is a sediment-and-salt speech.
5. **Ask what else would belong** — and what would be a tropical holiday with no return ticket.
6. **Check later** — a tide chart, a field guide, a photo from the other low tide. Water lies on a schedule.

This is literacy, not a licence to eat anything with a shell. The ocean is not a menu.

## What This Is Not 🚫

- Not a fish ID bible. Indicator fauna, not a Pokémon list.
- Not a spearfishing manual, a swimming lesson, or "this shellfish is fine."
- Not a complete guide to Australian marine habitats. We need the *types*.
- Not the Reef documentary. If you want coral logic, it can visit. It does not get to move in and redecorate Port Phillip.
- Not UFO water. The alien-looking bit of Earth is still Earth. Actual other worlds live in the [living-world series](../🌱-the-living-world/README.md), Arc 9.

If a rockpool looks like a mess of slime and snacks, you are in the right place. That mess is zonation, feeding, and a timetable.

## The Trail (chapters still in the pack)

Until a chapter exists, it lives here as a description, not a dead link.

**01. Tides, salt, and the shape of the shore**
How Australian coasts actually work. Tides, swell, aspect. Why a west-facing rock platform is not an east-facing bay. Why Port Phillip can look polite from a ferry and still be a different country from Bass Strait. Energy and salt are the first two cards. Organisms are the comment they leave in the margin.

**02. Rockpools as compressed textbooks**
Zonation you can see in ten metres: lichens, barnacles, molluscs, anemones, the "if this is here the tide reaches here" inference. A rockpool is a landscape painting that gets rewritten twice a day. That is annoying for picnics and perfect for literacy.

**03. Kelp, seagrass, mangrove, estuary**
The big habitat types of south-east Australia as a codebook. What each one says about light, salt, and sediment. Kelp is not "seaweed" as a personality. Seagrass is not a failed lawn. Mangroves are not a swamp you drive past. Estuaries are mixing desks. Once you can tell the rooms apart, the furniture starts making sense.

**04. The animals that give the game away**
Indicator fauna without becoming a catalogue. Filter feeders, grazers, the odd vertebrate cameo. An octopus as "the smart puddle." What is here tells you about oxygen, food, and hiding places. What is *missing* tells you about energy, harvest, or a bay that looks healthier than it is.

**05. Therefore this water could hold that**
The marine version of the bush "grow Y here" chapter. If the rockpool, seagrass, or mangrove is X, the conditions are Y, so Z is plausible. Also why tropical dreams die in Port Phillip, why Bass Strait is not a swimming pool with opinions, and why "I saw it on the Reef" is not an argument that belongs at Point Lonsdale.

**06. Walkable water case studies**
Worked examples:

- Port Phillip: bay versus heads
- A Great Ocean Road rock platform
- A Victorian estuary / mangrove edge
- Sunshine Coast rock and warmer water
- A "this would be Reef logic" contrast, clearly labelled as travel, not local

Each case: what you see → what it implies → what would *not* belong. Water looks the same until it does not. The absences are the exam.

## A Tiny Taste Of The Method 🔍

You do not need chapter 02 to start. Try this on the next low tide, then come back and forgive your first draft.

| You notice | A decent first inference | A bad leap |
|---|---|---|
| A crisp barnacle line, then bare rock below | That line is the high-water argument. Life is zoned like apartments with worse plumbing | "The rest of the rock is dead" (it is just on a different lease) |
| Thick kelp, cold clear water, surge | Light plus nutrients plus energy. This is a high-output room | "Seaweed means dirty water" |
| Grey-green seagrass in a calm shallows | Sediment, light, less smash. A nursery pretending to be a lawn | "I could plant this in any bay if I tried hard" |
| Mangroves, mud, that sweet-rot smell | Fine sediment, salt-fresh mixing, a coast that is catching leftovers | "Swamp, so nothing interesting lives here" |
| An octopus in a rockpool looking like a discarded sock | Shelter, hunting ground, a tide that leaves groceries | "It is lost" (it is at work) |

Inference is a first draft. Check the tide chart. The water will not wait, but it will repeat itself tomorrow.
