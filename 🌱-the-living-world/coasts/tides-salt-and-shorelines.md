# Tides, Salt and Shorelines 🌊

A coast is shaped by moving water, and its organisms must cope with that movement. Waves apply force, tides alternate immersion and exposure, currents deliver food, and freshwater changes the chemistry.

Two shores a short distance apart can therefore support different communities. Port Phillip's entrance and its sheltered inner margins are connected water, but very different habitats.

[Reading a seascape](../reading-a-seascape.md) brings these conditions together with the organisms that respond to them.

## 🌬️ Waves, tides and currents do different things

**Wind waves** form as wind transfers energy to the water. Their development depends on wind strength, duration and **fetch**, the distance over which the wind acts. A large bay can develop substantial waves even when sheltered from ocean swell.

**Swell** is wave energy that has travelled away from its generating weather system. It can reach a shore under apparently calm local skies. Wave period, direction and the shape of the seabed influence how it behaves near land.

**Tides** are long-period changes associated primarily with the gravitational effects of the Moon and Sun, modified by the shape and depth of ocean basins and coasts. They produce changing water levels and tidal currents.

Currents also arise from wind, density differences and other processes. A calm surface does not establish that the water beneath it is stationary.

## 🌙 Tide patterns are local

Some coasts have two broadly similar high and low tides each day; others have unequal pairs or predominantly one of each. The timing and range depend on the location.

Spring tides generally occur around new and full moons, when the solar and lunar contributions reinforce one another more strongly. Neap tides occur around the quarter moons, when the range is generally smaller. “Spring” has nothing to do with the season; the tide has enough scheduling complications already.

Predictions describe the astronomical component. Wind and atmospheric pressure can change actual water levels, while river flow matters near mouths and estuaries. [The Bureau of Meteorology explains these distinctions](https://www.bom.gov.au/resources/learn-and-explore/marine-knowledge-centre/tides-and-sea-level).

For an observation, record the place and time and use predictions for that location. A tide listed for another side of a bay may differ in both timing and height.

## 🚪 Port Phillip shows why range and current differ

A large volume of water exchanges through Port Phillip's relatively narrow entrance. This can produce strong currents at the Heads even though the vertical tidal range farther inside the bay is comparatively small.

Water levels inside and outside do not rise and fall in perfect synchrony. Consequently, slack water at the entrance does not simply coincide with local high or low water. The [Bureau's Port Phillip Heads explanation](https://www.bom.gov.au/australia/tides/about/p4a-port-phillip.shtml) describes this phase difference.

Farther inside, ocean swell is much reduced and wind-generated waves become more important. Near the entrance, currents and the irregular seabed produce another set of conditions. “The bay is sheltered” is therefore a regional description with very important local qualifications.

## 🧂 Salt changes what cells must regulate

Typical open-ocean salinity is around 35 grams of dissolved salts per kilogram of seawater, but coastal values vary.

Freshwater input lowers salinity. Evaporation removes water while leaving salts behind. An isolated pool can become saltier on a hot day or much fresher during rain; an estuary can change markedly after a flood.

Water crosses biological membranes in response to differences in dissolved substances. Organisms must maintain suitable internal conditions despite those movements. Species differ in how closely their body fluids follow the surrounding water and how actively they regulate particular ions.

An animal adapted to stable seawater may be unable to tolerate a rapid freshwater pulse. Estuarine species often cope with a wider range, but those abilities have limits and energetic costs. Salt is an ecological constraint, not simply flavouring.

## 💧 Fresh and salt water need not mix evenly

Fresh water is generally less dense than seawater under comparable conditions. It can spread above saltier water, producing a stratified water column. Tidal mixing, wind and channel shape can weaken or disrupt that layering.

This matters for oxygen as well as salinity. If deeper water is poorly replenished while respiration consumes oxygen, conditions near the bottom can differ substantially from the surface.

A brown river plume is visible evidence of material entering the sea. It does not reveal the exact salinity, oxygen level or pollutant concentration. Those require measurements. [Estuarine habitats](kelp-seagrass-mangroves-and-estuaries.md) show how organisms respond to these gradients.

## 🪨 The seabed supplies different opportunities

Stable rock provides attachment sites for algae and animals. Sand can support burrowing communities and, where suitable, rooted seagrass. Fine mud often accumulates in sheltered areas and supports its own organisms and chemistry.

The substrate is also modified by water movement. Waves can shift sand over rock, burying attached organisms. Currents can remove fine particles from one place and deposit them elsewhere. Living structures, including seagrass and shellfish beds, can alter those flows in return.

Coastal shape influences exposure. A sheltered cove, projecting headland, narrow inlet and broad mudflat should not be expected to support identical communities even within one region.

## 🐚 Biological bands are not surveyed tide marks

Barnacles, snails and seaweeds often form recognisable zones on rocky shores. Their boundaries reflect the combined effects of immersion, splash, drying, temperature, competition, grazing and recruitment.

A barnacle band's upper edge is therefore evidence about the conditions that species tolerates. It is **not an exact ordinary high-water line**. Exposure can carry spray above tidal inundation, and shaded crevices can provide different conditions at the same height.

Wrack lines have similar limits. Stranded seaweed may record recent delivery by waves and tides, but wind and storms can move it afterwards. It may have travelled from a habitat some distance away.

The [rockpool ecology note](rockpool-ecology.md) explains how these gradients become visible at small scales.

## 📝 A useful shore description

Record the substrate, degree of exposure, observed water movement, signs of freshwater input and the organisms present. Then distinguish the observation from its possible cause:

> **Observed:** fine sediment and seagrass in a sheltered shallow area; exposed rock and attached brown algae around the headland.
>
> **Inference:** differences in sediment stability and wave exposure may help explain the vegetation.

Temperature, water clarity and seasonal history could refine that explanation. The aim is to build a testable account of the shore, not make one photograph explain an entire coast.
