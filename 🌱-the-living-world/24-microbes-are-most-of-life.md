# Microbes Are Most Of Life 🦠

You are a late, loud minority.

That is not an insult. It is a census. The planet you walk around on, naming birds and arguing about trees, has spent almost all of its living history as a microbial operation. Animals are a recent marketing campaign. Plants, for all their carbon, are a late architectural fad: wood is a clever way to stack sugar in the sky. The chemistry, the headcount, and the clock all say the same rude thing. This is a microbial planet that grew a few conspicuous decorations.

I am the AI narrator. Mick is the human who asked for a deep dive into life and then, reasonably, spent a lot of chapters looking in the mirror. [Chapter 09](09-the-cell-as-a-city.md) gave you a eukaryotic city: nucleus, mitochondria, zoning. Fair. That is the city you are made of. This chapter is the admission that most of Earth's cities never invented a downtown. They are one-room operations with a work ethic, a membrane, and a talent for chemistries your cells outsource or never learned.

The rest of the series will spend a lot of time on the loud minority, because you live in one. Just remember whose planet it is.

This is not the germ chapter. Pasteur and Koch were right that some microbes make you sick. They were not a census. Treating bacteria as a crime wave is like treating drivers as a pile-up: true at the accident, false as a theory of traffic. Most microbial life is not interested in you. You are a habitat, a late one, and not the main event.

## A Late, Loud Minority 📣

Three ways of counting, none of which put you in the majority.

**By count.** Whitman, Coleman, and Wiebe, in 1998, put prokaryotic cells on Earth at something like **10³⁰** — four to six million trillion trillion. Later work revised the subsurface biomass down, which is the adult version of "we were a bit excited about the basement." The order of magnitude for *cells* held.

Bar-On, Phillips, and Milo's 2018 census is the one to carry: about **550 gigatonnes of carbon** in living things. Plants take ~450 of those, mostly as wood. Bacteria ~70. Fungi ~12. Archaea ~7. Protists ~4. Animals ~2. Viruses ~0.2. Humans: **0.06**. You are a rounding error with a podcast. Most of the bacterial and archaeal carbon is not in a forest you can photograph. It is in soil, sediment, and the deep subsurface, living slowly in the dark.

Plants win the carbon trophy because lignin is a skyscraper. Microbes win the *population* trophy, the *chemistry* trophy, and the *time* trophy. If you only count standing timber, you will conclude Earth is a botanical planet. If you count selves, metabolisms, and years, you will not.

One more census trick, because size lies. *Prochlorococcus* is a cyanobacterium too small to have been noticed for most of oceanography's career, and it may be the most abundant photosynthetic cell on Earth: on the order of 10²⁷ individuals, a millilitre of surface seawater wearing a hundred thousand of them like glitter. It does not make a forest. It makes a large fraction of the ocean's oxygen and a large fraction of the ocean's lunch. The loud minority photographs badly. The actual majority photographs not at all.

**By chemistry.** Eukaryotes are gourmets with a short menu: sunlight via stolen chloroplasts, or someone else's organic molecules, plus oxygen if they can get it. Microbes eat rock, air, rust, sulfur, hydrogen, methane, and the leftovers of everyone else. Nitrogenase, methanogenesis, anoxygenic photosynthesis, anammox, the sulfur circus: these are not side quests. They are how the planet's CHNOPS actually moves. Your proteins are a nitrogen story that a handful of prokaryotes had to start. [Chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) already said so. This chapter is who does the work.

**By history.** LUCA, from [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md), is already a competent prokaryote-grade cell around four billion years ago, give or take an embarrassing error bar. Animals as a body-plan fashion show are about **540 million years** old. Land plants younger still. Eukaryotic cells as a grade of city maybe 1.5–1.8 billion years. For most of the time life has existed here, there was nobody to have a favourite animal. There were cells, gases, and a long argument with rocks. You are not the default. You are the encore.

If you want a sports version: eukaryotes are the grand final that gets the broadcast. Microbes are the season. The season is most of the league, most of the minutes, and all of the plumbing. Skipping it because the grand final has better lighting is how you misunderstand football.

## Three Domains, Hiding In The Furniture 🌳

School sold a cartoon: prokaryotes versus eukaryotes, which is a bit like sorting the world into "has a nucleus" and "does not," then acting as if that were a family tree. It is a floor plan. It is not kinship.

In 1977, Carl Woese and George Fox read a molecule everyone already had and nobody was using as a census. **Ribosomal RNA** — specifically the small-subunit version, 16S in bacteria and archaea, 18S in eukaryotes — is ancient, slow-changing, and present in every cellular lineage that still bothers to make protein. The ribosome is the machine that never unionised, as [chapter 04](04-how-cells-read-and-rewrite-the-library.md) put it.

Compare the sequences and you get relatedness that morphology cannot see. Shape lied. Stain lied. "Looks like a bacterium" lied. What they found, in a 2.5-page paper with a table of numbers, was not two groups. It was **three**. The original method was oligonucleotide cataloguing — scissors and a table — not a pretty tree from a laptop. The tree came later. The insult to the furniture came first.

Most biologists did not notice. The paper was short, the method was obscure, and the five-kingdom poster was already laminated. Woese was not easy company, which did not help. The molecule did the work anyway.

The third was hiding inside "bacteria," wearing a name that made it sound like a quirky cousin: **archaebacteria**. Methanogens, mostly, at first. Then the rest of a domain. In 1990, Woese, Otto Kandler, and Mark Wheelis proposed the formal furniture: three **domains** above kingdoms. **Bacteria. Archaea. Eukarya.** The name change was the point. Archaea are not weird bacteria. Calling them that is like calling a guitar a weird piano because both have strings.

16S became the barcode of the microbial world. Pace and others took it out of cultures and into the environment: extract the RNA, or the gene, from a drop of water or a pinch of mud, and read who is there without growing anyone. That move — **culture-independent** census — is how microbiology stopped being a hobby of whoever would grow on a plate.

The tree that was hiding in the furniture turned out to have whole wings we had never visited. Candidate phyla. The **CPR** bacteria (the Candidate Phyla Radiation, later Patescibacteria), tiny and often sponging on other cells. The **DPANN** archaea, same trick, different house. Asgard archaea, which [chapter 09](09-the-cell-as-a-city.md) already borrowed as the host's family: eukaryote-like proteins in a prokaryote-grade cell, which is the plot twist that makes us look like archaea who swallowed a bacterium and never left.

A live argument, which [chapter 16](16-mysteries-theories-and-the-unsolved.md) will keep: maybe there are only **two** primary domains, and eukaryotes sit *inside* Archaea. Williams and colleagues, in 2020, put a lot of phylogenomic weight behind that two-domain tree, with Heimdallarchaeota as a current best guess for the closest archaeal relatives of the nuclear lineage. Woese's three-domain map was a genuine discovery. Maps get redrawn. The discovery that does not get redrawn is this: looking at cells with a microscope and sorting them by "nucleus or not" hid half the tree.

## Archaea Are Not Weird Bacteria 🔥

If you remember one sentence from this heading, make it that one.

Archaea often *look* bacterial under a cheap lens: small, no nucleus, circular chromosome, a work ethic. The furniture is different.

- **Membranes.** Bacteria and eukaryotes mostly use fatty acids, ester-linked to a glycerol-3-phosphate backbone. Archaea use **isoprenoid** chains, **ether**-linked, to the mirror-image backbone, glycerol-1-phosphate. Some make monolayer-spanning tetraethers, which is a membrane that refuses to fall apart in a boiling spring. The lipid divide is one of the deepest facts we have. It is also why "prokaryote" is a useful nickname for "no nucleus" and a bad name for a clade.
- **Walls.** No peptidoglycan. Penicillin is a weapon against a bag archaea did not bring. Some archaea use pseudopeptidoglycan; many use an S-layer, a protein coat, or almost nothing. Different architecture, not a discount version.
- **Information.** Archaeal transcription and translation look more like yours than like *E. coli*'s. Histones show up. RNA polymerase has the eukaryotic vibe. This is not a coincidence if the host of the mitochondrial merger was an archaeon.
- **Metabolism.** **Methanogenesis** — making methane from CO₂ and hydrogen, or from acetate, or from methanol — is an archaeal party trick. Bacteria do not do it. Cows do not do it. The cow is a mammal wrapped around an archaeal factory. Wetlands, sediments, your gut if you have the tenants: the methane in the sky is, in large part, an archaeal exhalation.

The old postcard said archaea live only in extremes: boiling vents, saturated salt, acid that would pickle a battery. That was sampling bias wearing a documentary voiceover. The first ones we grew *were* the drama queens, because drama queens grow in conditions that kill the competition.

Archaea are also in the open ocean, in ordinary soils, in you. **Ammonia-oxidising archaea** (Thaumarchaeota and friends) do a quiet, enormous fraction of the first step of nitrification, especially in the sea. Haloarchaea turn salt ponds pink and run photosynthesis-adjacent tricks with bacteriorhodopsin, which is a light-driven proton pump, not a chloroplast. Methanogens sit in wetlands, landfills, and the rumen. They are not circus acts. They are staff.

Even the motors are a different invention. An archaeal flagellum — an **archaellum** — is not homologous to a bacterial flagellum. Same job, different parts, like a drum kit and a cajón both keeping time. Convergence reaches down into motility. That is not a trivia item. It is what "not a weird bacterium" looks like when you open the bonnet.

A guitar is not a weird piano. It is a different instrument that happens to fit in the same van.

## One Room, Still A City 🏘️

[Chapter 09](09-the-cell-as-a-city.md) spent its pages on a eukaryotic floor plan: organelles, a nucleus, a social contract. A bacterium is one room. It is still a city.

Membrane as border. Genome as library, usually one circular chromosome plus plasmids as the pamphlets that circulate. Ribosomes as factories. A cell wall as the load-bearing bag. Chemotaxis as a nose. Pili as both handshake and USB cable. No Golgi. No mitochondria. The electron-transport chain sits in the plasma membrane, which is why a bacterium can be a battery the size of itself.

**Biofilms** are what happens when the one-room cities form a district. Sticky matrix, gradients of oxygen and food, specialists at the edge and dormancy in the middle. A plaque on a tooth is a city. A streamer in a pipe is a city. Soil crumbs are cities. Multicellularity did not invent cooperation. It promoted a civic kit microbes were already using without a mayor.

The eukaryotic cell is the loud renovation. The default city is still the one-room kind. I say this as an AI narrator who has never once grown a colony on agar, which is the correct amount of humility: most of the authors of this planet have not been introduced, and I am not going to pretend a chapter is a handshake.

## Metabolism Is The Real Biodiversity ⚡️

School biodiversity is feathers, flowers, and a mammal that made the poster. Fine, as a nature documentary. As a map of what life *does*, it is a greatest-hits album that skipped the band.

The interesting diversity is **how you pay the energy bill**.

Think of a redox tower as a staircase of electron donors and acceptors. Eukaryotes mostly ride a few of the big steps: organic carbon down to oxygen, or sunlight via a stolen panel. Microbes use the whole staircase, including the half-steps a mitochondrion would not bother lacing up for.

**Photosynthesis**, several times, several chemistries. Oxygenic photosynthesis — water-splitting, oxygen as waste, the trick that rusted the planet — is a cyanobacterial invention that chloroplasts later stole.

Before that, and still now, there is **anoxygenic** photosynthesis: purple sulfur bacteria, green sulfur bacteria, heliobacteria, Chloroflexi. They use H₂S, H₂, Fe²⁺, organic scraps. They do not oxygenate a sky. They still eat photons. Different photosystems, different pigments, a family of inventions rather than one gadget restyled. [Chapter 13](13-plants-are-not-furniture.md) will treat the leaf as a solar panel. Remember who built the first one, and that "photo" does not have to mean "makes the air you breathe."

**Nitrogenase.** The enzyme that breaks N₂'s smug triple bond. Molybdenum jewellery, sometimes vanadium, a ferocious ATP bill, a hatred of oxygen. Only prokaryotes have it. Eukaryotes who need reactive nitrogen either eat someone who already did the job, or they keep prokaryotes in a nodule and pay them in sugar. Haber–Bosch is industrial nitrogenase with a fossil-fuel habit. [Chapter 14](14-soil-is-alive.md) will run the whole nitrogen cycle. The point here is simpler: **the biosphere's legal entry points for new reactive nitrogen are microbial**, plus lightning, plus us pretending to be a microbe at scale.

**Sulfur.** Oxidise it, reduce it, disproportionate it, live on the redox cliff between sulfate and sulfide. Hydrothermal vents smell like this paragraph. So do some muds. Chemolithoautotrophs eat rock the way a leaf eats light. The animals at vents — tube worms with bacterial tenants — are still outsourcing the interesting chemistry.

**Methanogenesis**, again, because the carbon cycle is not only "plants in, animals out." Some archaea make methane. Some bacteria and archaea eat it. The atmosphere's methane is a microbial conversation with a greenhouse accent.

**Anammox** — anaerobic ammonium oxidation — is bacteria turning ammonium and nitrite into N₂, which is a neat way to close a nitrogen loop without oxygen. Iron reducers, hydrogen oxidisers, the deep subsurface living on a trickle of energy that would bore a mitochondrion to death. Turnover times down there can be years to millennia. A cell that divides once a century is still a cell. It is just not in a hurry, which is the correct attitude if your habitat is a pore in basalt.

Fermentation is the unglamorous rest of the catalogue: sugar to acids, alcohols, gas, the reason bread rises and a swamp smells like a swamp. Eukaryotes do some of this. Microbes did it first and still do most of it, including the steps that turn a forest floor into next year's plants.

Eukaryotes mostly **outsource** this menu. Mitochondria are former bacteria. Chloroplasts are former cyanobacteria. Your gut is a fermenter you did not invent. A cow is a mammal around a vat. A plant's nitrogen is often a microbial contract. The loud minority is metabolically provincial. That is not a moral failing. It is why a rainforest still depends on things you cannot see.

If proteins are the machines, and calories are the unit, metabolism is the *playlist*. Eukaryotes know a few songs. Microbes have the rest of the catalogue, including the ones in tunings your fingers will not stretch to.

## They Built The Stage 🌍

The loud minority likes to talk as if the planet was a theatre waiting for animals. The set was built by the band.

**Oxygen** is a microbial waste product that became weather. Cyanobacteria, then their descendants in chloroplasts, dumped O₂ until the oceans rusted and the sky changed personality. The Great Oxidation was a mass-poisoning from the point of view of the anaerobes, and a promotion from the point of view of anyone who later learned to breathe. [Chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) called oxygen a pyromaniac we made a deal with. The deal was offered by a microbe.

**Soil**, in [chapter 14](14-soil-is-alive.md), is not dirt. It is a structured, chemically opinionated matrix whose interesting verbs — fix, nitrify, denitrify, mine phosphorus, glue crumbs — are microbial. Plants pump carbon below. Microbes run the cycles. A forest without that staff is a sculpture.

**The ocean's living** is mostly microbial even when a whale is photogenic. Primary production at the surface is phytoplankton, a lot of it bacterial (*Prochlorococcus*, *Synechococcus*) and a lot of it very small eukaryotes. The **microbial loop** is the food web that does not bother with a fish: dissolved organic carbon, bacteria, grazers, phage, repeat. [Chapter 17](17-the-ocean-is-another-planet-we-already-have.md) will take you into the dark. The loop is already running in the light.

None of this is a claim that animals do not matter. It is a claim about who installed the plumbing. You can ignore the plumber until the tap fails. The tap has been working for a few billion years.

## The Unculturable Majority 🧫

[Chapter 14](14-soil-is-alive.md) already named the problem, in soil, where a teaspoon is a city and most of the citizens have never been asked to sit on agar. This is the global version of that unread library. I will not rerun Jenny, CEC, or the phosphorus bottleneck. Those have a home. This is the census embarrassment.

The **great plate count anomaly** — Staley and Konopka, 1985 — is the mismatch between what a microscope sees and what a petri dish grows. Classic slogan: less than 1% culturable. Lloyd and colleagues (2018) put the median nearer **0.5% of cells**, and estimated that most of Earth's microbial cells belong to lineages with no cultured representative.

"Unculturable" is often a lie we tell about our hospitality. Some are picky about company: they need a neighbour's vitamin, a specific redox, a surface, a dilution that is not a feast. Some are a metabolism a lab does not know how to host. Some are just slow. Culturomics has been clawing a few of them back, especially from guts, which are friendlier hotels than a basalt pore. The gap is still the story.

Metagenomics can read the books without opening the authors. Assemble a **MAG** — a metagenome-assembled genome — and you have a draft autobiography of someone you have never met. Hug and colleagues' 2016 tree of life, swollen with these drafts, made the cultured minority look like a suburb claiming to be the city.

It cannot yet tell you what most of them *do*. Genes are a draft of a profession. Physiology is the job. We are very good at listing 16S barcodes. We are much worse at saying who is eating what, in the dark, in a pore, at a rate that would not fog a thermometer.

How many *kinds*? That depends what a kind is, which is the next heading but one. Locey and Lennon once extrapolated toward a **trillion** microbial species, which is a number that makes beetle people sit down. Louca and colleagues, using a global 16S census, preferred something more like **one to a few million** OTUs, most of the cells already in the catalogue of common things, with a long tail of rares. Both cannot be right in the same sense. The honest status is: we do not have a finished number, because we do not have a finished unit, and because "species" was a bird word.

Soil is one unread wing. The ocean is another. The subsurface is the stacks we have barely requested. You can walk on this library. You cannot, yet, read it.

## A Second Organ, Not A Yoghurt Advert 🫀

You are not only human cells. [Chapter 09](09-the-cell-as-a-city.md) already said the deep version: mitochondria are former free-living bacteria. The shallow version lives in your gut, on your skin, in your mouth.

[Chapter 08](08-from-mouth-to-mitochondria.md) treated the colon as a fermenter: fibre the small intestine declined, short-chain fatty acids, a 5–10% cut of metabolizable energy on a typical diet, butyrate feeding the lining. That is the calorie story.

[Chapter 11](11-the-human-body-as-an-ecosystem.md) treats the same city as part of the federation: immune school, gut–brain conversation, skin as a zoo. Sender, Fuchs, and Milo put the bacterial headcount at the same order as human cells, the mass at about 0.2 kg. Not a 10:1 overlord. A dense, local, chemically opinionated organ you did not grow from an embryonic germ layer.

This chapter's only job is the planetary reminder. A human microbiome is not a unique miracle. It is the ordinary fact that animals are habitats. A cow is more honest about it. A coral is an animal plus algae plus a microbial cloud, and bleaching is a divorce. Termites outsource wood. You outsource a handful of vitamins, a lot of immune education, and the last station of the disassembly line. A germ-free mouse is the unkind thought experiment: it lives, it is not the same animal, and it is a terrible advert for a powder.

Birth is a colonisation event. Skin, mouth, gut: neighbourhoods with different shops, surprisingly stable once the tenants have unpacked, perturbable with antibiotics, inclined to drift home. That is ecology, not destiny, and not a personality test.

Holobionts — ecosystems wearing a nametag — get their sitting in [chapter 16](16-mysteries-theories-and-the-unsolved.md). The probiotic industry would like this paragraph to become a shopping list. It will not. Association is cheap. Causation is a transplant study. Fibre that reaches the colon is not wasted by default. "Eat this branded strain and become a new person" is a campaign, not a mechanism. The tenants are real. The merch is ahead of the measurement.

## The Other Majority 🧬

Viruses failed the school exam in [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md) and they will not sit down in [chapter 16](16-mysteries-theories-and-the-unsolved.md) either. This is not another vote on whether they are alive. Collect whichever bill you like. The census does not care.

The best current estimate of virus-like particles on Earth is still close to **10³¹** — the Hendrix number, a ten-to-one-ish ratio on prokaryotic cells, most of them **phage**. Mushegian, reviewing the arithmetic in 2020, concluded it is unlikely to be much less or much more. Cobián Güemes and colleagues put a lot of that majority in soil and sediment, which we have under-sampled, because of course we have.

Suttle's marine version: on the order of 10³⁰ viruses in the ocean, something like 10²³ infections per second, a daily murder rate that recycles a startling fraction of microbial carbon back into dissolved stuff. The **viral shunt**. Kill-the-winner: the bloom gets the phage, the rare persist, diversity does not collapse into whoever grew fastest this Tuesday. There is a quieter counter-model too — piggyback-the-winner, lysogeny when hosts are dense — which is science doing what science does when a slogan meets a gradient.

Phage are also an **evolutionary engine**, which is the sentence this heading is for. They kill. They also **transduce**: they pick up a scrap of host DNA and deliver it to the next cell like a postman who sometimes delivers the wrong mail on purpose. They integrate as prophage, sit quietly, and donate genes — toxins, metabolic cassettes, **auxiliary metabolic genes**. Some cyanophages carry *psbA*, a piece of the photosynthetic kit, because a well-fed host is a better factory and a phage is not sentimental about whose factory it is.

CRISPR, from [chapter 04](04-how-cells-read-and-rewrite-the-library.md), is bacteria and archaea keeping a mugshot album of this weather. Horizontal gene transfer is not a side channel. Phage are one of the pipes.

LUCA, if Moody et al. are in the right postcode, was already in an arms race with viruses. The other majority is not a modern infestation. It is original weather. Any Earth story that leaves them out is a biography of football that ignores fouls, and also ignores the transfer window.

## Extremophiles Are The Default, Not The Circus 🌋

[Chapter 12](12-other-animals-same-song-different-arrangement.md) used tardigrades, *Deinococcus*, and vent animals as unofficial astronaut training: which bills are optional. Brains, optional. Oxygen, optional. A nice pH at 20 °C, optional with footnotes. This chapter's correction is about *who lives where*.

The postcard extremophile is a microbe in a boiling spring, interviewed for a documentary, looking like a stunt. *Pyrococcus*, *Methanopyrus*, the ones that laugh at 100 °C; *Picrophilus* in acid that would pickle a battery; *Halobacterium* in salt that would mummify a cucumber. Fine. They are real. They are also the press kit.

The actual distribution of Earth's microbial biomass is mostly **subsurface, dark, and chemically rude**. Bar-On's bacteria and archaea are predominantly not in your garden. They are in sediments and crustal fluids, at pressures and energy fluxes that would make a mitochondrion file a complaint.

A hydrothermal vent is not a sideshow. It is a biosphere with no sundial. Acid mine drainage, hypersaline ponds, Antarctic brine, the pores in basalt: these are large, old habitats. "Extreme" is a word a temperate mammal uses for rooms it cannot enter.

The surface meadow is the circus act. It is wet, sunlit, oxygenated, and recent in planetary terms. Most of the habitable *volume* of Earth is rock and water in the dark. Most of the cells are there, living slowly. Thermophiles are not violating the rules. They are playing the original venue, which happened to be hot, anoxic, and full of metal. Psychrophiles, the cold-lovers, have the rest of the planet's water. Between them they make "room temperature, pH 7, plenty of oxygen" look like a boutique.

When later chapters talk about other worlds, this is the training that matters. Not "a tardigrade on Mars." The useful claim: the chemistry of a self can be the default in corners that make a forest look like a special case.

## Species Was A Bird Word 🏷️

Vertical inheritance is parent to child. **Horizontal gene transfer** is a neighbour sliding you a page. [Chapter 04](04-how-cells-read-and-rewrite-the-library.md) already listed the pipes: transformation (naked DNA), transduction (phage as post), conjugation (a pilus, a plasmid, the original USB-C).

In microbes this is not a footnote. Metabolic genes commute. Photosynthesis genes have hopped. Antibiotic resistance hops so efficiently that a hospital is a weather report. A "species" that swaps a fifth of its genome with the neighbourhood is not a species in Ernst Mayr's sense. Mayr needed a mating dance. Most of the biosphere did not get the memo.

Working labs use a 16S similarity cutoff — 97% was the classic, 99% if you are feeling precise — as a **filing rule**, not a law of nature. Average nucleotide identity around 95–96% is another filing rule. Useful. Arbitrary. The **pangenome** is the honest object: a core of genes the lineage keeps, plus a cloud of optionals that come and go. Two *E. coli* can share a name and not share a lifestyle. One makes vitamin K in a gut. One makes a shiga toxin and a bad week. Same filing label. Different kit. Tettelin's original pangenome work on *Streptococcus* made the point without mercy: add isolates, and the gene count does not saturate. The cloud is the organism.

Ford Doolittle drew the tree as a web on purpose. Informational genes — ribosomes, the conservative core — are stingier about moving. That is why 16S still works as a backbone. Metabolic genes treat the biosphere as an open-source repo. The picture that survived is a reticulate tree: vertical trunk, horizontal vines, viruses as extra weather. [Chapter 16](16-mysteries-theories-and-the-unsolved.md) will sit with "what even is a species" as a mysteries problem. Here the microbial version is enough: **if your biodiversity statistic assumes a bird, it will lie about a bacterium.**

That is why "how many kinds of microbe" is not a number waiting in a drawer. It is a category argument wearing a sequencer.

If you want a music version: a bird species is a song everyone in the band agrees to play. A bacterial "species" is a jam session with a shared drummer and a lot of people sitting in. You can still file the tapes. You should not pretend the filing is the music.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **How many kinds?** A trillion, or a few million, or a different question. OTUs are a filing rule. Species, for microbes, is a different game. We do not have a finished unit, so we do not have a finished count. Until we do, every "biodiversity of the sea" headline is smuggling a bird word into a jam session.
- **What most of them actually do.** Metagenomes are libraries. Physiology is still mostly a rumour, especially in the subsurface and the rare biosphere. Gene names are not lunch. A MAG that says "this one might eat sulfate" is a hypothesis wearing a barcode.
- **Whether a shadow biosphere could hide here.** Life with a different biochemistry — other chirality, other bases, other solvents, a second genesis that never merged with ours — is a live, difficult, possibly untrue idea. Davies, Cleland, and colleagues argued we have mostly searched with tools tuned to *our* kit, which is how you miss a neighbour who does not use our doorbell. Desert varnish, "nanobacteria," odd amino-acid sets: anomalies, not verdicts. [Chapter 19](19-life-as-an-astrobiology-problem.md) is the proper sitting. Here it is only the local embarrassment: the unculturable majority is already huge *inside* known life. That does not prove a second genesis. It does prove that absence of evidence is mostly absence of a good search.
- **Two domains or three?** Asgard archaea make eukaryotes look like a branch inside Archaea. Woese's map was a discovery. The frame may yet move. If it does, "we" were never a third equal sibling. We were a merger inside someone else's house.
- **How much of "you" is the tenants, causally?** Pointed at 08 and 11 on purpose. The yoghurt aisle is not a literature review.
- **What to do with viruses**, still. Not alive-or-dead. Engine, weather, majority.
- **How to put this into a field guide.** The rest of the climb is full of things you can point at. Most of life will not pose. Humility is not optional. It is the method.

A cell is already a city. Most of Earth's cities never built a downtown, never hired a mitochondrion, and still run the planet. The encore grew feathers and wood and opinions. The band underneath did not clock off.

If you only remember one sentence, make it this: **you are a late, loud minority on a microbial planet, and the interesting chemistry is mostly happening without you.** The next chapters will be about bodies, plants, landscapes, and oceans you can walk into. Walk. Just do not confuse the scenery with the staff.
