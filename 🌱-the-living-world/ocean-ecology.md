# Life in the Ocean 🌊

The ocean is a three-dimensional habitat. Light, pressure, temperature, oxygen and food availability change with depth, while currents move organisms and materials sideways. Two places at the same depth can still be very different environments.

Most marine food webs ultimately depend on photosynthesis near the surface. Much of the ocean lies below that illuminated layer, so understanding how food reaches the dark is central to understanding ocean life.

## 💡 Light creates a shallow productive layer

Water absorbs and scatters light. The depth receiving enough light for substantial photosynthesis depends on clarity, season and location. In turbid coastal water it may be shallow; in clear open water it can extend much deeper.

Conventional depth zones provide useful orientation:

| Zone | Approximate depth | Broad conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epipelagic | Surface–200 m | Includes much of the sunlit water |
| Mesopelagic | 200–1,000 m | Diminishing sunlight; generally insufficient for net photosynthetic production |
| Bathypelagic | 1,000–4,000 m | No sunlight; usually cold |
| Abyssopelagic | 4,000–6,000 m | Deep, dark open water |
| Hadal | Below about 6,000 m | Trenches and their associated environments |

These boundaries classify depth, not perfectly uniform biological conditions. The **euphotic zone** is defined by light rather than one fixed depth.

Red wavelengths disappear relatively quickly. In dim deep water, a red animal can be difficult to see because little red light remains to reflect. Bioluminescence supplies another source of light, used in signalling, concealment and hunting. The deep sea has excellent lighting effects and very little general illumination.

## 🌿 Small producers, rapid turnover

**Phytoplankton** include photosynthetic microbes such as diatoms, cyanobacteria and many other algae. Their combined contribution is roughly comparable to land vegetation in global net primary production, despite far less standing biomass.

The difference is turnover. Trees retain large amounts of carbon in wood for years or decades. Many plankton populations grow, are consumed and are replaced quickly. A small standing population can therefore support a large annual flow of production.

Light is only one requirement. Nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and, for diatoms, silicon can limit growth. Large regions with abundant nitrate still have limited phytoplankton production because another requirement, often iron, is scarce.

The biochemical process is explained in [plant biology](plant-biology.md), although many marine producers are not plants in the ordinary taxonomic sense.

## 🌡️ Mixing controls access to nutrients

Sunlight warms the surface, often creating a less dense layer above colder water. A **thermocline** is a region where temperature changes rapidly with depth; salinity also affects density and stratification.

Stratification can keep phytoplankton near light while limiting the upward supply of nutrients. Mixing can replenish nutrients, but can also carry cells into darker water. Productivity depends on the balance, not simply “more mixing is better”.

**Upwelling** brings deeper water towards the surface and can support highly productive regions. Seasonal changes in light and mixing help explain spring blooms in many temperate seas.

Currents also distribute heat, oxygen and larvae. A suitable habitat may lack a species because its offspring rarely arrive. That is one reason [predicting marine habitats](coasts/predicting-marine-habitats.md) requires more than matching temperature.

## ❄️ Food reaches the dark in several ways

Dead plankton, faecal pellets, mucus and other particles sink as **marine snow**. Microbes and animals consume and transform this material during descent. Only a fraction reaches the deep seafloor.

Many organisms also migrate vertically, feeding nearer the surface at night and retreating during daylight. Their movement and waste transport carbon between depths. Large carcasses provide more concentrated, occasional food supplies.

These processes contribute to the **biological carbon pump**. Carbon fixed near the surface can be transported into deeper water and sediments, with storage times depending on where it is transformed and how circulation returns it.

## 🔥 Vents provide another primary energy source

At hydrothermal vents and some cold seeps, microbes obtain energy by exploiting chemical disequilibria. Some use that energy to fix carbon dioxide into organic material. Animals can feed on these microbes or house them as symbionts.

Vent animals inhabit tolerable mixing zones, not the hottest fluid emerging from the seafloor. The thermal and chemical gradients create a patchwork of conditions over short distances.

This is primary production without local sunlight. It does not mean every vent ecosystem is entirely independent of surface photosynthesis: many depend on oxygen or other oxidants ultimately linked to surface processes. The supply of both electron donors and acceptors matters.

Vents are also studied as possible environments for life's origins. Their existence today demonstrates a functioning habitat; it does not prove that life first arose there. [Open questions in biology](open-questions-in-biology.md) keeps those claims separate.

## 🧱 Pressure and oxygen shape the inhabitants

Pressure rises by roughly one atmosphere per ten metres of seawater depth. Deep-living organisms have molecular and physiological adaptations that allow proteins and membranes to function under those conditions.

Gas-filled spaces create particular difficulties, but pressure is not simply an external crushing force acting on an otherwise empty animal. The chemistry of cells and the behaviour of gases are central constraints.

Oxygen varies with circulation, temperature and biological consumption. Where decomposition uses oxygen faster than circulation replenishes it, low-oxygen zones can develop. They have their own communities and important effects on nutrient cycling.

A vent, a muddy plain, a rocky seamount and a trench are consequently distinct habitats. “Deep sea” locates a place vertically; it does not describe its full ecology.
