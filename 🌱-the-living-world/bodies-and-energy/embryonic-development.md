# How an Embryo Builds a Body 🥚

You began as one cell. Somehow its descendants made eyes, bones, a heart and fingers—usually in the right places, with no tiny foreman checking a drawing. How does a growing collection of cells find its shape?

A **cell** is a tiny living unit enclosed by a membrane. An **embryo** is an organism in an early stage of development, while its basic body structures are forming. In humans, that development starts when a sperm joins an egg. Cells then divide, move, exchange chemical signals and take on different jobs.

The remarkable part is how reliably this produces organised tissues from one starting cell. The complications are equally informative: development depends on timing, physical forces and material already inside the egg, as well as **DNA**—deoxyribonucleic acid, the molecule carrying inherited information. Sections of DNA called **genes** help cells make the molecules they need.

## 🧬 What egg and sperm contribute

Human eggs and sperm normally contain one set of 23 **chromosomes**, packages of DNA in the cell's **nucleus**, its main genetic-information compartment. Their production involves **meiosis**, which reduces chromosome number and reshuffles inherited information by exchanging chromosome sections and distributing the resulting chromosomes into different eggs or sperm.

Fertilisation brings the two sets into one cell, the **zygote**. The egg also supplies nearly all the **cytoplasm**, the material surrounding the nucleus, along with internal compartments called **organelles** and stored molecules supporting early development. Nuclear DNA from two parents is therefore only part of the starting condition.

Sperm and egg production distribute resources differently. Sperm development produces small specialised cells; egg development keeps most cytoplasm in one large cell. Small cells called **polar bodies** receive the other chromosome sets. Developing human eggs, or **oocytes**, can pause partway through meiosis for many years.

Errors in chromosome separation can produce missing or additional chromosomes. Errors after fertilisation can produce **mosaicism**, where groups descended from different cells carry different genetic information. These describe mechanisms, not a complete prediction of an individual's development.

For the chromosome machinery itself, see [DNA and chromosomes](../genetics-and-evolution/dna-and-chromosomes.md) and [inheritance](../genetics-and-evolution/inheritance.md).

## 🫧 More cells, without immediately getting bigger

Early **cleavage divisions** partition the zygote into smaller cells without comparable growth in overall size. The cells compact into a **morula**, and then a fluid-filled cavity develops within a **blastocyst**.

The blastocyst has an outer layer of cells and an inner cell mass. The outer layer contributes to supporting structures, including the baby's part of the **placenta**, the organ through which materials are exchanged with the mother. The inner mass gives rise to the embryo and other supporting tissues. Position, cell shape and which genes are active are already beginning to differ.

In typical human development, blastocyst formation occurs during the first week after fertilisation, followed by the beginning of **implantation**, when it attaches to and becomes embedded in the lining of the uterus, or womb. The stages are biological processes with variation, not appointments at which every embryo checks its watch.

Stored maternal molecules support the earliest stages, while using the embryo's own genetic information becomes increasingly important. Development depends on this transition as well as on cell number.

## 🧭 How do the inside and outside take shape?

During **gastrulation**, cells move and reorganise into three starting layers of tissue called **germ layers**. Here, “germ” means an origin or beginning; it has nothing to do with an infection. In humans this begins around the third week after fertilisation.

| Germ layer | Some tissues it gives rise to |
| --- | --- |
| Ectoderm | Outer skin layer, brain, spinal cord and other nervous-system tissues |
| Mesoderm | Muscle, connective tissue, blood, heart and much of the urinary and reproductive systems |
| Endoderm | Inner lining of much of the gut and breathing passages, and tissues in associated organs |

These are starting families of cells, not exclusive labels for entire organs. A lung includes tissues derived from more than one layer.

The head-to-tail, back-to-front and left-to-right directions emerge through interacting signals and cell movements. **Morphogens** are signals whose concentration or exposure history can influence cell fate. Signals change which genes a cell uses, and the response also depends on signals it has received before. A cell's position and history help determine what it becomes.

Mechanical effects matter too. Cells adhere, pull, fold and change shape. A sheet becoming a tube is simultaneously a genetic, biochemical and physical event.

## 🏗️ Building organs uses addition and subtraction

Organs form as cells multiply, move, specialise and sometimes undergo controlled death. **Differentiation** is the process by which a cell becomes specialised for a particular role. More cells alone would produce a larger mass, not a functional heart or a hand with separated digits.

Developmental signals are reused in different settings. Their effect depends on location, timing and the cells receiving them. This helps explain how a finite set of signalling pathways can contribute to many structures without each organ requiring completely unrelated molecular machinery.

The standard human **embryonic period** extends through the end of eight weeks after fertilisation. The **fetal period** follows, with substantial growth and continued differentiation. Clinical gestational age is usually counted from the last menstrual period, approximately two weeks earlier than fertilisation-based age. The two clocks should not be mixed.

## 🌿 The placenta is an active organ

The placenta supports gas exchange, nutrient transfer, waste removal and hormonal signalling. Maternal blood and fetal blood normally remain in separate circulatory compartments while substances cross the intervening tissues.

Oxygen crosses by **diffusion**, net movement from where it is more available to where it is less available. Glucose, a sugar, and amino acids, the building units of proteins, use transport proteins. Fetal **haemoglobin**, the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells, binds oxygen in ways that help transfer it to the fetus. The umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood towards the fetus, while the arteries return blood towards the placenta. Vessel names describe direction relative to the heart, not oxygen content.

The immune relationship is also active. Local regulation permits contact between genetically different maternal and fetal tissues while maintaining defence. It is more accurate than saying pregnancy switches immunity off. The broader mechanisms of tolerance appear in [immunity](immunity.md).

## 👯 Twins and developmental flexibility

**Dizygotic**, or fraternal, twins develop from two fertilised eggs. **Monozygotic**, often called identical, twins originate from one fertilised egg. Later changes to DNA, differences in gene activity and developmental conditions can still make them different. “Identical” is doing more work in that name than biology can entirely support.

Monozygotic twinning shows that early development has a capacity for reorganisation. The familiar relationship between developmental timing and shared membranes is a useful descriptive model, but it is not a complete explanation of the cellular causes of twinning.

More generally, researchers can explain many local developmental mechanisms without yet predicting an entire human body's formation from first principles. Work in other animals, human embryonic material and models grown from **stem cells**, cells capable of producing specialised descendants, illuminates different parts of the process; a model reproducing one feature does not automatically reproduce the whole embryo.

[Gene regulation](../genetics-and-evolution/gene-expression-and-regulation.md) explains how cell identities persist, and [animal body plans](animal-body-plans.md) shows how related developmental tools have produced very different anatomies.
