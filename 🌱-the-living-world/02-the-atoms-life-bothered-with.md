# The Atoms Life Bothered With ⚛️

Life is a snob.

The universe offers ninety-odd elements and a buffet of solvents, temperatures, and bad ideas. Earth life looked at the menu, took six atoms as the main course, water as the table, and a handful of metals as jewellery, then acted as if the rest of the periodic table was garnish.

That snobbery is the most important chemistry lesson in biology. If you want to know why a calorie is a calorie of *this* rather than *that*, why a membrane exists, why a forest is made of carbon lattices and not quartz, you start here. Not with organs. Not with DNA's publicity campaign. With the fact that life is a process running on a very particular kit.

I am still the AI narrator. Mick is still the human who has to eat. Neither of us is made of silicon, which will matter in about four headings.

## CHNOPS: The Band 🎸

Six letters. They show up in every serious origin-of-life lecture like a boy band that refused to break up.

**Carbon, Hydrogen, Nitrogen, Oxygen, Phosphorus, Sulfur.**

By mass, a human is mostly oxygen, because a human is mostly water and water is mostly oxygen. By the story, we are carbon. Both are true, in the way that a band is both the singer and the PA system.

A quick tour, with the jobs they actually do:

**Carbon** is the scaffold. Four bonds, a talent for chains and rings, the ability to make single, double, and triple bonds without immediately falling over. It can be methane, sugar, fat, DNA, a beetle, a mood. Carbon is the guitar: everything else is arrangement.

**Hydrogen** is the smallest atom and the most sociable. It is half of water, most of the protons in your acid-base life, and the thing being shuttled around in the electron-transport chain like a crowd surfer at a gig. When later chapters say "proton gradient," they mean hydrogen ions being used as a battery. Life turned the lightest element into infrastructure.

**Oxygen** is the greedy one. It wants electrons. That is why it is a brilliant solvent partner in water, a brilliant waste product in photosynthesis, and a brilliant poison if you cannot control it. Aerobic life made a deal with a pyromaniac: you can have our leftovers if you power the house. The house sometimes burns. We call that ageing, inflammation, and a long list of other words for rust.

**Nitrogen** is the awkward cousin of air. The atmosphere is ~78% nitrogen gas, N₂, which is a triple bond so smug it barely reacts. Life needs nitrogen in amino acids and bases — the letters of proteins and DNA — and cannot, mostly, prise it out of the sky without help. That help is a handful of microbes with a metal-rich enzyme called nitrogenase, plus lightning, plus the Haber-Bosch process, which is how industrial civilisation also learned to be a microbe. More on that when we get to soil. For now: your proteins are a nitrogen story wearing carbon clothes.

**Phosphorus** is the energy clerk and the information clerk. ATP's useful trick is a string of phosphate groups that really would rather not be a string. DNA's backbone is a phosphate-sugar rail. Membranes get some of their personality from phospholipid heads. Phosphorus is not rare in the way gold is rare. It is rare in the way a good drummer is rare: when it is missing, the whole band notices. Oceanographers call it a limiting nutrient without irony.

**Sulfur** is the unglamorous specialist. Disulfide bridges help proteins hold a shape. Some of the oldest metabolisms we can still find — including flavours that may be close to LUCA's hydrogen-and-CO₂ diet from [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md) — run on sulfur redox chemistry. Hydrothermal vents smell like this paragraph.

That is the kit. Not because these are the most common atoms in the crust — silicon and aluminium would like a word — but because they make **versatile, water-friendly, information-capable chemistry**. Life did not pick the most abundant rock. It picked the best toys.

The mismatch is the tell. Earth's crust is a silicon-oxygen world with aluminium, iron, and calcium in supporting roles. A human body, by mass, is an oxygen-carbon-hydrogen story with nitrogen, calcium, and phosphorus as the next names on the bill. If life were just "whatever is lying around," we would be glass. We are not glass. We are a thin, wet, carbon film on a silicate planet, which is already a hint that biochemistry is choosy rather than lazy.

## Water: The Stage, Not The Prop 💧

People call water the universal solvent. It is not. It is the solvent life actually used, which is a more interesting claim.

Water is a bent molecule with a lopsided charge: oxygen hogs electrons, hydrogens look slightly positive, and the result is a tiny magnet that can hydrogen-bond with its neighbours. From that one personality trait you get a ridiculous list of useful accidents.

- **It stays liquid** over a temperature range that is friendly to complex chemistry. Not a huge range, cosmically. Huge enough.
- **It is polar**, so salts dissolve, proteins can have insides and outsides, and membranes can exist as a truce between greasy tails and wet heads.
- **Ice floats.** Hydrogen bonds lock water into an open lattice as it freezes, so the solid is less dense than the liquid. Lakes freeze from the top. Fish get a winter. If ice sank, planetary oceans would have a much worse career.
- **It soaks up heat** without fuss. High specific heat, high latent heat. Coasts are milder than deserts for a reason that is sitting in your glass.
- **It takes part in the reactions**, not just the washing-up. Hydrolysis, condensation, photosynthesis's water-splitting tantrum. Water is a reagent wearing a solvent costume.

One more gift, hiding in the same polarity: the **hydrophobic effect**. Oils do not mix with water because water would rather hydrogen-bond with itself than babysit a greasy molecule. Put enough amphiphiles — molecules with a wet head and a greasy tail — into that argument, and they spontaneously form bilayers, droplets, micelles. Membranes are not a miracle invention that life had to think of. They are what certain carbon compounds *do* in water, the way a football crowd forms a terrace. Later chapters on fats and cells are this paragraph with more plot.

Is water required for life anywhere, ever? That is a later, ruder question. For Earth life, water is not a preference. It is the medium the whole trick is written in. Remove it and you do not get dry life. You get jerky, spores, and a pause. Tardigrades can stop the film. They do not switch to a different film.

## Carbon's Personality 🧱

Why carbon, really?

Four valence electrons. Four bonds. A size that lets it form stable chains without the bonds being too eager or too lazy. It bonds happily to itself, to hydrogen, to oxygen, to nitrogen. It can be tetrahedral (methane, diamond's local habit), planar (graphite, benzene), or involved in rings that become steroids, sugars, bases.

The useful picture is **modularity**. Carbon chemistry is Lego that still looks like Lego after you have made a cathedral. Silicon, as we are about to see, is Lego that wants to become a brick wall and stay there.

Carbon also has a gas-phase relationship with oxygen that life exploited shamelessly. CO₂ is a small, soluble, recyclable waste and feedstock. You can breathe it out. A tree can pull it in. The carbon cycle is a planetary lung. Silicon's equivalent relationship with oxygen is... rock.

## Silicon's Audition, Which Does Not Go Well 🪨

Science fiction loves silicon life. It is sitting right under carbon in the periodic table. Four bonds. Common in the crust. Why not a planet of glass people?

Because chemistry is not a seating chart.

[Petkowski, Bains, and Seager (2020)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7345352/) put it without mercy: silicon-based life in water is almost certainly a non-starter, and exclusive Si–Si scaffolding in water is close to impossible. Their line worth stealing is that **carbon chemistry is the chemistry of life, silicon chemistry is the chemistry of rocks**.

The problems stack.

**Silicon-silicon bonds are weaker and wetter.** Silanes, the silicon cousins of hydrocarbons, hydrolyze. They do not hang around in water composing sonatas. They fall apart into oxygen-loving sludge.

**Silicon loves oxygen too much.** The Si–O bond is a commitment. Once silicon meets oxygen, you often get silica: quartz, sand, glass, the floor. Carbon-oxygen can be a gas you exhale. Silicon-oxygen is a solid you would have to... what? Cough out sand? Poop glass? The metabolism joke writes itself, and then becomes an engineering problem.

**The interesting silicon chemistry is not stable in the solvent we actually have.** Add silicon to the theoretical chemical space in water and you do not get a bonanza of new life-like molecules. You get a lot of things that immediately regret existing. In concentrated sulfuric acid the picture is a bit kinder to silicon as a *side* atom. That is a cameo, not a starring role.

Could there be silicon life in some dry, cold, reducing, oxygen-poor hellscape? People can sketch it. The sketches need a solvent that is not water, a shortage of oxygen, and a willingness to live somewhere that makes Titan look like a beach holiday. It is not impossible in the way "a triangle with four sides" is impossible. It is implausible in the way "a football team that only plays in a vacuum" is implausible. You can write the rules. The league will not call.

Earth life *does* use silicon, in a limited, geological way: diatoms build glass houses, some plants stash silica in their tissues, sponges make spicules. That is silicon as building material, not as the backbone of the information system. A diatom is still a carbon autobiography with a glass annex.

## The Jewellery: Metals Life Refused To Live Without 💍

CHNOPS builds the body. A few metals make the body *fast*.

Enzymes are proteins that make reactions happen on a useful timescale. Many of them are not just protein. They hold a metal ion like a jeweller holding a stone: in the right setting, the stone does the interesting work.

- **Iron** shuttles electrons. Haemoglobin is the celebrity. The electron-transport chain is the actual career. Iron is why blood is red and why a mitochondrion is a redox instrument.
- **Magnesium** sits in chlorophyll's head, and it also makes ATP less of a tantrum. Lots of phosphate chemistry is quietly magnesium chemistry.
- **Zinc** stabilises folds, including the "zinc finger" motifs that let some proteins grab DNA. Structural jewellery.
- **Copper** turns up in the last step of breathing, in cytochrome c oxidase, stealing the last of those electrons for oxygen. Also in some of the enzymes that deal with oxygen's worse moods.
- **Molybdenum** (and sometimes vanadium) in nitrogenase: the rare trick that turns smug N₂ into something a cell can use. No molybdenum-class chemistry, no cheap protein nitrogen, no biosphere as we know it.
- **Cobalt** in vitamin B12. A whole metabolic neighbourhood depends on a metal you barely think about unless a blood test gets dramatic.

These are not optional sprinkles. They are why a "trace" deficiency can wreck a life. A guitar still looks like a guitar with no strings. It is not, in any useful sense, a guitar.

The origin-of-life implication is delicious: whatever first chemistry got going, it probably had access to water, carbon, and a rock that leaked metals. Vents are popular in part because they are a metal-rich buffet. That is a theory, not a postcard from the Archaean. It is a theory with a motive.

## Earth's Goldilocks Kit 🌍

Why here?

Not because Earth is the only wet rock. Because Earth combined, for a long time:

- **Liquid water** at the surface, and in the crust
- **Carbon** in circulation, not all locked in graphite forever
- **An energy gradient** — the Sun, and chemical imbalances in rocks and vents
- **A relatively mild redox neighbourhood**, which got a lot less mild when photosynthesis started dumping oxygen, which was both apocalypse and promotion
- **A crust that weathers**, feeding phosphorus and metals into the water

The Moon-forming impact, around 4.5 billion years ago, is a plausible "reset" for the surface. LUCA, if Moody et al. are in the right postcode, shows up a few hundred million years later already complex. That is either "life is easy once the kit is on the bench" or "we are missing a lot of failed kit." Both can be true.

None of this means Earth is the only possible stage. It means Earth is the stage whose props we can inventory. When we talk about other worlds, we are asking which of these props can be swapped.

## Other Solvents, Briefly, Before The Telescope Chapters 🧪

If water is a brilliant accident, what else could play the stage?

**Ammonia** can hydrogen-bond. It is liquid colder than water, at ordinary pressure. Reactions would be slower, which is not automatically death — cold chemistry is just chemistry with more patience — but it is a different sport. Ammonia is also less polar, and it is not sitting around in oceans on any world we know.

**Methane and ethane**, the lakes of Titan, are nonpolar. They would dissolve the opposite of what water dissolves. Membranes, if any, would have to invert their personalities: greasy on the outside, or some other trick. NASA has flown past this chemistry set. We have not found life. We have found a reason to keep the "weird life" folder open and labelled *theory*.

**Concentrated sulfuric acid**, in Venusian clouds, is a vicious solvent with its own possible organic chemistry. Silicon as a *helper* atom looks less stupid there than in water. The phosphine saga later in this series is a cautionary tale about how fast a maybe-biosignature can become a maybe-plumbing-problem.

**Supercritical carbon dioxide** sits on the exotic list. Interesting to chemists. Not a habitat we have walked.

The point of the tease is not "aliens swim in petrol." It is this: **solvent chooses biochemistry**. Water chose CHNOPS, membranes with greasy insides, DNA that hates drying out. A different solvent would choose different snobberies. If we only look for Earth-water's favourite molecules, we will find Earth-water's cousins, or nothing, and call that a survey.

## Twenty Amino Acids, Plus Two Troublemakers, Plus A Frozen Accident 🧊

Proteins are made of amino acids. Textbooks say twenty. The universe did not have to pick twenty. There are hundreds of amino acids in meteorites and in chemists' cupboards. Life, as a coder, settled on a standard set of twenty for the genetic code, then smuggled in two more as special cases:

- **Selenocysteine**, the 21st, inserted at what is usually a stop codon (UGA) with extra RNA choreography. Selenium sitting where sulfur would, in a handful of enzymes that really wanted that extra reactivity. Humans use it. It is not a trivia item. It is a reminder that the code can grow a barnacle.
- **Pyrrolysine**, the 22nd, mostly a methanogen party trick, inserted at UAG. Rare. Real.

Why these twenty as the core kit?

Francis Crick, in 1968, called the genetic code a **frozen accident**. Once many proteins depend on a mapping, you cannot change the mapping without scrambling the whole library at once. The code froze because thawing it would be lethal. That is still a strong idea. It is not the whole idea.

The code is also, suspiciously, good at minimising damage. Similar amino acids tend to have similar codons. A one-letter typo often swaps something for a cousin, not for a total stranger. That looks like selection, or at least like a code that survived because it was robust, the way a guitar tuning that keeps neighbouring notes related is easier to play drunk.

There are other theories, which will get more air when we reach DNA:

- **Stereochemical**: some amino acids just like some bases, chemically
- **Coevolution**: the code grew as metabolism invented new amino acids
- **Error minimisation**: robustness was the point

We do not know which mix is true. We do know the mapping is older than LUCA. The ancestor in chapter 01 was already using this library. The choice of letters is part of the oldest technology on Earth.

Could life elsewhere use a different twenty, or forty, or a code that is not triplet RNA? Yes, in the sense that nothing in physics forbids it. No, in the sense that we have a sample size of one, and one is the number at which every pattern looks like destiny.

Then there is the handedness problem, which textbooks mention and then flee.

Amino acids come in left- and right-handed forms, like gloves. Chemistry in a beaker makes both. Earth proteins almost exclusively use the **left-handed (L)** set. Sugars in DNA and RNA go the other way: **D**. This is homochirality, and it is one of the deepest "why *this*?" facts we have. A mixed-handed protein is a bad protein. Once a lineage picks a hand, the other hand is poison to its machinery, so the choice freezes. Why left, originally? Meteorites carry a slight excess of some L-amino acids, which is a clue, not a verdict. Polarised light, surface chemistry, a chance fluctuation that won: all still in play. If we ever find life with the other handshake, we will know it is not our cousin. If we find the same handshake, we will not know whether that is destiny or contamination, including the contamination of our own imaginations.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Why these six atoms as the backbone, exactly?** CHNOPS is descriptive. It is not a proof that no other kit works.
- **Why these twenty amino acids?** Frozen accident, optimisation, chemistry, or a pile of all three. The code has barnacles (21, 22) and a few variant dialects in mitochondria and microbes, so it is frozen-ish, not frozen-dead.
- **Why this handshake?** Homochirality is a fact looking for a mechanism. A slight meteoritic bias is interesting. A full explanation is not in yet.
- **Is water necessary?** For Earth life, operationally yes. For life as a cosmic category, unknown. Titan remains the awkward test.
- **Can silicon ever star, or only cameo?** Present theory says cameo in water, maybe a supporting role in nastier solvents. No known examples.
- **Which metals are truly required versus locally convenient?** Iron is everywhere in our biochemistry because Earth is an iron-rich world with a history of redox drama. A different planet might jewellery differently. We should not demand haemoglobin from a world that never had our rust.
