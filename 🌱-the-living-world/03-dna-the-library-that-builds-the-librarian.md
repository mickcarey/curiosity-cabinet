# DNA: The Library That Builds The Librarian 📚

A library is usually furniture. DNA is a library that also builds the librarian, the shelves, the building, and the person who keeps forgetting where they left their card.

That is the trick school posters flatten into a logo: a pastel helix, a few letters, a caption that says "the blueprint." Blueprints do not hire contractors. DNA does. It is a molecule with a sequence, and the sequence is instructions for making the machines that copy, read, repair, and — when the mood strikes — rewrite the sequence. Information that pays rent. Schrödinger's aperiodic crystal from [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md), now with a chemical name and a packing problem.

I am the AI narrator. Mick asked for DNA as a molecule, not as merch. Fair. You cannot understand a calorie, a forest, or a karyotype if you think the helix is a brand.

The last chapter gave us the kit: CHNOPS, water, a handful of metals. This one is what carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, and phosphorus *do* when they decide to remember.

## The Molecule, Not The Logo 🧬

DNA is a polymer. Two long strands, each a chain of nucleotides. A nucleotide is a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate, and a base. Four bases do the talking:

- **A** adenine
- **T** thymine
- **G** guanine
- **C** cytosine

They pair. **A with T**, two hydrogen bonds. **G with C**, three. That extra hug is why GC-rich stretches are a bit harder to unzip, the way a barre chord is harder than an open one. Chargaff had already noticed, in the late 1940s, that A and T show up in equal amounts, G and C likewise. The pairing is why. It is also why one strand is a template for the other. Complementary, not identical. A photocopy that is also a negative.

The strands run **antiparallel**. One goes 5′ to 3′, the other 3′ to 5′. Those numbers are carbon atoms on the sugar: the 5′ carbon wears the phosphate that starts the chain, the 3′ carbon is where the next nucleotide gets bolted on. DNA polymerases only add to a 3′ end. They will not work backwards. This is not a personality quirk. It is a chemical constraint that will, in a few headings, force the lagging strand to be copied in fits, like a drummer who can only play fills from left to right and has to restart every bar.

The famous twist is a **right-handed double helix**, bases stacked inside like rungs, phosphates on the outside where water can see them. Rosalind Franklin's data put the phosphates there; Watson and Crick's model made the pairing and the antiparallel twist click. There is a wide **major groove** and a skinny **minor groove**. Proteins that need to read a sequence without unzipping the whole thing often grab the major groove, the way you can tell a chord from the shape of a guitarist's hand without hearing the note.

RNA is the same idea in a slightly different fabric: ribose instead of deoxyribose, **U** (uracil) instead of T. DNA is the archival copy because the missing oxygen makes it a bit less chemically fidgety. RNA is the working copy because it can fold, catalyse, and get degraded when the gig is over. School treats this as two molecules. It is one chemistry with a day job and a night job.

The two strands are not redundancy for its own sake. They are the backup *and* the template *and* the reason a mutation on one side can still be spotted as a mismatch. Information that can check itself. That is a very particular kind of memory.

That is the molecule. A four-letter alphabet, a two-sided tape, a twist that is both archive and instruction. It is not "life." It is the memory life uses. A USB stick on a bench is not a computer. A virion is a USB stick. You, currently, are the computer that keeps copying the stick.

## Two Metres In A Tennis Ball 🧳

Unzip a typical human cell's nuclear DNA, end to end, and you get about **two metres** of molecule. The nucleus is five to ten micrometres across. That is not a metaphor. Each base pair is about 0.34 nanometres long. A diploid human genome is on the order of six billion base pairs. Multiply. You get a couple of metres of thread that has to live in a room the size of a dust mote, stay readable, and copy itself without turning into a bird's nest.

Nature's own analogy is even ruder: it is like stuffing forty kilometres of fine thread into a tennis ball and still being able to find page 847.

Bacteria have the same problem at a smaller scale. *E. coli* packs about a millimetre of DNA into a cell a few micrometres long. Humans just have more thread and a nuclear envelope to lose it in.

The packing is not storage in the attic sense. Genes have to be *on* in the right cell at the right time. A suitcase that cannot be opened is a brick. Chromatin is a suitcase designed to be rummaged.

## Beads On A String, Then A Suitcase 🧵

The first fold is the **nucleosome**. DNA wraps about **1.65 to 1.7 left-handed turns** — roughly **147 base pairs** — around a spool of eight histone proteins: two each of H2A, H2B, H3, and H4. Linker DNA between spools. Histone H1 sits on some of those linkers like a clip. Under an electron microscope the thing looks like beads on a string, which is the rare case of a textbook cartoon being basically honest.

Histones are positively charged (lots of lysine and arginine) because DNA's phosphate backbone is negatively charged. Jewellery from [chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md), except the jewellery is the spool.

Nucleosomes plus DNA plus a crowd of other proteins is **chromatin**. Open, readable chromatin is often called **euchromatin**. Tight, quieter chromatin is **heterochromatin**. Those are working descriptions, not two drawers. The same stretch can be more or less available depending on the cell, the hour, and which chemical tags are hanging off the histone tails. Chapter 04 will treat those tags as the volume knobs. For now: packing is also regulation. You cannot shout a gene that is sat on.

Textbooks then coil the beads into a neat 30-nanometre fibre, then loops, then a chromosome. Living nuclei are ruder. The rigid 30-nm fibre is hard to find in real cells; chromatin looks more like a crowded, liquid-ish polymer, nucleosomes clustered, H1 acting as a loose glue rather than a drill sergeant. The cartoon is a map. The city is messier. Both are useful if you know which one you are holding.

A **chromosome** is one very long DNA molecule, plus its proteins, plus its personality. Chromosome 1 is a single thread of about 249 million base pairs. It is not a bundle of shorter genes tied with string. It is one molecule. That still surprises people who think of genes as beads you could pour into a jar.

In working cells (interphase) a chromosome is a messy **territory** in the nucleus, not a sausage. Each chromosome tends to keep a neighbourhood rather than dissolving into a communal soup, which is how two metres of thread avoids becoming a single knot of despair. The sausage is the **condensed** form, visible at mitosis, the version you see on a karyotype poster because that is when it will sit still for a photograph. Condensed is for moving house. Decondensed is for living in the house.

**Topoisomerases** deserve a nod before we leave the suitcase. Twisting two metres of helix to copy it or unpack it will tangle it. These enzymes nick the DNA, let the supercoils slip, and reseal it. Without them, replication forks stall and the library becomes macramé. They are the roadies who detangle the cables so the band can actually play.

## The Human Karyotype: Forty-Six Books 📖

Humans, typically, keep nuclear DNA in **46 chromosomes**: **22 pairs of autosomes** plus a pair of **sex chromosomes**. That is 23 pairs. Each pair is **homologous**: same genes in the same order, more or less, one copy from each parent. Homologues are not identical. They are the same book in two editions, with different typos, different marginalia, sometimes different plot twists in the footnotes.

A **karyotype** is the family photo: chromosomes stained, paired, lined up roughly from largest (chromosome 1) to smallest. The dark and light **G-bands** are not genes; they are a staining habit that happens to be reproducible, a subway map of AT-rich and GC-rich neighbourhoods. Addresses like 7q31.2 live on that map. The numbering is a historical sketch, not a tape measure. **Chromosome 21 is actually smaller than 22.** Nobody wanted to reprint the posters. Biology is full of this: the map got painted before the survey finished, and the paint stuck.

The sex chromosomes in the usual textbook pairing are **XX** and **XY**. X is a real chromosome with a lot of work to do. Y is smaller and, in humans, mostly in the sex-determination and sperm-related business. XX cells typically silence one X (**X-inactivation**), so the double dose of X does not drown the cell; the silenced X can show up as a Barr body, a little heterochromatin pebble at the nuclear edge. This is a sketch of a common pattern, not a census of every body. Chromosome number and sex-chromosome combinations vary; some of those variations are survivable, some are not. That is biology as it is, not a morality play, and it is not medical advice. The GP is not a chapter. Chapter 10 will pick up what happens when the dance of chromosomes in a gamete goes wrong.

Homologous pairs matter because of **diploidy**. You have two copies of most genes. Recessive trouble can hide. Dominant trouble cannot. Sexual reproduction will shuffle which copy goes into which gamete. Mitosis copies the whole set. Meiosis deals half. Next chapter.

School mixes up two kinds of "pair," so here they are on separate stools:

- **Homologous chromosomes**: the maternal and paternal editions of chromosome 7, say. Same genes, different history. They only pair up to swap and separate in meiosis.
- **Sister chromatids**: the two identical copies of *one* chromosome, made during replication, glued at the centromere until mitosis (or meiosis II) pulls them apart.

A condensed metaphase chromosome that looks like an X is one chromosome, two sisters, one centromere. It is not a homologous pair hugging. The X-shape is a duplicate waiting to be torn in half. The homologous pair is two such X-shapes that find each other only when gametes are being made.

Other species did not sign our 46. A fruit fly has eight. A dog has 78. An adder's-tongue fern has been clocked with hundreds. Chromosome *number* is not a sophistication score. It is a filing choice. What matters is that the homologues can find each other when it is time to deal a haploid hand.

One more bookkeeping fact, because school is sloppy with it: the 46 are **nuclear** chromosomes. They live in the nucleus. They are not the whole genome.

## Centromeres, Telomeres, And The Arms 🦾

Look at a condensed chromosome. It has a pinch. That pinch is the **centromere**: a stretch of (mostly repetitive) DNA where the **kinetochore** assembles, which is where the spindle fibres grab when the cell divides. Without a centromere, a chromosome is luggage with no handle. It will not get on the train.

The short arm is **p** (from *petit*). The long arm is **q** (the next letter, or *queue* if you like the French gossip better). Geneticists give addresses like 7q31.2 the way a city gives postcodes. The centromere's position decides the silhouette:

- **Metacentric**: pinch in the middle, arms fairly even
- **Submetacentric**: pinch off-centre
- **Acrocentric**: pinch near one end, a stubby p arm. In humans, 13, 14, 15, 21, and 22. Those short arms are busy with ribosomal RNA genes, which is why they hang around the nucleolus like session musicians who all play bass.

The ends are **telomeres**. In humans they are tandem repeats of **TTAGGG**, thousands of copies, bound by a protein complex (shelterin) that says to the DNA-repair crew: this is a *foot*, not a *break*. Linear chromosomes have an **end-replication problem**. DNA polymerase needs a primer and only works 5′ to 3′, so the lagging strand cannot finish the last bit. Each copy job nips a little off the end. Telomeres are the sacrificial tape at the end of the cassette. **Telomerase** — a reverse transcriptase with its own RNA template — can add the repeats back. Germline cells and some stem cells keep it on. Most of your somatic cells let the tape shorten. Cancer cells often steal the remote. That is a later, darker chapter. Here it is just architecture: chromosomes need caps, and the caps are a sequence.

Condensed versus working form, again, because it is the bit posters hide. A metaphase chromosome is a moving crate. An interphase chromosome is a workspace with territories. Genes in the crate are mostly closed for business. Genes in the workspace can be opened. When someone says "look at the chromosomes," ask: packed for shipping, or unpacked for reading?

## Copying The Library 🖨️

Replication is **semiconservative**. Each new double helix keeps one old strand and builds one new one. Meselson and Stahl showed this in 1958 with a density-label trick that still looks like a magic act. The library is not photocopied and then shredded. Each original page becomes the template for its partner.

It starts at **origins of replication**, many of them in a eukaryotic genome, so two metres does not have to unzip from one end like a single stubborn zip. Helicase unzips. Single-strand binding proteins keep the unzipped bits from clinging back together. Primase lays a short RNA primer, because DNA polymerase cannot start from nothing — it can only extend. DNA polymerase then adds nucleotides to the 3′ end, A opposite T, G opposite C. Ligase later seals the nicks. Topoisomerases, already on the payroll, keep the fork from turning the rest of the chromosome into a pretzel.

Because the strands are antiparallel and the fork only goes one way, one strand (the **leading** strand) can be copied in a smooth 5′ to 3′ take. The other (the **lagging** strand) is copied in short bursts, **Okazaki fragments**, each starting with a primer, later stitched. It is the difference between a guitarist who can play through a song and one who has to restart every bar because the instrument only works in one direction. Same song. Different logistics. The RNA primers get ripped out and replaced with DNA. Nothing in the finished helix should still be RNA. The archive prefers its own fabric.

**Proofreading** is the underrated hero. Replicative polymerases have a 3′ to 5′ exonuclease: if the last base does not fit, they bite it off and try again. The enzyme can un-hit a missed beat. Rough textbook numbers, from Alberts and friends: polymerisation alone misincorporates about 1 in 10⁵ bases; proofreading knocks that down about a hundredfold; **mismatch repair** after the fact knocks it down again, using the fact that the new strand is briefly distinguishable from the old one. End result, on a good day, on the order of **one error per billion bases**. Your six billion base pairs still collect a handful of typos every division. Most do nothing. Some do cancer. Some, in a germline, do evolution.

That last sentence is the whole of chapter 04's mutation heading, arriving early like a drummer who cannot wait for the bridge.

Fidelity is not perfection. Perfection would freeze the library. Life needs a library that is mostly right and slightly wrong. Schrödinger wanted an aperiodic crystal that could carry a story. The story has typos on purpose, or at least on sufferance.

## The Other Genome In The Basement 🔋

Mitochondria have their own DNA.

Human **mitochondrial DNA** is a small **circular** molecule, **16,569 base pairs**, encoding **37 genes**: 13 proteins for the respiratory chain, 22 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs. It is not a nuclear chromosome. It does not have histones in the nuclear sense, does not line up on a spindle with the 46, and does not get a number in the karyotype. It is a leftover bacterial genome, trimmed to the bone, living in the power stations. Chapter 09 will do endosymbiosis properly. Here the point is bookkeeping: when people say "the human genome," they often mean the nucleus. The cell did not get that memo.

You have hundreds to thousands of copies of mtDNA per cell, because you have hundreds to thousands of mitochondria, each with a few copies. Inheritance is **typically maternal**: egg contributes the cytoplasm, sperm contributes almost no intact mitochondrial genome. A 2023 *Nature Genetics* paper argued that human sperm even strip out mtDNA during development, which would make the maternal rule a manufacturing decision, not just a numbers game. Rare claims of paternal leakage exist and are fought over. Treat "your mitochondria come from your mother" as an excellent rule with a small, disputed asterisk, not as scripture.

mtDNA mutates faster than nuclear DNA, has thinner repair, and has its own genetic-code dialect (a frozen accident with local amendments, as [chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) warned). That is why a maternal lineage can be traced when nuclear DNA is a blender, and why some energy disorders run down the maternal line. Not medical advice. A map of where the other library lives.

Most of the proteins that mitochondria need are encoded in the nucleus and imported. The 13 proteins left on the circle are stubbornly local, mostly for the respiratory chain. The basement genome is a remnant, not a second set of chromosomes that forgot to go upstairs. When a karyotype technician counts to 46, they are not ignoring mitochondria out of spite. They are counting the nuclear set. Different room, different rules.

## Photo 51 And The Room Franklin Was Already In 📷

The helix did not arrive as a lone eureka in Cambridge.

In May 1952, Rosalind Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling, at King's College London, took an X-ray fibre diffraction image of wet B-form DNA that the world now calls **Photo 51**. It is an X, which is what a helix looks like when you scatter X-rays off it, if you know how to read the scatter. Franklin already knew DNA came in A (drier, crisper) and B (wetter, more biological) forms. She had the phosphates on the outside. She was measuring.

In January 1953, Maurice Wilkins showed Photo 51 to James Watson, without Franklin's knowledge. Around the same time, Franklin's quantitative data reached Cambridge via an MRC report. Watson and Crick built the model: two antiparallel strands, base pairing, the right dimensions. In April 1953, *Nature* ran three papers back to back: Watson and Crick's model, Wilkins's group, Franklin and Gosling. Watson and Crick's line of credit was a sentence about being "stimulated by" unpublished results from Wilkins and Franklin. In a 1954 follow-up they admitted the structure would have been "most unlikely, if not impossible" without her data.

The popular story is theft and a woman who did not understand her own photograph. The cabinet already keeps a version of that story in [Forgotten Women Who Changed Everything](../✈️-airplane-reading/forgotten-women-who-changed-everything.md), and the anger in it is earned: Watson's memoir was unkind, the Nobel in 1962 went to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins, and Franklin had died in 1958, aged 37. Nobels are not awarded posthumously. That part is institutional, not subtle.

The 2023 rereading by Matthew Cobb and Nathaniel Comfort, in *Nature*, is worth holding next to the legend. They argue Franklin was not a bystander who missed the helix in her own data. She was an equal contributor: the experimental half of the question, close to a helical, two-chain, bases-in, phosphates-out picture of her own. Photo 51 was not a magic decoder ring Watson uniquely possessed the eyes for. It was one image in a pile of measurements. Franklin had even distinguished A-form from B-form DNA, which is the sort of unglamorous experimental win that actually lets a model be right. The injustice is real. The cartoon of the clueless heroine is also a way of not letting her be a scientist.

Either way, she belongs in the room. The molecule on the page is partly her work. Say the names. If you only remember Watson and Crick, you remember the model-builders and not the diffraction. Both were required. The helix is a collaboration that the Nobel paperwork refused to spell.

## "Junk DNA" Was A Rude Name For An Unread Wing 🗑️

Protein-coding sequence is about **one to two percent** of the human genome. GENCODE currently annotates on the order of **19,000 to 20,000** protein-coding genes — a number that has been drifting down from the old "100,000 genes" guess and now sits, stubbornly, near twenty thousand. The rest is not empty. It is also not automatically a cathedral.

Roughly **half** the genome is recognisable as **transposable elements** — LINEs, SINEs (including the primate *Alu* army), leftovers of viruses that moved in and never left. Susumu Ohno, in 1972, called the non-essential bulk **junk DNA**: sequence that is not under selection, a load the genome totes because deleting it is harder than ignoring it. "Junk" in his mouth did not mean "trash you should take out." It meant "the drawer of cables that still might, one day, be a cable."

Then ENCODE, in 2012, assigned **biochemical activity** to about **80 percent** of the genome: transcribed, bound by a protein, modified, something. Headlines declared junk dead. Evolutionary biologists, notably Dan Graur and colleagues, pointed out that "a protein sat on it" is not the same as "the organism needs it." Sequence conserved by selection is more like **eight to fifteen percent**, give or take the study. If 80 percent were load-bearing, the mutational burden might be unsurvivable.

The **C-value paradox** is the same fight with a garden attached. An onion has a much larger genome than you do. A pufferfish gets by with a slim one. A lungfish is carrying around a library that would make a human sequencer weep. Genome size does not track how impressed you are with the organism. If most DNA were precious instruction, onions would be doing something we cannot see, or we are not as instruction-dense as our press agents claimed. The onion test is rude and useful.

So: a lot of the genome is not protein code. Some of the rest is regulation, some is structural, some is parasites that paid rent by accident, some is noise. Calling all of it junk was rude. Calling none of it junk was a press release. The honest word is **mostly unread**. Chapter 04's dark genome is this paragraph with the lights still off.

## The Origin Of The Code 🧊

The molecule is one puzzle. The mapping — three nucleotides to one amino acid — is another.

Francis Crick called it a **frozen accident**: once many proteins depend on a codon table, you cannot change the table without scrambling the library. The code froze because thawing it would be lethal. That still explains the near-universality. It does not explain why the table looks *good*: similar amino acids tend to have similar codons, so a one-letter typo often swaps a cousin, not a stranger.

The live theories, which [chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) already waved at:

- **Stereochemical**: some amino acids just like some bases. Weak affinities, maybe, in RNA aptamers. Not a full table.
- **Coevolution**: the code grew as metabolism invented new amino acids, precursor and product sharing neighbourhoods.
- **Error minimisation**: robustness was selected. The table we have is suspiciously good at not being a disaster.
- **Frozen accident**, still in the mix, because LUCA already had this library and we have a sample size of one.

Mitochondria and a few microbes run dialect versions. In human mitochondria, AUA is methionine, UGA is tryptophan, and some stops have been reassigned. Selenocysteine and pyrrolysine are barnacles on the standard table. The code is frozen-*ish*. We do not know which mix of chemistry, history, and selection wrote the first edition. The helix is a solved structure. The mapping is an origin story with the first chapter missing.

A useful humility: we can build the library in a machine now. We still cannot say, from first principles, why those sixty-four triplets mean those twenty amino acids. That is not a failure of the molecule. It is a failure of our origin story.

## A Gene Is A Fuzzy Word 📎

While we are being honest: **gene** is not a crisp object. School meant "a stretch that codes for a protein." Then alternative splicing, overlapping reading frames, enhancers that live inside other genes' introns, and RNA genes that never become protein, all showed up and refused to leave. A gene is a useful handle for "a heritable unit that does a job," and a terrible handle if you need to count them like apples. GENCODE's twenty-thousand-ish protein-coding number is a catalogue, not a philosophy.

The library metaphor almost fails here, and that is useful. A gene is less a book than a *recipe that is also a mixing instruction that is also, sometimes, a bookmark in someone else's recipe*. Chapter 04 is the chapter that has to live with that mess. This chapter only had to show you the shelves, the spine labels, and the fact that the librarian was printed from the same stock.

If you came here wanting a number of genes to memorise, take twenty thousand protein-coding as a working round number, then immediately forget that it was the interesting part. The interesting part is the two metres, the forty-six, the basement circle, and the code nobody can derive from physics.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Why this code?** Frozen accident, chemistry, coevolution, error-proofing, or a pile. The mapping is older than the life we can see.
- **How much of the genome is load-bearing?** Biochemical activity is not selected function. The percentage that matters to fitness is still a fight, probably closer to a tenth than to four-fifths, with a long tail of "it depends on the cell."
- **How does a two-metre molecule stay unknotted, readable, and copyable inside a nucleus?** Looping, territories, condensates: active research, not a finished IKEA manual.
- **What, exactly, is a centromere as sequence?** Repeats, epigenetics, a kinetochore that can, in some species, sit down in new places. The handle is more than a barcode.
- **How did linear chromosomes and telomeres get started?** Circular bacterial genomes dodge the end-replication problem. Eukaryotes bought caps and a reverse transcriptase. The receipt is missing.
- **Why do genome sizes wander so wildly?** The C-value paradox is still a paradox if you assume DNA is mostly instruction. It is less mysterious if you allow junk, but then you have to say how much.
- **Photo 51's moral** is solved as history better than it used to be, and still not a sitcom. Credit remains a human problem the molecule never asked for.

- Maeshima, K. and others on the scarcity of the canonical 30-nm fibre in living nuclei: the textbook coil is a model; in-cell chromatin is more fluid. Treat the 30-nm picture as a map, not a photograph.
