# The Climate And The Rock 🌧️🪨

The Otways and the You Yangs are not two moods of the same forest. They are two different arguments, happening an hour and a bit apart on a Victorian map, and the trees are only the press conference.

Drive the Great Ocean Road after rain and the windscreen wipers work for a living. Hopetoun Falls sits in a gully that smells rinsed. Tree ferns behave as if drought were a rumour. Drive the other way, out past Little River, and Flinders Peak is a pile of granite with a haircut. The grass is honest. The light comes all the way to the ground. Same state. Same ocean somewhere to the south. Completely different first two cards.

The global method lives next door in [how to read a landscape](../🌱-the-living-world/15-how-to-read-a-landscape.md). This chapter is the local version of the opening move: **climate and parent rock**. Plants come later. Plants are the comment in the margin. If you start with the comment, you will invent a story about "nice trees" and miss the sentence it is attached to.

## The First Two Cards 🃏

A landscape is not a vibe. It is a stack.

1. **Climate** — how much water arrives, when it arrives, how hot the waiting is, how hard the wind works.
2. **Parent rock** — what the ground is made of before soil has an opinion. Sandstone. Basalt. Granite. Limestone. The quiet chemistry under the boots.

Then relief, time, organisms, fire, humans. Hans Jenny wrote the academic version as a function: soil is climate, organisms, relief, parent material, and time, all arguing at once. You do not need the equation on a walk. You need the habit. Look at the sky's annual behaviour. Look at the rock. Then guess why the trees look like that.

Victoria first. The Sunshine Coast as the second dialect. The rest of Australia is the same song in a different key.

This is Wadawurrung, Gadubanud, Eastern Maar, Wurundjeri, Bunurong, Dja Dja Wurrung, Taungurung, Kabi Kabi, Jinibara Country, and more besides, depending where you stand. The rock is older than the names. The reading of it is not.

## Rainfall You Can Feel In The Windscreen 🚗

Victoria's median annual rainfall runs from more than 1800 mm in parts of the Otway Ranges and Eastern Highlands to less than 300 mm in the Mallee. That is not a trivia fact. That is the difference between a closed wet forest and a place that thinks twice before growing a shrub.

You can feel the gradient without a Bureau of Meteorology map.

- **Otway ridge:** winter-wet, orographically greedy. Valleys funnel moisture-laden air uphill; the air cools; the water falls out. Some western-slope sites push toward 1900 mm. Tanybryn once copped 375 mm in a day, which is the sort of number that makes a guttering catalogue look optimistic.
- **Otway rain shadow:** the ranges wring the westerlies. North-east of the ridge, toward Colac and then the western side of Port Phillip, the totals drop hard. The You Yangs sit in one of the driest pockets south of the Divide, around 450 mm. Granite plus rain shadow is a personality, not an accident.
- **Melbourne's wet-dry split:** a north-south line through the city is a decent first cartoon. Melton and the western basalt plains live nearer 500 mm. Mount Dandenong has been clocked around 1200 mm. Same metropolitan area. Different forest, different garden deaths, different excuses.
- **Box-ironbark country** inland: often 500–800 mm, on the northern foothills. Enough for a woodland. Not enough for a fern gully unless a creek is cheating.

Rainfall is not "wet" or "dry" as a lifestyle brand. It is millimetres plus **season**. A place that gets 700 mm mostly in winter is not the same place as one that gets 700 mm in three tropical afternoons and then stares at the sky until October.

## When The Rain Falls Is A Dialect 🥁

Southern Victoria is a winter-rainfall conversation. The Southern Ocean sends fronts. Summers can be sharp, bright, and mean. Plants here have to survive a dry heat with a full fuel load, then a wet cold that sits in the clay.

The Sunshine Coast is the other dialect. Nambour's long-term mean is on the order of **1700 mm**, humid subtropical, with the heavy months in summer. February can out-rain a Victorian winter. The dry is relative, and it happens when a Victorian forest is just getting interesting. That is why a Victorian sclerophyll plant can look heroic in a Sunshine Coast winter and then sulk through a Queensland February as if someone moved the goalposts.

Same literacy. Different calendar.

If you only remember one climate trick: **annual total is the volume; season is the time signature.** A drummer who hits 1700 notes a year in summer bursts is not playing the same song as a drummer who spreads 900 through winter. The band — the plants — will sound different even if the venue looks green.

Evaporation is the uncredited extra. Hot, windy, rain-shadowed granite loses water as fast as it gets it. Cool, cloudy Otway sandstone keeps it. That is why "it rained last week" is not a soil moisture reading. Ask the ferns. They are ruder and more honest.

## Geology You Can Actually See 🔨

You do not need a hand lens and a first-year lecture. You need four Victorian rocks and the honesty to say when you are guessing.

### Sandstone and mudstone: the Otways

The Otway Ranges are an uplifted, deeply dissected block of **Lower Cretaceous** sandstone, siltstone, and mudstone — the Otway Group, dumped in lakes and rivers while Australia and Antarctica were still having a slow breakup. The beds are not polite. They make steep slopes, stepped creeks, waterfalls over the harder sandstone bands, and soils that are moderately heavy, acid, and in the wet bits stuffed with organic matter.

This is why Hopetoun Falls looks like a fantasy novel and the You Yangs do not. The rock is catchable, erodible, and sitting in a rain trap. Wet forest is the logical comment.

### Basalt: the western plains

Western Victoria is a volcanic province wearing a thin, stubborn veneer. About **400 volcanoes** of the Newer Volcanics, mostly in the last few million years, poured basalt across older ground. The Werribee and Keilor plains are this story: dark rock, cracking clays in places, stony rises in others, grasslands and open woodland where the rainfall does not argue back.

Basalt weathers toward clay-rich, often more fertile soils than granite or quartz sandstone. "More fertile" in Australia is a relative term, like "taller than a shetland." It still is not a maple's childhood home. But it is why volcanic country can grow a different paddock, a different remnant, and a different set of garden deaths than the sand country ten kilometres away.

### Granite: the You Yangs and the tors

The You Yangs are **not a volcano**. They are a Devonian granite that cooled underground about 365 million years ago and has since had the roof eroded off. Big feldspar crystals. Tors. Sandy, hungry soils where the rock has grussed. Rain shadow on top.

Granite country often looks like it is showing you the bones: boulders, shallow soil, water shooting off the rock after rain and then vanishing. River red gum in the gullies, tougher woodland upslope, light everywhere. If you try to read it as "degraded wet forest," you will plant the wrong thing and then blame the council.

### Limestone and calcareous sand: the smug coast

West of Cape Otway, and in a different costume on the Mornington and Bellarine Peninsulas, the coast is often **calcarenite** and calcareous dune sand. Shell fragments. Wind-blown. Cemented in places. Alkaline in a continent that is famous for being acid.

This is why coastal tea-tree and moonah look at home on the Nepean dunes and why a camellia looks personally betrayed. Calcium carbonate is not a decorative beige. It is a pH and nutrient regime. Plants that evolved for sour, low-phosphorus sand will not automatically love it, and plants that evolved for sour mountain loam will not either. The limestone is choosy.

### Rhyodacite: the Dandenongs' other trick

The Dandenongs are not "just wetter Melbourne." The high country there is built of Late Devonian silicic volcanics — rhyolite and rhyodacite of the Mount Dandenong Volcanics — hard, quartz-rich, making steep, dissected hills that wring rain out of easterlies and southerlies. Deep, acid, well-watered soils on the sheltered faces. Mountain ash and wet sclerophyll as the comment. The exotic oaks and maples in the villages are a subplot, not the native plot.

## Relief: The Quiet Third Card 📐

Climate and rock are the headline. **Aspect and position** steal scenes.

A south-facing Otway gully and a north-facing Otway ridge can be different forests on the same geology. The gully is cooler, wetter, fire-shy. The ridge is drier, wind-trained, ready to burn. In Victoria the difference is not as theatrical as a Queensland monsoon scarp, but it is enough to put tree ferns in one photo and grass trees in the next.

Creeks cheat. A dry landscape can host a wet line of manna gums, blackwoods, and ferns along a drainage line, then snap back to box and ironbark twenty metres upslope. That is not inconsistency. That is hydrology wearing a costume.

Wind is climate with elbows. Coastal tea-tree is pruned by salt-laden air whether or not the rainfall chart looks generous. A plant that "should" grow here on millimetres alone will still fail if the wind never clocks off.

Temperature is the other climate number people forget because rain is more photogenic. Frost is rare on the Otway coast and fussy inland. Snow sits on the higher Otway ridge a couple of days a year and makes a Victorian look excited. The Sunshine Coast hinterland can still drop below zero on a Nambour winter night, which shocks people who packed for "Queensland," but the *mean* winter is a different animal from a Dandenongs July. Heat, not just millimetres, decides whether a myrtle beech is living its best life or just hanging on in a gully like a band in a shrinking venue.

## Four Victorian Sentences, Same Grammar 🗺️

If climate and rock are cards, here is the opening hand for the places this shelf will keep walking.

| Place | Climate, roughly | Rock you can see | First plant comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Otway wet ridge / fern gully | 1000–2000 mm, winter-wet, cool, cloudy | Cretaceous sandstone and mudstone | Tall wet forest, myrtle beech in the wettest shelter, tree ferns as a water meter |
| You Yangs | ~450 mm, rain-shadowed, evaporative | Devonian granite tors | Low woodland, light to the ground, red gum cheating in the gullies |
| Dandenongs wet face | ~1000–1200 mm at the hill, orographic | Rhyodacite, steep, acid | Wet sclerophyll, mountain ash on the deep sheltered soils, ferns in the gullies |
| Nepean / calcareous coast | Moderate rain, salt wind, summer-dry | Calcareous dune sand over limestone | Tea-tree and moonah, alkaline chemistry, drainage as personality |
| Box-ironbark foothills | 500–800 mm, hot summers | Palaeozoic sediments, stony, often gold-bearing | Open box and ironbark, tough grass, fire as furniture |

Those numbers are regional, not a promise to your particular creek. Use them as a tuning fork. If the forest in front of you violently disagrees with the row, you are either in a gully, on a weird soil, looking at a plantation, or standing in a story that fire or people rewrote.

From Keilor Downs, this is not abstract. West is basalt plains and rain shadow. East is the Dandenongs getting orographic about it. South-west is granite. Further south-west is the Otway rain machine. The [hike list](../🏙️-melbourne/hikes.md) is a set of field sites sitting on those sentences. Teddy's Lookout is not Sherbrooke Falls with a better view. Point Nepean is not the 1000 Steps with salt.

## A Road Cutting Is A Free Museum 🛣️

You do not need a soil pit. You need a cutting, a creek bank, a boulder, or a tree thrown over in last year's wind.

- **Pale, gritty, single-grained:** sand, often coastal or granite wash. Water will not linger unless a pan stops it.
- **Red, sticky, structured:** basaltic or deeply weathered volcanic; iron is showing off.
- **Yellow-grey clay under a sandy top:** duplex country, common, winter-wet on top of a clogged subsoil. Victoria does this a lot.
- **White crystals in a grey rock, boulder piles:** granite. The soil will be coarse and hungry unless something else has been dumped on it.
- **Creamy, shelly, maybe cemented:** calcarenite. Taste is a bad idea. pH is the point.
- **Bedded, brown-grey, waterfall steps:** Otway sandstone and siltstone. The creek is reading the hard bands for you.

If the cutting and the canopy tell different stories, believe the cutting first and then ask what water or fire is doing. A wet-looking garden on sand is a tap. A dry-looking ridge on deep loam is a drought, a burn, or a cow.

## Plants Are The Margin Comment 📝

Once you have climate and rock, the vegetation stops being decoration.

Tall wet forest with tree ferns on Cretaceous sandstone is the Otways saying: reliable moisture, shelter, fire as a visitor. Open woodland on granite in a rain shadow is the You Yangs saying: water is rationed, soil is shallow, light is the default. Banksias and tea-tree on pale calcareous sand are the coast saying: drainage is the whole personality, and the chemistry is not the Dandenongs'.

The method from the expedition map:

1. Notice wet or dry, open or closed, ferns or grass, sand or clay on the boots.
2. Name the *kind* of country, not the species.
3. Ask what climate and rock would have to be, to make this the logical comment.
4. Check later.

Inference is a first draft. A weed infestation is a liar with good lighting. A garden is a liar with a tap. A single street tree is a municipal decision, not a biome.

## The Sunshine Coast, Briefly, As The Other Key 🌞

[Nambour, Sippy Downs, and the Sunshine Coast](../🕰️-origin-stories/nambour-sippy-downs-and-the-sunshine-coast.md) already told the human story: Kabi Kabi and Jinibara Country, timber, sugar, a remnant wallum block that nobody wanted until a university arrived. The ecological story is the same method with the volume turned up.

Hinterland ranges catch summer rain and can hold rainforest and wet sclerophyll on richer or more sheltered ground. The coastal plain is often old sand, sour, nutrient-poor, seasonally wet: **wallum**. Sippy Downs was described in the 1870s as open wallum, swampy, sandy, full of holes, unsuitable for agriculture. That is not an insult. That is a soil and climate diagnosis that later chapters will cash.

Victorian wet forest and Queensland rainforest can both look "lush" from a car. They are not interchangeable. The rain arrives in a different season, the winter is a different temperature, the fire is a different frequency, and the rock under the sand is a different argument. Same literacy. Do not pack the Otways in the ute and expect them to applaud February.

A useful Sunshine Coast split, before [chapter 07](07-walkable-case-studies.md) does the walking:

- **Hinterland ranges and wet gullies:** more rain, more shelter, basalt or weathered sediments in places, rainforest and wet sclerophyll as the comment. Flooded gum and brush box at the eucalypt edge. Vine forest where fire has been a rare visitor.
- **Wallum sandplain:** pale sand, acid, low phosphorus, coffee rock downstairs, banksia and heath as the comment. Seasonal wet feet, then drought in the same profile. This is the land selectors did not want. The plants that live there are not "struggling." They are specialised.
- **Calcareous versus siliceous coasts:** not every beach is wallum, and not every dune is alkaline. Victorian calcarenite and Queensland siliceous wallum can both be "sand" and still be different chemistries. Sand is a texture. It is not a personality test.

If you grew up with one dialect, the other will feel like the drums have been moved. That is the point of a second dialect. It stops you from thinking Victorian wet is the default setting of "Australian bush."

## A Walk, Before The Latin 🚶

You do not need [chapter 03](03-eucalypts-as-a-reading-key.md) to start. Try this.

Stand still. Is the air rinsed or dusty? Can you see the sky through the canopy, or is the canopy a ceiling? Look at a cutting, a creek bank, a boulder: pale sand, red clay, dark basalt, granite crystals, crumbling limestone? Feel the slope. Note the aspect if you can.

Then guess, out loud, like a person who is allowed to be wrong:

- "This feels like more than a metre of rain, winter-wet, sheltered."
- "This feels like rain-shadow granite, shallow, light-filled."
- "This feels like coastal lime sand, and the tea-tree is not confused."

Check it against a map, a ranger sign, Costermans, or the [hike notes](../🏙️-melbourne/hikes.md) that already told you where to stand. The bush will not mark the homework. It will just keep being itself.

If you want a sports analogy you can actually use: climate is the fixture list, rock is the ground staff. You can guess a lot about the match from those two before anyone kicks a ball. You still have to watch the game. Fire, water, and succession are later chapters. They are the game. This chapter is why the Otways are not playing on the same ground as the You Yangs, and why a Sunshine Coast February is not a Victorian July in a louder jumper.

One more honesty check. **Could grow here** is not a conclusion this chapter is allowed to reach. Climate and rock tell you the envelope. Soil, fire, fungi, and a hundred years of clearing tell you the rest. A maple can be kept alive on Otway rainfall with enough mulch and denial. That is horticulture, not literacy. Literacy is noticing that the remnant vegetation never asked for a maple.

Climate and rock are the first two cards. [Soils you can see](02-soils-you-can-see.md) is the third, and it is where the boots get involved.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Microclimate is real and rude.** A gully can be a different climate from the ridge on the same geology. Models smear. Boots do not.
- **Rainfall maps lie at paddock scale.** Orographic funnelling, rain shadows in small valleys, and the difference between rain and *effective* rain (after evaporation and runoff) are still easy to get wrong.
- **We do not always know the pre-colonial fire and water story in enough detail** to say what "natural" meant on this slope. The rock is clearer than the regime.
- **Maps of "average rainfall" hide droughts and flood years.** A wet-forest plant lives through the worst year, not the mean. The mean is a brochure.
- **Climate is moving.** A 1990s average is not a promise. Wet forest on the edge of its rainfall envelope is not obliged to stay wet forest because a guidebook said so.
- **Parent rock is not destiny.** Colluvium, alluvium, wind-blown sand, and a century of scraping can put the "wrong" soil on the "right" geology. Look at the ground, not only the geological map.
