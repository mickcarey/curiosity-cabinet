# Death And Rotting 🍂

The forest walk in [chapter 15](15-how-to-read-a-landscape.md) was supposed to teach you climate, rock, soil, water, plants, in that order. Then you trip over a log.

The log is not a mess. It is a habitat. It is also a sentence about [chapter 07](07-what-a-calorie-actually-is.md): the calorie's last job is someone else's lunch.

So: what happens when the process from [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md) stops? Life was a verb. Death, on that picture, is the verb parking. Rotting is the neighbourhood noticing, and eating.

This is not a funeral guide. It is not a grief manual. It is not "death positivity" merch, which is a tote bag with a skull and a business model. If a sentence here is used as a eulogy, the sentence has been kidnapped. Biology first. Philosophy light, and only after the fungi have had their say.

## A Hard Line, Before The Fungi 🚫

Read this once.

This note is **what organismal death is as a process, and what decomposition is as ecology**. Typical mechanisms. Some of the ways the definition of "dead" is messier than a form wants. It does not tell anyone what to do with a body, a will, a hospital, a feeling, or a forest. Disposable soma is a theory of why bodies wear out, not a lifestyle. Whale falls and snags are habitats, not metaphors for your weekend. If you are here because someone died, talk to a human whose job is that. The cabinet will still be here when you want the detrital food web as a detrital food web.

Right. Verbs.

## Two Deaths, Not One 🕯️

[Chapter 09](09-the-cell-as-a-city.md) already owned **apoptosis**: programmed cell death, the cell packing itself, blebbing, eaten by neighbours, no siren. Kerr, Wyllie, and Currie named it from leaves falling. You have fingers instead of paddles because a demolition permit ran on schedule. Complementary to mitosis: one programme adds cells, one subtracts them, a tissue is the running total.

That is **cell death**. It happens in you, by the billion, while you read. It is not you dying. It is the city doing maintenance.

**Organismal death** is the other thing: the process from chapter 01 stopping. Energy flow fails. Information is no longer copied in a way that maintains the loop. Structure stops being repaired as a self. Schrödinger's radiator cools. Autopoiesis, Maturana and Varela's factory-that-builds-the-factory, ceases to produce the factory. Joyce's working definition has nothing left to work.

A mule can die. A sterile worker bee can die. Reproduction was never the whole bill. The bills that come due are the three: energy, information, structure. Stop paying, and the pattern is not a self. It is a pile of molecules that used to be organised as one.

Necrosis, from chapter 09, is the messy cell death: swelling, leaking, inflammatory. Apoptosis is the permit. Organismal death, in a mammal, is usually a lot of necrosis and a lot of apoptosis and then a lot of neither, because the coordination has gone. The cities stop talking. The country is the thing that ended.

Hold the poetry. The interesting trouble is that "the country ended" is a legal and medical sentence as much as a biological one, and the cells do not all get the memo at once. We will come back to that. First, why the country was never designed to last.

## Why Bodies Wear Out ⏳

[Chapter 16](16-mysteries-theories-and-the-unsolved.md) already has the aging section. This paragraph is a pointer, not a rewrite.

Peter Medawar noticed that selection gets weak after you have already reproduced. Tom Kirkwood's **disposable soma** idea, 1977, is the sports version: a club that invests in the academy and the first team, not in keeping veterans immortal, will beat a club that tries to make every player last forever. Resources spent on perfect repair are resources not spent on offspring. Once you are past the reproductive window, the accountant shrugs.

There is no law of physics that says you must age. There is an evolutionary accounting that says perfect somatic maintenance does not pay, in a world that will eat you for other reasons first. Aging is the pile of unrepaired damage that accounting allowed. López-Otín's hallmarks — genomic instability, telomeres, senescent cells that will not leave the pitch, a microbiome that stops being a good housemate — are the failure modes. They are not a single fuse.

The [immortal jellyfish](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-immortal-jellyfish.md) cheats by hitting reset on cell identity. That is a different exam. It is not a plan. Naked mole rats and bristlecone pines are reminders that "aging" is a family of strategies, not a universal countdown. Evolution can extend the warranty when it pays. It does not do it for free, and it does not do it because a tote bag asked.

So: bodies wear out because the lineage bet on reproduction over immortality, and because oxygen is a pyromaniac we hired, and because copy-error accumulates. Then the process stops. Then the calorie goes looking for a new job.

## Decomposition Is Ecology, Not A Bin 🪱

A dead body, a fallen tree, a leaf on the path: these are not waste. They are **detritus**, and detritus is a food web with its own celebrities.

**Bacteria** arrive first in most tissues, or were already there, waiting. They take the easy sugars, the proteins, the bits that do not need a specialist. Anaerobes take over where oxygen runs out. Putrefaction is chemistry with a smell: amines, sulfur compounds, the reason a carcass is a signal you can read from upwind.

Your own gut city, from chapter 11, is already a decomposer guild on a leash. When the leash goes slack, they do not become sentimental. They become the first shift.

**Fungi** take the rude stuff. Lignin, from [chapter 13](13-plants-are-not-furniture.md), is a polymer designed to be annoying.

- **White-rot** fungi chew lignin and leave pale, fibrous wood. They have the peroxidase kit. They are the specialists you would hire if wood were a crime.
- **Brown-rot** fungi leave the lignin and take the cellulose. The wood goes cubical and rusty. A brown-rot log is a carbon shape that used to be a beam.
- **Soft-rot** works the damp, slower, ascomycetes in the wet.

A log is a fungal city with a wood problem. Hyphae are the streets.

**Invertebrates** are the engineers and the mouths. Blowflies and their maggots on a carcass, on a schedule forensic people have tried to turn into a clock. The clock is real-ish and local, not a TV show: temperature, access, season, whether a fox opened the door first. Beetles. Worms. Springtails. Termites, in the countries that have them, turning wood into soil with a gut full of symbionts. *Osedax*, the bone-eating worms on whale skeletons, which chemically mine collagen and lipid and look like something a tired god invented at 3 a.m.

Scavenging vertebrates — vultures, foxes, hagfish, a neighbour's dog — are the megafauna version of the same job. They do not "clean up." They convert a rare, rich patch into muscle and faeces and a wider scatter of bone. The scatter is still habitat. A bone is a phosphorus slow-release tablet with a calcium shell.

The **detrital food web** is the name for this whole economy: energy that did not go through a green leaf *this week*, but through a dead one, or a dead animal, or a turd. Grazing food webs get the documentaries. Detrital food webs get the actual calories in a forest. Most of a woodland's energy does not go herbivore → carnivore. It goes leaf → fungus → mite → and so on, or leaf → bacterium → protozoan. The charismatic megafauna are a side hustle. The rotting is the job.

Swift, Heal, and Anderson wrote the textbook version of this in 1979: decomposition as a process controlled by the quality of the litter, the organisms present, and the physical environment. Temperature, moisture, oxygen, the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of the meal. Sawdust, as chapter 14 said, is a nitrogen sponge until the microbes finish the carbs. A carcass is the opposite meal: nitrogen-rich, a party. A eucalypt leaf is a chemist's joke, oils and phenolics, slow rotting on purpose.

If you want a music version: grazing is the single. Detritus is the catalogue. Most of the streaming numbers are the catalogue.

Fire, in Australia, is the competing decomposer. Chapter 14 already said it: sometimes the native accountant is a flame. A hot fire does in an hour what fungi would do in a decade, and it does not leave the same habitat. Charcoal is the residue that rots slowly. Ash is the nutrients that did not get to be a beetle. The landscape method's disturbance slot is this paragraph wearing a different hat. Rotting is not the only way a calorie leaves a log. It is the way that builds soil instead of a smoke column.

## Nutrient Return: The Atoms Clock Off And On Again 🔁

[Chapter 14](14-soil-is-alive.md) already ran nitrogen as a character and phosphorus as the bottleneck that does not do interviews. Jenny's factors stay there. This heading is only the **return ticket**.

**Carbon**: respiration by the decomposers. CO₂ back to the air, or into a microbial body, or into humus that hangs around, or into a deeper, mineral-associated pool that can sit for centuries. A log is not a puff of smoke the week it falls. Harmon and colleagues, compiling decay rates across biomes, found *k* for downed coarse wood varying more than two-hundred-fold globally. Species, diameter, standing versus downed, canopy openness, temperature. A 10 °C rise in mean annual temperature, in their synthesis, multiplied *k* by about 2.6, with a lot of scatter. Standing dead wood sometimes barely decays, sometimes goes faster than the log on the ground. The time course is the point. Death is not a carbon pulse with a date stamp.

**Nitrogen**: mineralisation. Organic N becomes ammonium as microbes eat the dead. Then the nitrifiers, the plants, the denitrifiers, the leachate, the whole play from chapter 14. A carcass is a nitrogen bomb on a small patch. A leaf litter layer is a slow drip. Immobilisation if the meal is carbon-heavy: the microbes keep the N for themselves until the carbs run down.

**Phosphorus**: the rock never had a gaseous stage worth mentioning. Organic P in the dead — nucleic acids, phospholipids, phytate — is the vault the phosphatases work on. In old, weathered landscapes the dead *are* the phosphorus economy. Lambers' "vegetarian" phosphorus strategy, from chapter 14: recycle the corpse, because the apatite is a rumour. A rainforest on a laterite is a lush closed loop wearing a disguise. Kill the decomposers, in the thought experiment, and the disguise falls off.

The atoms do not die. The arrangement does. Chapter 02's CHNOPS kit is rented. Rotting is how the lease returns to the pool.

## Dead Wood, Carcasses, Litter: Habitats, Not Mess 🪵

A tidy forest is a hobby. A functioning forest is untidy on purpose.

**Coarse woody debris** — snags (standing dead trees), logs, big branches — is structure. Harmon, Franklin, and a small army, in a 1986 review that became a field, listed the jobs: habitat, energy flow, nutrient cycling, influence on soil and sediment. Cavity-nesting birds. Bats. Salamanders under the bark. Beetles that will not live anywhere else. A log dam in a stream, storing sediment, making a pool, rewriting the reach. Nurse logs, in wet forests, that become the next tree's first soil.

Pan and colleagues, 2011, put dead wood at about **8% of the carbon stock in the world's forests**, litter another 5%, live biomass 42%, soil to a metre 44%. Those are global averages with error bars that should humble a press release. In some old temperate stands the dead fraction is much larger. In a heavily tidied plantation it is a rounding error, which is one reason a plantation is not a forest with the serial numbers filed off.

**Leaf litter** is the inbox. Chapter 14's O horizon. Fungi, bacteria, mites, springtails, the slow grind. A dry sclerophyll floor in Australia is a different inbox from an Otway gully: oils, lignin, fire as the competing accountant. Litter is not dirt. It is a layer of last year's solar panel, being unbuilt.

**Carcasses** are rare, rich, and loud. A kangaroo on a verge. A fish on a tide line. A whale, later. Scavengers first — the mobile mouths — then the insects, then the microbes, then a patch of grass that is greener than it has any right to be, because nitrogen does not stay in the corpse. Carrion is a habitat with a deadline. Some beetles are specialists. Some plants will wait.

The landscape method said a gap from a fallen tree is succession in miniature. Believe it. The log is the gap's furniture.

A sports version, because "habitat" is not a synonym for "messy": a dead tree is not a player who left the pitch. It is the stadium becoming a skate park, then a garden, then a rumour of a stadium in the soil. You can prefer the original fixture. The mites do not.

## Marine Snow: The Ocean's Rotting ❄️

The land has logs. The ocean has a blizzard that is not ice.

[Marine snow](../🌙-bedtime-reading/marine-snow-the-oceans-slow-blizzard.md) is the detrital food web standing up and falling down: dead plankton, fragments, fecal pellets, mucus, dust, the crumbs of the sunlit world, drifting through the dark. NOAA calls it a continuous shower. The deep sea cannot grow a surface-style garden. It waits. A fraction of the snow is eaten on the way. A fraction feeds the twilight-zone gatekeepers. A smaller fraction reaches the mud and is someone's lunch, or someone's climate, because this is also the **biological carbon pump**.

[Chapter 17](17-the-ocean-is-another-planet-we-already-have.md) already treats the ocean as another planet we already have. This chapter only needs the admission: **rotting is how the abyss is paid**. A calorie fixed by a diatom at noon may be respired at 500 metres by a particle-attached bacterium at Thursday tea, or parked in sediment for a geological minute. The snow is not poetry. It is the catalogue, wet.

Most days are snow. Some days are a whale.

## Soil Is, In Large Part, Processed Death 🪦

Say it without flinching.

Humus is not a mystical essence. Chapter 14 already refused that. It is a messy, partly decomposed, chemically stubborn pool of **carbon that was alive**. Living roots and hyphae are in there too. So are the microbes themselves, which are alive, and then dead, and then someone else's meal. A teaspoon of soil is a city. A large fraction of the city's building stock is corpses in various stages of becoming chemistry.

The O horizon is last year's leaves. The A horizon is those leaves plus mineral, mixed, the living room. Charcoal is fire's version of the same accounting: carbon parked in a form that rots slowly. Mineral-associated organic matter is the bit that stuck to clay and got a longer lease.

Jenny's organisms-as-a-factor includes the dead. A forest that exported every fallen stick would be a forest that exported its own capital. The "humus capital" Jenny later treated as a national asset is, bluntly, processed death plus the living that feeds on it. You are walking on an unread library, chapter 14 said. You are also walking on a graveyard that is a kitchen. Both can be true. Only one of them is supposed to sound spooky, and it should not.

Dirt is what you call soil when you have not been introduced. "Clean" is what you call a habitat when you have not noticed it is hungry.

## Succession After A Death 🌳

A death is a disturbance. Chapter 15 already said landscapes are mosaics of different-aged patches. Here are two patches with extra plot.

**A fallen tree.** Gap in the canopy. Light hits the floor. Seedlings that were waiting, or seeds that were in the bank, or a lateral branch from a neighbour, take the space. The log itself goes through decay classes: bark on, bark off, moss, softness, a rectangular stain of darker soil, gone. Insects sequence themselves. Fungi sequence themselves. A salamander does not care about your climax-forest poster. Primary versus secondary still earns its keep: the soil and the seed bank usually survived, so this is secondary succession with a piece of furniture. Connell and Slatyer's facilitation / inhibition / tolerance models from 1977 are the mechanisms people argue about. The walkable fact is: the gap is a clock. The log is a clock. They are not the same clock.

**A whale fall.** Smith and Baco, and the people who parked submersibles on purpose, described overlapping stages on the deep seafloor:

1. **Mobile scavengers** — hagfish, sleeper sharks, crabs — strip soft tissue. Months to a couple of years, depending on the whale. Forty to sixty kilograms a day, in some of the measured feasts.
2. **Enrichment opportunists** — polychaetes and crustaceans in ridiculous densities on the bones and the enriched sediment. Months to years.
3. **Sulfophilic stage** — anaerobic microbes eat bone lipids, leak sulfide, and a chemosynthetic neighbourhood clocks on: bacteria, clams, mussels, worms, a vent-like town with no vent. Decades, on a large carcass. A 40-ton whale may carry two to three tonnes of lipid in the skeleton alone.
4. **Reef** — when the organics are gone, hard substrate, suspension feeders, a quieter furniture.

Stages overlap. Small whales skip plot. *Osedax* mines the bones and shortens the play. A whale fall is a carcass that became a town, then a factory, then a rock. Chapter 17's "one body, decades of a town" is this paragraph. Most days, again, are snow. The whale is the drum fill.

A wood fall on the abyssal plain is a cousin: xylophagaid bivalves instead of hagfish, a different specialist guild, the same idea. Death as a resource pulse. Succession as the pulse being spent.

## What "You" Is When The Process Stops 🪞

Biology first.

"You," as this series has used the word, were never a substance. You were a process that paid three bills, in a nested community, with a nametag of convenience. Mitochondria from an egg. A microbiome rented from the world. A repertoire of lymphocytes that learned a working self. Thirty trillion human cells in conversation.

When the conversation stops, the nametag is a memory other people hold. The atoms are still rented.

Some cells keep going for a while: hair, a cruel anecdote, is not a soul lingering, it is keratin production with leftover ATP. Gut bacteria do not know the landlord left. They throw a party. That party is decomposition starting from the inside, which is why a body is not a sterile log.

You were a holobiont. The holobiont does not end in one minute. The *organismal* pattern does.

A Ship of Theseus problem, if you want the cabinet's [own note](../💡-concepts/ship-of-theseus.md): you were already a replacement project while alive. Death is not the first time the planks changed. It is the first time the crew stopped caulking. The ship does not become a different ship. It becomes driftwood, then a reef, then someone else's keel, if we are still doing metaphors. Biology would like us to stop at "the process is not running."

Philosophy light, because the cabinet already parked consciousness next door.

Is the self the process, or the atoms, or the information, or the story told about it? Chapter 01 refused a definition everyone would sign. Death inherits that refusal. A flame goes out and the air is still air. A river is diverted and the bed is a scar. You were closer to the flame and the river than to the rock. The rock was the kit. The verb was the point.

The forest does not agree to a moment. A snag can stand for decades, dead as an organism, fiercely alive as a habitat. The tree stopped. The city on the tree did not. That is not a consolation prize in a funeral home. It is a fact about trophic levels. If it feels like a consolation anyway, that is your business, not this chapter's product line.

## The Definition Is Messier Than A Form 📋

People want a switch. Biology offers a dimmer, then a committee.

**Brain death**, as a clinical and legal category, is younger than rock and roll. A Harvard ad hoc committee in 1968 proposed irreversible coma as a criterion for death, in a world that had learned to ventilate bodies whose brains had stopped organising breath. The 1981 President's Commission and the Uniform Determination of Death Act offered two roads: irreversible cessation of circulation and respiration, or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. Death as the loss of the organism as an integrated whole.

The trouble, which bioethicists have not stopped poking: some functions can linger. Hypothalamic bits. Nests of cells. A body on a ventilator can still heal a wound, fight an infection, even, in grim documented cases, gestate. Alan Shewmon and others used those facts to argue that "the organism as a whole" had not obviously left the building. Defenders of the standard say the integration that matters is the brain's, and the leftovers are a collection of parts. The argument is not settled in the way a form is settled. The form is a policy. The biology is a pile of cells that did not all get the memo.

This chapter is not going to tell a hospital what to do. It is going to say: **organismal death is a definition we need, and the cells are not obliged to honour it on a schedule.** Chapter 01's spore problem — is a paused process alive? — returns in a darker suit. A frozen cell is a pause. A ventilated body with no brainstem is a different, ethically loaded pause, or not a pause at all, depending who you ask. Anyone who says the embryology or the ICU trace *settles the noun* is selling a mapping they brought with them. Biology will give you stopped hearts, flat EEGs, cells that still metabolise, guts that still digest. It will not give you the legal noun for free.

Meanwhile, in the forest, the question is almost funny.

**How much of a forest is standing death?** Snags. Heart-rot in a living trunk, a tree that is a chimney of fungi and still flushing leaves. Dead branches on a live crown. Coarse roots that died and were not told to the canopy. Pan's 8% is dead *wood* as a carbon pool, not "how much of the scene in front of you is dead." The scene in front of you is worse, or better, depending on taste: a lot of the structure is necromass. Old-growth is partly a standing graveyard that is also a standing hotel. Tidying it is a human aesthetic. The woodpecker did not get the memo.

Harmon's point about standing versus downed decay is the ecological version of the brain-death argument: position changes the clock. A snag is not a log. A ventilated body is not a corpse in a fridge. Clocks are local. Definitions are committees. The fungi do not attend the committee.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Where, exactly, organismal death is.** Heart, brain, integration, information, the last cell. Policy picked two roads. Cells keep running past both in awkward cases. The spore problem from chapter 01 never left.
- **How much of a forest is standing death**, and how we should even count. Carbon pools (dead wood ~8% globally, more locally) are not the visual fraction, and the visual fraction is not the functional fraction. Heart-rot, snags, dead roots: the living tree is already a mixed estate.
- **Why decay rates scatter so hard.** Species, size, position, climate, the decomposer guild. Harmon's 244-fold range is a warning to anyone who models a dead tree as a puff of CO₂ next Tuesday.
- **How much of deep-sea carbon actually arrives, and in what form.** Marine snow's flux is measured, argued, climate-relevant, and still a budget with holes. Twilight-zone gatekeepers do not file timesheets we like.
- **Whether disposable soma is the whole evolutionary why of aging.** It is a strong theory. Antagonistic pleiotropy is a cousin. Programmed-aging views keep applying and mostly failing the evidence. Chapter 16 keeps the pile. This chapter only needed the accounting.
- **What "you" refers to when the process stops.** Biology: a nested community whose organising verb ended. Philosophy, law, grief: not this series' job. Consciousness stays in [its own note](../📰-articles/consciousness-and-the-mind.md).
- **How to put humans into the rotting.** We bury, we burn, we refrigerate, we landfill, we compost, we delay the detrital food web on purpose. That is a soil-forming factor Jenny put in the dots. It is also a culture. This chapter will not moralise a burial.
