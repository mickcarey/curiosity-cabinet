# How to Build a Tree Out of Thin Air ⚛️🌳

A tiny seed becomes a tree heavy enough to crush a car. Where does all that extra stuff come from?

The ground seems an obvious answer. Yet much of a tree's dry material began as a gas in the air. A tree is doing something far stranger than drinking dirt through a straw.

To see how, we need to meet its building pieces.

## 🧱 The pieces are too small to see

Everything around you is made of **atoms**: tiny pieces of chemical elements, such as carbon, oxygen and iron. Different elements are different kinds of atom.

Atoms can join to make **molecules**. A water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Its name tag, H₂O, is a little ingredient list.

Chemical reactions rearrange these pieces. The atoms in a leaf weren't created by the tree. They were borrowed from its surroundings and joined in new ways.

Carbon is especially handy because one carbon atom can make four strong connections. Carbon atoms link into chains, branches and rings. Add other kinds of atom, and you can make everything from sugar to the molecules that carry inherited instructions. One building kit, astonishingly many models.

## 🍃 Here comes the air-powered part

Leaves take in **carbon dioxide**, a gas containing carbon and oxygen. Roots collect water. Using energy from sunlight, the plant turns these ingredients into sugars and releases oxygen. This is **photosynthesis**.

The plant can join sugar units into **cellulose**, the tough material supporting its tiny living units, called cells. Cellulose helps build stems, leaves and wood.

Roots also supply minerals the plant needs. They matter enormously, but they aren't delivering ready-made timber. The carbon in the trunk arrived largely through the leaves.

[Plant biology](../plant-biology.md) follows the whole operation, including how a tree gets water up to those leaves.

## 💧 Why life is so wet

Inside a cell, water helps dissolved ingredients move around and meet. Its molecules have slightly positive and slightly negative regions, which attract other charged particles. This helps explain why salt dissolves in water.

Oil doesn't mix in the same way. Some oily molecules have one end that mixes well with water and another that avoids it. Put enough together and they can form a double layer, hiding the oily ends inside. That is the basic arrangement of a [cell's boundary](cells-and-organelles.md).

Water has another useful trick: ice floats. Water expands as it freezes, making ice less dense than liquid water. A lake can freeze across its surface while liquid water remains underneath. A welcome arrangement if you are a fish.

## 🌬️ Ingredients can be present but unusable

Plants need nitrogen to make many working parts. Air contains plenty of nitrogen gas, yet plants cannot use that gas directly. Its atoms are joined too tightly for their usual machinery to separate.

Certain microscopic organisms can do the job. They turn nitrogen gas into substances that other life can use. Some live with plant roots; others work elsewhere in soil and water.

Phosphorus, another important ingredient, comes largely from minerals and recycled remains. Iron helps our blood carry oxygen. Magnesium helps plants capture light. Tiny amounts can make enormous differences: a bicycle doesn't need much metal in its chain, but try riding without it.

## 🧤 Even the shape of the pieces matters

Some molecules come in mirror-image versions, like left and right hands. Living machinery often works with one version much better than the other. A left glove and a right glove contain the same material; that doesn't make them interchangeable.

Why life settled on particular versions remains a puzzle. So does whether life elsewhere could use very different chemistry. Silicon, common in rocks, is a favourite science-fiction candidate, but copying carbon's versatility in watery conditions is difficult.

Here on Earth, the everyday result is already extraordinary: air, water and minerals can become a sunflower. The trick is the arrangement—and a continuing supply of energy.
