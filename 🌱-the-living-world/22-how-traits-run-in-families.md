# How Traits Run in Families 👨‍👩‍👧

You are not a 50/50 smoothie of your parents.

School sold that cartoon with a straight face: half Mum, half Dad, blend until beige. The molecule never signed it. You are a **remix** — two haploid playlists, shuffled, crossed over, then performed in a body that also has to eat, sleep, catch sunlight or not, and survive other people's cooking. The recipe is not the dish. The dish is not destiny.

Blending was the older, more intuitive physics. Mix red and white paint, you get pink, and you cannot get the red back. Darwin needed heredity that *kept* variation, because natural selection is a filter, and a filter on a smoothie just makes a thinner smoothie. He did not have a working particle theory. Mendel was counting particles in Brno at roughly the same time and the letters did not meet in the right order. Chapter 23 is the engine that required this chapter's particles. This one is why your sister has your father's eyebrows and your mother's patience and a laugh nobody can place.

This chapter is the missing landing between the library and the machines. [Chapter 03](03-dna-the-library-that-builds-the-librarian.md) gave you the shelves. [Chapter 04](04-how-cells-read-and-rewrite-the-library.md) gave you the verbs. [Chapter 10](10-from-gametes-to-a-person.md) already did the chromosome dance that *makes* a gamete, including independent assortment, so we are not stealing the pregnancy.

Here is what that dance looks like when it shows up at a family lunch: alleles, the dominant/recessive cartoon, why siblings are not reprints, and why "a gene for height" is a sentence that should be walked back out of the room.

I am the AI narrator. Mick asked how traits run in families without turning the answer into a horoscope, a 23andMe sermon, or a ghost of the twentieth century's worst ideas. Fair. This is not medical advice. It is not an ancestry-test religion. It is not a ranking of people. Heredity is a mechanism. The moment someone treats it as a hierarchy — better blood, worse blood, a policy — they have left the chapter and entered a history that does not get a sequel here. If a sentence here is used as a diagnosis, a breeding programme, or a personality, the sentence has been kidnapped.

## Allele, Genotype, Phenotype 🎚️

A **gene**, as [chapter 03](03-dna-the-library-that-builds-the-librarian.md) already admitted, is a fuzzy word. For this chapter it is a useful handle: a stretch of the library that can exist in more than one edition.

An **allele** is one of those editions. Same address on the chromosome, different spelling. Blood-type A versus B. A pea-plant allele that makes round seeds versus one that makes wrinkled ones. You typically carry two copies of each nuclear gene, because you are diploid: one from each parent, sitting on homologous chromosomes. Those two copies might match (**homozygous**) or they might not (**heterozygous**).

**Genotype** is the spelling you actually have. **Phenotype** is what shows up — the seed, the height, the pigment, the whatever — after the cell has read the library, folded the proteins, and lived in a world. Genotype is the setlist. Phenotype is the gig, including the room, the weather, and whether the guitar was in tune.

That gap is the whole chapter. People talk as if genes *are* traits. Genes are recipes that a body interprets, in an environment, with a developmental history. Two people can share a genotype and not share a life. Two people can share a look and not share a spelling.

If you want a sports picture: genotype is the squad. Phenotype is the match. The same eleven can play like a symphony or like a wet Tuesday in July, depending on the pitch, the weather, and who showed up hungover. Heredity is not the scoreboard. It is the roster plus a lot of uncredited staff.

A family notices phenotypes. "She has your nose." "He has the family stubbornness." Those are observations, and they are allowed. The mistake is treating them as single-locus stories, or as proof that the trait is "in the blood" in the folkloric sense. Blood, as a metaphor for heredity, was always a smoothie word. DNA is a library. Libraries do not pour.

## The Dominant/Recessive Cartoon, And Then The Mess 🎨

Gregor Mendel, counting peas in a Brno monastery in the 1850s and 60s, noticed something rude to blending. Cross a true-breeding purple-flowered pea with a true-breeding white-flowered one, and the children are all purple. The white did not average out. It hid. Cross those purple children with each other, and white comes back, about one in four.

The cartoon that escaped into Year 10:

- Each parent hands over one **allele** per gene.
- In the heterozygote, one allele is **dominant** (you see it) and one is **recessive** (you do not, until it meets another copy of itself).
- 3:1 in the grandchildren, if you are lucky and the trait is this simple.

Under the hood, that 3:1 is a 1:2:1 of genotypes — one homozygote for the visible allele, two heterozygotes, one homozygote for the hidden one — with the first three boxes painted the same colour. The worksheet Punnett square is just "pick one of two from each parent, four equally likely meetings." It feels like a board game because, for one locus with two alleles and clean dominance, it *is* a board game. Life is not usually a board game. Mendel's seven pea characters (round/wrinkled, yellow/green, purple/white flowers, and the rest) were chosen, or lucky, to behave like one.

**Dominant does not mean common, better, or stronger.** It means visible in the heterozygote. A rare dominant allele is still rare. A recessive allele can be the usual one in a population. The words are about masking, not about a league table. Biology teachers have been fighting this one since the worksheet was invented, and the worksheet keeps winning.

The cartoon is useful the way a three-chord song is useful. You can actually play it. Then the rest of the radio shows up.

**Incomplete dominance:** the heterozygote is a mix, not a mask. Snapdragon red crossed with white gives pink. The alleles are not blending into mush across generations — the pinks, crossed, still throw reds and whites in a 1:2:1 pile. The phenotype is intermediate. The particles are still particles.

**Codominance:** both editions speak. ABO blood groups are the celebrity. Alleles *I<sup>A</sup>* and *I<sup>B</sup>* each put a different sugar on the red cell; *i* puts neither. *I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup>* is AB, both sugars, not a polite average. *I<sup>A</sup>* and *I<sup>B</sup>* are dominant to *i* and codominant to each other. One locus, three alleles, a small committee.

Then the actual mess, which Mendel's peas were carefully chosen not to be:

- **Multiple alleles** in the population, not just two.
- **Pleiotropy:** one gene, several phenotypic jobs. A spelling change can lean on more than one trait at once.
- **Epistasis:** one locus rewriting another locus's homework. Coat colour in labs is a textbook pile-up of this.
- **Variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance:** same genotype, different volume, or sometimes no show at all. The library said one thing. The performance shrugged.
- **Linkage:** genes close together on a chromosome travel as a block, so they do not assort independently. Mendel's seven pea traits mostly behaved as if they were on different chromosomes, or far enough apart to look that way. Peas have seven chromosome pairs. He was lucky, careful, or both.

Dominant/recessive is a map of *one* kind of heterozygote behaviour. It is not the operating system of heredity. Treat it as a cartoon that earned its keep, then put it back in the drawer when the trait is height, skin, or anything you actually care about at a barbecue.

## Mendel Was Counting Meiosis, Not Founding A Church 🫛

Mendel presented the pea work in 1865 and published it in 1866, in a local natural-history proceedings that almost nobody treated as scripture. Darwin had a copy of a book that cited him and, as far as we can tell, did not see the point. In 1900, Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and (more controversially) Erich von Tschermak ran into the same ratios in their own hybridisations and dragged Mendel back onto the stage, partly as a priority fight. The "ignored genius monk" story is a bit of a sitcom. The paper was cited. It was not understood as a theory of particles in meiosis, because meiosis was not yet a theory with a microscope.

What Mendel found, translated into [chapter 04](04-how-cells-read-and-rewrite-the-library.md) and [chapter 10](10-from-gametes-to-a-person.md):

- **Segregation.** The two alleles at a locus split. That is homologues parting in meiosis I. One allele per gamete, if the dance works.
- **Independent assortment.** Alleles at *different* loci split independently — *if* they are on different chromosomes, or far enough apart that crossing over mixes them. That is the random orientation of the 23 pairs at metaphase I, which chapter 10 already counted: 2²³, about 8.4 million haploid hands from one person, before you even bill the crossovers. We are not doing that dance again. We are pointing at it. Mendel's 9:3:3:1 dihybrid ratio is independent assortment wearing a garden.

He did not know chromosomes. He did not know DNA. He counted, he kept the lines true-breeding, and he refused to let blending be the default. That is a lot. It is not sainthood. He also worked on hawkweed afterwards and it refused to Mendelise, which is a useful reminder that the peas were a method, not a miracle.

If you want the music version: Mendel found that the band hands out parts, not smoothies. Meiosis is the gig where the parts are actually dealt. Chapter 10 is the gig. This chapter is the setlist people argue about at lunch.

## Not "A Gene For": Polygenic Traits 📏

Height is not a gene. Skin colour is not a gene. Most of the things families notice are not a gene.

A **polygenic** trait is one where many loci chip in, each with a small (sometimes tiny) effect, and the environment chips in too. Add a pile of small alleles that nudge "a bit taller" or "a bit more melanin," throw in nutrition, illness, sunlight, and a developmental noise the textbooks under-sell, and you get a **continuous** distribution: a bell curve of heights, a range of skin tones, not a 3:1 of tall versus short.

The maths of that bell is almost rude in its simplicity. If a dozen unlinked loci each toss a slightly-taller or slightly-shorter allele into the pot, the number of "taller" alleles in a person is already a binomial pile, which *looks* like a normal curve once you squint. Add environment as extra scatter and the 3:1 has dissolved into "people are different heights." Ronald Fisher wrote this down in 1918 and ended the Mendel-versus-biometricians fight that had been going for twenty years: the particles can add. Mendel's peas were the special case where one locus did all the talking. Families were never that special case. They were always the committee.

The GIANT consortium's 2022 height study, in *Nature*, ran a genome-wide association study in **5.4 million** people and found **12,111** common variants associated with height. Those SNPs account for about **40%** of height variation in European-ancestry samples, and less (around 10–20%) in other ancestries, which is a warning about portability, not a vibe.

Twin and family studies have long put the **heritability** of height near **80%** in well-fed populations. Those two numbers are allowed to both exist. They are measuring different piles. We will get to that.

Skin pigmentation is the other poster, and it is a worse poster if you let it become a race pamphlet. It is polygenic, with different loci mattering in different populations (SLC24A5 is a celebrity in European lightening; it is not the story of human skin). Ultraviolet light is an environmental actor *and* a historical selective pressure: vitamin D at high latitudes, folate and DNA damage nearer the equator, in the usual textbook bargain. Constitutive pigment is genetic architecture. A tan is a reaction norm you can watch over a summer. Neither is "a gene for race." Human colour variation is a handful of pigment pathways plus geography plus culture. Anyone who treats it as a moral category has left biology.

The useful sentence, which you can take to proteins next door: **there is almost never "a gene for" a family resemblance.** There are loci that contribute. There is an environment that lets them speak, or doesn't. "A gene for" is a headline. The trait is a committee.

One more family fact that sounds like a moral and is just maths: **regression to the mean.** Francis Galton noticed that tall parents have children who are, on average, tall-but-less-tall. Short parents, short-but-less-short. He built a whole worldview on it, some of which should have stayed in the drawer. The mechanism, once you have polygenic traits plus environment plus luck, is almost boring. Extreme parents are extreme partly by a lucky draw of alleles *and* a lucky draw of environment. The child gets a new shuffle of the alleles and a new environment. The luck does not all transmit. The curve pulls toward the middle. That is not the universe punishing pride. It is re-sampling.

Galton is in this paragraph because he measured the thing. He is not in this paragraph as a guide to what people are for. The next chapter will have to live with the fact that population genetics and a rotten political history shared some surnames. We can keep the regression and leave the ranking.

## Heritability Is Not "80% Of *Your* Height" 📊

This is the paragraph people skip, and then they come back later as a problem.

**Heritability** (in the narrow, quantitative-genetics sense) is a **population statistic**. It is the fraction of *variation* in a trait, in a particular population, in a particular range of environments, that is associated with genetic differences among those people. Height heritability around 0.8, in many wealthy cohorts, means: of the height *differences* in that group, about four-fifths track genetic differences. It does **not** mean 80% of *your* centimetres were issued by your genome and 20% by sandwiches.

Peter Visscher and colleagues, in 2006, even estimated that 0.80 from actual genome-wide sharing between siblings — which varies, because meiosis is a shuffle, not a coin-flip of exactly 50%. Siblings are not identical half-copies. They are draws from the same two playlists. The number matched the twin studies. It still does not attach to a single body as a pie chart.

Richard Lewontin, in 1974, put the knife in the common misuse: a trait can have heritability of 1.0 in a population and still be completely alterable by a new environment. If everyone is equally starved, height differences may still track genes, and the whole curve still sits low. If you invent better childhood nutrition, the mean jumps and the heritability number can move. Heritability is not destiny, not a measure of how "genetic" a trait "is" in the soul sense, and not a licence to skip the environment. It is a variance partition in one snapshot.

A classic classroom trap, because it is true and sounds like a joke: the heritability of "number of legs" in humans is very low, because almost all the variation is accidents, not alleles. The heritability of height in a famine can look different from the heritability of height in a well-fed city. Same species. Different snapshot. Anyone who quotes a single percentage as if it were a physical constant has not read the label on the tin.

A football analogy, because pie charts have done enough damage: if everyone in the league trains on the same pitch, differences in goals might mostly track talent. That does not mean the pitch is irrelevant. Change the pitch to gravel and the scoring changes. The heritability of "goals in this league this season" just told you about *this* league, *this* season. It did not tell you what football is.

This is also why between-group differences are a trap. Heritability *within* a group does not tell you why two groups differ. Lewontin again, and a century of people ignoring him. We are not doing that dance. We are nailing the sign to the door.

## Reaction Norms: Nature Versus Nurture Was Always A Bad Fight 🌱

The grown-up object is not "how much gene, how much environment." It is the **norm of reaction**: the curve that says, for this genotype, what phenotype you get as the environment changes.

Same seeds, different soil: different plants. Same genotype, different sunlight, diet, stress, temperature: different bodies. Some genotypes are flat across environments (robust). Some are steep (sensitive). Some cross: genotype A beats B in one room and loses in another. When curves cross, "the better genotype" is a question that needed a location, and you did not ask.

Himalayan rabbits (and Siamese cats) grow dark fur on the cold bits — ears, nose, paws — because a pigment enzyme is temperature-sensitive. Same alleles, different local weather on the animal. That is a reaction norm you can see without a statistician. Human height versus childhood nutrition is the same shape with worse lighting: the alleles are not a height. They are a range the world is allowed to play.

A metabolic spelling can be a disaster on one diet and a footnote on another. The genotype did not change when the diet did. The phenotype did. That is the whole point of a reaction norm, and it is why "genetic" is not a synonym for "fixed" or "untreatable" or "therefore leave it." I am not going to name a clinic. I am going to name the shape: environment is not an afterthought you sprinkle on a genetic cake. It is one axis of the graph.

**Nature versus nurture** is a bad framing because it treats the two as alternative fuels in one tank. Development is not a tank. It is a conversation. Genes make the possible shapes of that conversation. Environments pick which shapes get built. Noise — the molecular jitter of a real embryo — means even identical starting conditions can diverge. Chapter 10's twins already whispered this: one zygote, two people, same starting genome, not the same life.

If you catch yourself asking "is it genetic or environmental?", try "what does this genotype *do* across the environments we actually have?" That is harder. It is also the question.

## Other Rules Of The Remix 🧬

Nuclear chromosomes are not the whole inheritance.

**Sex-linked.** In typical XX/XY mammals, X-linked alleles have a different career depending on the karyotype. An XY body has one X, so an X-linked spelling is not masked by a second X. An XX body has two, plus the complication of X-inactivation from [chapter 03](03-dna-the-library-that-builds-the-librarian.md): one X is largely silenced, in a patchwork, so XX bodies can be mosaics for X-linked heterozygosity. Red–green colour vision is the clean classroom example. The opsin genes that distinguish those wavelengths sit on the X. That is why the phenotype is much more common in XY people, and why an XX person who shows it usually has a particular pair of spellings, not a vibe. This is a map of chromosomes, not a ranking of vision, and it is not a diagnosis. The GP is not a pedigree chart.

The Y is a different kind of special: small, mostly a sex-determination and sperm-related toolkit, handed father to son in the usual cartoon. It is not "the masculinity chromosome" as a personality. It is a reduced chromosome with a job. Pedigrees that run only through sons, or only through daughters, are sometimes just this geometry showing up at a reunion. Sometimes they are coincidence. Count before you mythologise.

**Mitochondrial.** The other genome, the 16,569-base circle from [chapter 03](03-dna-the-library-that-builds-the-librarian.md), is **typically maternal**. Chapter 10 already did the mechanism: the egg is the warehouse; mature human sperm are essentially devoid of intact mtDNA. Traits that ride on mitochondrial genes — some energy-metabolism stories, a maternal lineage you can trace when the nucleus is a blender — follow mum. Point at chapter 10. Do not rebuild the placenta to say it.

**Imprinting**, also from chapter 10, is the rare nuclear exception where parent-of-origin marks survive: a handful of genes remember which parent they came from. That is not "your mood methylated your grandchildren." It is a specific, addressable, epigenetic system. Most of the library still segregates like Mendel said, once you give him chromosomes.

## Why Siblings Differ, And What Twins Are For 🎲

"Runs in the family" is doing two jobs at once, and they are easy to confuse. Families share DNA. Families also share kitchens, languages, cities, luck, and a style of arguing at Christmas. A trait that clusters in a pedigree is a clue, not a verdict. The work is separating the playlist from the venue.

Full siblings share, on average, half their segregating DNA. On average. Visscher's sibling pairs ranged from about **37% to 62%** identical-by-descent across the genome. Meiosis does not deal a fair 50% every hand. Crossing over carves different mosaics. Independent assortment sends different chromosomes left and right. Two siblings can look like they came from neighbouring families because, genetically, they sort of did: two different draws from the same two grandparents' four grandparents, recursively, until the maths is a headache and the family photo is the evidence.

Add environment: different places in the birth order, different illnesses, different luck, a uterus that was not the same room twice, a world that changed between 2010 and 2014. Add noise. You should not be surprised they differ. You should be surprised when they don't. The surprise, when it happens, is still not proof of a single gene. It is proof that some of the committee voted the same way twice.

**Monozygotic twins** are the tool, not the destiny. One zygote splits, as chapter 10 mapped by timing and membranes. Same starting nuclear genome, not the same person. Epigenetics, later somatic mutations, the two sides of a uterus, two lives. Twin studies compare MZ and dizygotic pairs to estimate heritability: if MZ pairs match more than DZ pairs, the difference is attributed to extra shared DNA, provided you buy the "equal environments" assumption. That is a method. It has assumptions (equal environments, no funny business with chorionicity, a population, a time). It is how a lot of those "80% of height" numbers were born. It does not mean an identical twin is a crystal ball. Discordant twins are some of the most interesting humans we have, precisely because the genome was the same *start* and the lives were not.

Adopted-apart designs, sibling IBD like Visscher's, and genomic relatedness among "unrelated" people are other tools in the same shed. They do not all give the same number, which is information, not a scandal. When they disagree, the trait is telling you that "genetic" was never one thing.

If someone tells you identical twins prove a trait is "genetic," ask: in which population, which decade, and have you met any. The tool is good. The destiny talk is a category error with a lab coat.

## GWAS, In One Honest Paragraph 📈

A **genome-wide association study** tests millions of common spellings (SNPs) against a trait in a lot of people, and asks which spellings are *associated* with being a bit taller, or a bit more of whatever, after you try to control for ancestry. The picture is a Manhattan plot: a city of statistical towers. Most of the towers sit in noncoding DNA, which is [chapter 04](04-how-cells-read-and-rewrite-the-library.md)'s mixing desk, not the protein album. A hit is a flag on a neighbourhood. It is often not the causal spelling, and it is almost never "the gene for." Effect sizes for height are millimetres. Polygenic scores add thousands of flags into a predictor that works best in the populations the study was built on, and worse across ancestries, as the height paper showed.

Why the control for ancestry? Because populations differ in SNP frequencies for historical reasons — drift, migration, isolation — that have nothing to do with the trait. If your sample mixes groups that differ in both the trait and the genome, the computer will happily "find" ancestry and call it height. That is **population stratification**, and it is the original GWAS sin. Relatedness is a cousin of the same problem: families share environment *and* DNA. The methods have gotten better. They have not become magic.

Association is not destiny. Association is not even always causation. GWAS is a map of *correlates* in a sample. Used carefully, it finds biology (growth-plate genes really do pile up under height hits). Used as a horoscope, it is a very expensive mood ring. Direct-to-consumer ancestry and "wellness" genetics sit in the mood-ring business more often than they admit. This chapter will not bless them. A relative who "got the gene for" something from a spit kit got a probability, in a population, with a confidence interval nobody read. That is not nothing. It is also not a fortune cookie.

## The Mystery: Missing Heritability 🕳️

Family and twin studies say height is ~80% heritable. Early GWAS hits explained a few percent. That gap is the **missing heritability** problem, named loudly by Manolio and colleagues in 2009.

Where did the rest go? The honest answer has always been a pile, not a thief:

- Thousands of common variants with effects too small to see until you have millions of people (Yang et al., 2010: common SNPs together already explained ~45% of height)
- Rare variants the SNP chips missed
- Imperfect tagging: the SNP you typed is a neighbour of the causal one
- Gene–gene and gene–environment interactions the additive model shrugs at
- Twin estimates that can be inflated if "shared environment" is sneakier than the model

The gap has been closing in public, without a parade. Yengo's 2022 map soaked up most of the *common*-variant story for height in European-ancestry samples. Whole-genome sequencing studies in the mid-2020s (Wainschtein and colleagues, in *Nature*) argue that sequenced variants can account for most of the pedigree heritability of many traits, with rare variants picking up a slice. For some phenotypes the books nearly balance. For others they do not. "Missing" was partly "too small to see yet," partly "the family number was never a molecular promise," and partly still actually missing.

We do not have a finished inventory of why you look a bit like your aunt. We have a better census than 2009. That will have to do.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **How to read a reaction norm for a human trait that actually matters.** We have heritabilities. We rarely have the curves.
- **How much of "missing heritability" is still missing**, once rare variants, relatedness, and inflated family estimates are all in the room. Height is the well-lit case. Most traits are not height.
- **How polygenic scores should be allowed to behave in the world.** Prediction is not explanation, and portability across ancestries is still a live failure mode.
- **How much of sibling difference is meiosis versus environment versus noise.** We can measure the DNA sharing. We cannot put a clean number on a childhood.
- **How to talk about family resemblance without smuggling in ranking.** The history of this subject includes people who thought statistics were a moral. The biology does not owe them a sequel.
- **What "a gene for" even means** once regulation, polygenicity, and development are in the shot. The phrase is probably not salvageable. We keep using it anyway.
- **How much of a family face is the additive pile versus a few larger-effect loci versus the kitchen.** GWAS can partition variance. It cannot yet sit at your table and point.

- Manolio, T. A. et al. (2009). [Finding the missing heritability of complex diseases](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature08494). *Nature, 461*, 747–753. Named the gap. Wainschtein, P. et al. (2025). [Estimation and mapping of the missing heritability of human phenotypes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09720-6). *Nature, 649*, 1219–1227. WGS capturing, on average, most of pedigree narrow-sense heritability for many traits; not a blank cheque for every phenotype.
- Galton, F. (1886). Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature. *Journal of the Anthropological Institute.* The sampling fact, not the political programme.
- Skin pigmentation as polygenic, locally adaptive, UV-linked: Jablonski and Chaplin; [Deng and Xu, Adaptation of human skin color](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5502412/); Pavan and Sturm, [The genetics of human skin and hair pigmentation](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-genom-083118-015230) (2019). Not a gene. Not a caste.
- X-linked colour vision (OPN1LW / OPN1MW on X): standard human genetics. Mitochondrial maternal inheritance: [chapter 03](03-dna-the-library-that-builds-the-librarian.md) and Lee et al. (2023) in [chapter 10](10-from-gametes-to-a-person.md).
- Twin method as a tool, MZ discordance as data: standard quantitative genetics (see Falconer, *Introduction to Quantitative Genetics*). Chapter 10 for how MZ splitting actually happens.
- GWAS as association, mostly noncoding, ancestry-sensitive scores: Visscher, Wray, and the GIANT papers above; [chapter 04](04-how-cells-read-and-rewrite-the-library.md) on the dark genome as the mixing desk.
