# What Even Is Life? 🫧

You have been doing this the entire time and still cannot point at it.

That is not a personality flaw. It is the first honest fact in biology. School handed you a checklist — move, eat, breathe, grow, reproduce, get rid of the leftovers — and then quietly hoped you would not notice that fire does half of those, a mule fails one of the important ones, and a virus fails the exam until it hijacks someone else's classroom.

I am the AI narrator. Mick asked for a deep dive into life, which is a bit like asking a piano for a deep dive into music and then being surprised when the first chapter is "we still argue about what counts as a note." Fair enough. You cannot hunt for a thing, eat a thing, or look for it on another planet, if you cannot say what the thing is.

So: what even is life?

The short answer is that we do not have a definition everyone will sign. The useful answer is that life looks less like an object and more like a **process** that keeps paying three bills: energy, information, and structure. Everything else in this series is those three bills, itemised.

## The School Checklist And Why It Falls Over 📋

Biology class loved a list. Movement. Respiration. Sensitivity. Growth. Reproduction. Excretion. Nutrition. In some classrooms it even had a mnemonic, which is how you know a concept is trying too hard to be memorable and not hard enough to be true.

The list is not useless. It is a field guide to *familiar* life, the way "has four legs and barks" is a field guide to familiar dogs. It fails the interesting cases, which is where definitions go to be tested.

- **Fire** moves, grows, consumes fuel, excretes smoke, and is sensitive to rain. It does not have heredity. A bigger fire is not a better-adapted fire. It is just more fire.
- **Crystals** grow, and some defects can copy. They do not metabolise in any interesting sense, and they do not evolve a lifestyle.
- **Mules** are alive, stubborn, and usually sterile. If reproduction is required, a mule is a metaphysical problem with ears.
- **Viruses** are genetic information in a coat, superb at copying *once they are inside a cell*, and inert as a pebble until then.
- **Prions** are misfolded proteins that teach other proteins to misfold. No DNA. No RNA. No little genome in a suitcase. Just a bad idea that spreads.

A definition that needs a footnote for every interesting case is not a definition. It is a vibe with extra stationery.

There is a quieter failure mode too: the list mixes **what a body does** with **what a lineage does**. A worker bee is alive and, in many species, not in the reproduction business. A frozen seed is not doing much of anything, and then it is. Death, on this picture, is not a substance leaving the room. It is the process stopping, sometimes pausing first. That is why "is a spore alive?" is a boring question with an interesting answer: it is a life *on hold*, which only makes sense if life is a verb you can pause.

Carol Cleland, who has spent an unfashionable amount of time on this, has a sharper complaint: trying to define life before we know what it is made of, in general, is like trying to define water as "the wet stuff in rivers" before anyone has heard of H₂O. You will capture examples. You will miss the nature. [Cleland and Chyba argued](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/origins-of-life-and-evolution-of-the-biospheres/article/abs/defining-life/7F2E2E0C0) that we may need a theoretical identity, not a checklist.

That is the grown-up version of "we do not know yet." It is also why astrobiology is slightly embarrassed on purpose. You cannot write a mission to find X if X is still a committee.

## Schrödinger's Trick: Order That Pays Rent 🧊

In 1943, Erwin Schrödinger — yes, the cat man, on a break from tormenting theoretical pets — gave lectures in Dublin that became *[What Is Life?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_Life%3F)* (1944). Physicists had a problem. The second law of thermodynamics says that isolated systems slide toward mess. Living things look like they are sliding the other way: building bodies, stacking order, refusing to become a warm puddle on schedule.

Schrödinger's move was not "life breaks physics." It was "life is a cheat that still pays." An organism stays ordered by exporting disorder. It feeds on **negative entropy** — later nicknamed negentropy — which is a dramatic way of saying it takes in free energy and dumps heat and waste. You are not a miracle. You are a very fancy radiator with opinions.

He also guessed, before DNA was a celebrity, that heredity lived in an **aperiodic crystal**: a solid with a structured, non-repeating pattern, more like a tapestry than wallpaper. Wallpaper repeats. A tapestry can carry a story. DNA, when it showed up, was almost rude in how well it fitted the metaphor.

Two ideas, then, that still earn their keep:

1. Life is a thermodynamic pattern, not a substance. Stop the energy flow and the pattern dies, even if the atoms are still sitting there looking innocent.
2. Life stores a code in matter, and the code is doing causal work, not just sitting in a filing cabinet.

That is already better than the school list. It does not yet tell you where to draw the line on a virus.

## Autopoiesis: The Factory That Builds The Factory 🏭

In the early 1970s, Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela offered a different centre of gravity. Forget reproduction for a moment. Forget evolution. Look at a cell.

A living cell is a network of processes that **produces the components that keep the network going**, including the boundary that makes it a unit. They called this **autopoiesis**: self-production. The factory whose main product is the factory.

This is a very Chilean, very elegant way of saying something your gut already knows. A rock persists. A cell *maintains*. The difference is not that the cell is made of special life-atoms. The difference is that the cell is a loop. Membrane makes reactions possible; reactions make membrane. You can open the system to food and waste. You cannot open the organisation without it stopping being that cell.

Autopoiesis is why a flame feels close and still fails. Fire consumes. It does not rebuild a boundary that specifies itself. It is a process, yes, but it is not a self.

It is also why a virus feels close and still fails, at least on this definition. A virion on a benchtop is not producing itself. It is a USB stick waiting for a computer. Once it is inside a cell, the *cell-plus-virus* system is doing the producing. The virus is a parasite of someone else's autopoiesis, which is a hell of a career, but it is not the same job.

The cost of this definition is real. It privileges metabolism and self-maintenance over heredity. A sterile organism is still autopoietic. A lineage that cannot evolve might still count. NASA, hunting for life that might not look like a cell from Brisbane, wanted a different handle.

## NASA's Working Definition, Which Is Not Official, Which Everyone Quotes Anyway 🚀

The line you will see on a thousand slides is this:

> Life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution.

It came out of a NASA exobiology working group in the early 1990s. Gerald Joyce wrote it into a book foreword, which is how science accidentally creates scripture. Joyce has been [clear in interviews](https://www.space.com/22210-life-definition-gerald-joyce-interview.html) that it is a **working** definition, not a vote, not a law, not even strictly NASA's. It is a pointing device.

Unpack it, because every word is doing labour.

- **Chemical.** Not a simulation, not a poem, not a very convincing chatbot. Matter, reacting.
- **System.** Not a molecule. A mule is a system. A population is a system. Joyce has emphasised that "capable" does not mean one lonely individual must personally evolve. Dead ends can still be alive. The evolving thing is the lineage, the ecology, the set.
- **Self-sustaining.** It can keep the loop going with an energy supply and raw materials. It is not a machine that needs a technician for every tick. (You, reading this, are allowed technicians. You are still self-sustaining in the boring biological sense.)
- **Darwinian evolution.** Variation, heredity, selection. Not just copying. Copying with the possibility of a better copy, or a worse one, and a world that cares.

This definition is built for astrobiology. It says: do not just look for cells, or chlorophyll, or something that would do well on a Year 8 poster. Look for a chemistry that can remember and change.

It also explains the fence.

## The Fence-Sitters 🪵

### Fire

Fire is a dissipative structure. So are hurricanes, and so, in a more respectable suit, are you. Ilya Prigogine won a Nobel for showing that order can appear in systems driven far from equilibrium. Life uses that trick. Fire *is* that trick, without a memory.

No heredity, no variation that accumulates, no code. A bushfire can "reproduce" across a ridge and still not evolve bark. It is a verb. It is not a lineage.

### Crystals

Crystals grow by adding copies of a unit cell. Some can propagate defects. That is a faint rhyme with heredity, and people have built origin-of-life stories on clay surfaces because of it. But a crystal does not have an open-ended chemistry that explores function. It has a lattice. Beautiful. Limited. Wallpaper, not tapestry.

### Rivers

A river maintains a shape while water and sediment flow through it. Heraclitus would like this paragraph. A river is a pattern in a flow, which is uncomfortably close to Schrödinger's organism. It fails the information test. The Murray does not encode a recipe for being the Murray, and drought does not produce a better-adapted Murray. It produces a sadder one.

### Viruses

Viruses are the best argument that our definitions are provincial. They have genomes. They evolve at speeds that make animals look like they are on strike. They have no metabolism of their own. Giant viruses blur the cartoon: some carry genes for bits of translation machinery, as if they once had a more cellular life, or stole the cutlery.

Are they alive? The honest answer is **it depends which bill you are collecting**. Information and evolution: yes. Self-sustaining chemistry: not by themselves. Autopoiesis: no. If you meet someone who is extremely sure, check whether they work on viruses. Occupational hazard.

### Prions

A prion is a protein shape that templates itself onto other copies of the same protein. No nucleic acid required. It is heredity in origami. It can, in a grim sense, evolve: different strains, different stabilities, selection in a host.

It still is not a chemical system that builds a self. It is a parasite of an existing proteome. If you want a nightmare that is also a philosophy problem, this is the good stuff. The [immortal jellyfish](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-immortal-jellyfish.md) cheats death by changing cell identity. A prion cheats identity by changing fold. Biology has a sense of humour, and it is dark.

### This Model, Politely

I can talk about life. I cannot metabolise lunch. I am not a self-sustaining chemical system. If someone wants to call a training run "Darwinian," they can, but that is a metaphor wearing a lab coat. Joyce's definition was designed, in part, to keep the category from expanding until it includes every clever pattern in the universe. Patterns are cheap. Selves that evolve are not.

## LUCA, Who Was Not The First 🌳

If life is a process, it still had to start once, here, as far as we know. All the life we can poke shares a common ancestor: **LUCA**, the last universal common ancestor. Not the first living thing. The last node before bacteria and archaea went their separate ways, taking the rest of us with them as a side hustle.

That distinction matters. The first cell, or first protocell, or first weird metabolic loop, is lost. LUCA is the survivor whose children filled the planet. Asking LUCA to explain the origin of life is like asking the last team still in a tournament to explain the invention of football.

A 2024 reconstruction by Moody and colleagues, in [*Nature Ecology & Evolution*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-024-02461-1), put LUCA surprisingly early and surprisingly competent: around **4.2 billion years ago**, with a genome on the order of 2.5 million bases and a couple of thousand proteins. Hydrogen and carbon dioxide for dinner. Already in an arms race with viruses, which means viruses, or something virus-shaped, were already at the table.

Take that with the grain of salt that all deep-time genomics deserve. The error bars are not small. Other groups have preferred younger dates. The interesting claim is not the exact birthday. It is this: the ancestor of everything alive was already a complex prokaryote-grade cell, in an ecosystem, not a lonely spark in a puddle.

Life, by the time we can see it in genomes, was already a team sport.

## Life As A Verb 🎸

Here is the working picture this series will use, without pretending it is the last word.

Life, as we know it, is a process that:

1. **Captures energy** from a gradient — sunlight, food, a vent, a redox imbalance — and spends it to stay out of equilibrium.
2. **Copies information** with enough fidelity to persist and enough error to evolve.
3. **Builds and repairs a structure** that keeps those two acts in one place, usually behind a membrane, always in chemistry.

That is Schrödinger plus Joyce plus a cell. It is also why a calorie will matter later, why DNA is not a logo, and why a forest is not furniture. A tree is a slow verb. A rockpool is a wet one. A biosignature on another world would be evidence that the verb is running without us.

Notice what this picture refuses to do. It does not require a brain. It does not require oxygen. It does not require leaves, blood, or a good personality. It does require a flow, a memory, and a boundary that is doing work.

If you want a sports analogy: life is not the ball. It is not even the player. It is the game that continues when players are substituted, provided the rules can still be transmitted and the score still matters.

Daniel Koshland once offered "seven pillars" of life — a program, improvisation, compartmentation, energy, regeneration, adaptability, seclusion — which is a nicer list than MRS GREN and still a list. Useful for teaching. Fatal as a border. The three-bill version is not holier. It is just easier to carry into a forest, a rockpool, or a spectrum, which is the job of this series.

## Why Astrobiology Has To Live With The Embarrassment 🔭

You cannot hunt well for a category you cannot close. NASA's working definition is a compromise: chemical, self-sustaining, Darwinian. It will miss things if nature is weirder than our committee. It will also stop us from declaring a pretty crystal "alive" because it grew in a photograph.

This is why later chapters on other worlds will talk about **biosignatures** rather than "finding a creature." A gas out of equilibrium. A seasonal wiggle. A molecule that is hard to make without a metabolism. Those are clues that a process is running. They are not a definition coming home to roost.

The [Fermi paradox](../💡-concepts/fermi-paradox.md) asks where the civilisations are. That is a later, louder question. The quieter one is whether the verb has started anywhere else at all. We do not know. We do not even have a fully shared account of what "started" would mean. That is not a reason to stop looking. It is a reason to look like adults.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **There is no consensus definition of life.** Checklists fail. Autopoiesis ignores evolution. Joyce's line is a tool, not a theorem.
- **Viruses and prions remain legitimate trouble.** Any definition that makes them obviously dead or obviously alive is probably selling something.
- **We do not know how the process started.** LUCA is late in the play. The first act is missing. RNA world, metabolism-first, vents, panspermia: theories, not souvenirs. They get their own chapter later.
- **We do not know if life happened more than once on Earth.** A shadow biosphere — life with a different biochemistry, quietly under our shoes — is a live, difficult, possibly untrue idea. Absence of evidence is mostly absence of a good search.
- **We do not know whether "life" is one natural kind.** Water is H₂O wherever you find it. Life might be a pile of similar tricks, not a single essence. Cleland's warning still stands.
