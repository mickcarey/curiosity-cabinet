# Soil Is Alive 🪱

Dirt is what you call soil when you have not been introduced.

A geologist sees weathered rock. A gardener sees a medium. A microbe sees a city. All three are right, and the third is the one school skipped. Soil is not the brown stuff that inconveniences a shoe. It is a living, structured, chemically opinionated matrix: mineral particles, water films, air pockets, organic matter in every stage of becoming someone else's lunch, and a microbial city so dense that a teaspoon can hold more bacteria than there are people. It is the slow verb from [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md) running in the dark. It is also, if you are a plant, half the workplace.

You have been walking on an unread library and calling it mud. This chapter is the introduction. After this, "dirt" should feel like an insult.

If [chapter 13](13-plants-are-not-furniture.md) was the solar panel, this is the battery, the wiring, and the unlisted workforce. Skip it and the landscape method in the next chapter becomes gardening advice in a nice shirt.

This chapter is not a fertiliser catalogue and not a soil-taxonomy exam. It is the minimum kit: what you are standing on, why it holds nutrients or refuses to, and why most of the workforce has never been introduced.

## Texture: The Grain Of The Argument ⏳

**Texture** is the mineral particle sizes, the recipe of the inorganic half.

- **Sand**: 0.05–2 mm. Gritty. Big pores. Water falls through like a rumour. Low surface area, low cling.
- **Silt**: 0.002–0.05 mm. Silky. Holds water better than sand, still drains. The Goldilocks middle that becomes dust when you farm it badly.
- **Clay**: smaller than 0.002 mm. Plate-shaped, electrically busy, huge surface area. Holds water and nutrients as if they owed it money. Poorly managed, it sets like a bad drum solo: all thud, no air.

The USDA triangle turns those three percentages into twelve names: sand, loam, clay loam, silty clay, the whole unromantic vocabulary. **Loam** is the agricultural sweetheart because it mixes the jobs: drainage, retention, something a root can push through. Texture is inherited from **parent material** and the way it weathered. You can add organic matter. You cannot, in a season, turn sand into clay. Texture is the key the landscape is written in. Structure is how well the band is playing it.

A field trick: wet a pinch, ribbon it between thumb and finger. Long sticky ribbon? Clay is talking. Falls apart, feels like sugar? Sand. Silky, short ribbon? Silt is in the mix. This is not a lab. It is literacy.

Texture is also a water story in disguise. Sand has large pores that empty at a look. Clay has tiny pores that hold water so tightly a plant cannot always drink it. The useful water is the bit between **field capacity** (what drains away under gravity has gone) and **wilting point** (what is left is held too tight). Loams, again, tend to have a useful middle. A plant dying in wet clay is not a contradiction. It may be drowning, or it may be unable to extract the water that is technically present, the way a bank can be full of money you cannot withdraw after 5 p.m.

## Structure: Why A Crumb Is Not A Brick 🧱

**Structure** is how those particles are glued into **aggregates** — crumbs, blocks, plates, columns — with pores between.

Pores are the point. Too few, and roots drown and anaerobes throw a denitrification party. Too many and too big, and water leaves, taking nitrate with it. A good crumb structure is a city with streets and courtyards: air, water, hyphae, roots, a springtail making a living. Organic matter, fungal hyphae, root exudates, and clay surfaces are the glue. Tillage is a wrecking ball that can still be the right tool, and is often the lazy one.

Colour is a clue, not a diagnosis. Dark often means organic carbon. Red and yellow often mean iron oxides in aerated conditions. Grey and mottled often mean waterlogging, iron reduced, a soil that has been holding its breath. Laterite, podsol, rendzina: names that are really stories about climate, rock, and time. You do not need the full taxonomy to start. You need to notice whether the ground is a sponge, a brick, or a sieve.

**Horizons** are the soil's autobiography, stacked. A rough field alphabet:

- **O**: organic litter, the inbox.
- **A**: mineral mixed with humus, dark, the living room.
- **E**: leached, often pale, the hallway someone stripped the pictures from.
- **B**: where the washed-down clay, iron, or humus accumulated, the attic of consequences.
- **C**: weathered parent, not yet a soil in the full sense.
- **R**: rock, the floor.

A podsol shouts this alphabet: bleached E, rusty B, a chemistry set in a profile. A young alluvial soil may barely have learned the letters. Compacted urban fill may have no grammar at all. Dig a small hole, if you are allowed. The side of a track cutting is a free textbook. Put the turf back. Literacy is not a licence to vandalise a roadside.

## pH: The Quiet Mood 🌡️

**pH** is the hydrogen-ion mood of the soil solution. It decides which nutrients dissolve, which microbes clock on, and whether a plant's favourite metal becomes a poison.

Most nutrients are reasonably available in a slightly acid to neutral window, roughly pH 6–7. Too acid and aluminium and manganese can become toxic, phosphorus locks to iron and aluminium, bacteria slow down, fungi care less. Too alkaline and iron, zinc, manganese, and phosphorus throw a different kind of tantrum; calcium and magnesium look smug. Australian soils, especially old, weathered, low-buffer ones, have strong opinions here. A test kit is useful. The plants are ruder and sometimes more honest: you will meet that properly in the bush companion.

pH is not a moral quality. Acid peat is a legitimate lifestyle. Alkaline coastal sand is another. The mistake is planting a calcicole in a podsol and deciding the plant is dramatic.

pH is also why a hydrangea changes colour, which is a parlour trick, and why a protea dies in a limey garden, which is a landscape lesson. The parlour trick is famous. The lesson is the one that travels.

## Organic Matter: The Battery And The Glue 🍂

**Soil organic matter** is everything carbon that was alive, in every stage of not being a leaf anymore: fresh litter, the goo of decomposition, **humus** that hangs around, living roots, hyphae, the microbes themselves.

It is a battery (nutrients mineralised on a schedule), glue (aggregates), a sponge (water), and a cafeteria. A few percent by mass is a lot of function. Sandy soils feel organic matter like a guitar feels strings: the instrument exists without them, and is not, in any useful sense, the instrument.

Humus is not a mystical essence. It is a messy, partly decomposed, chemically stubborn pool that still participates. "Build organic matter" is one of the few agricultural slogans that is also just physics plus biology. Leave the carbon in. Stop setting fire to the capital unless fire is the native accountant, which, in Australia, it often is.

## CEC: The Quiet Politics Of Charge ⚡

**Cation exchange capacity** is the soil's ability to hold onto positively charged nutrients — Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺, NH₄⁺, H⁺ — and swap them with the soil solution as plants and microbes ask.

Clay particles and organic matter are the charged surfaces. Sandy soils have low CEC (a few cmol(+)/kg). Clays and high-organic soils have high CEC. Low CEC means nutrients leach if you look at them wrong; fertiliser is a pulse, not a savings account. High CEC means the soil can bank cations, and also that you can take longer to change its mind.

This is the "quiet politics" the expedition map promised. Ions compete. Sodium in the wrong amount disperses clays and ruins structure — **sodicity**, which is not the same as **salinity**. Salty soil is a water-potential problem: plants cannot drink. Sodic soil is a structure problem: the clay deflocculates, the pores collapse, the rain sits on top or cuts a gully. Calcium is often the diplomat (gypsum as a field trick). Aluminium at low pH occupies exchange sites like a bad tenant. You do not need to become a soil chemist. You need to know that "fertile" is partly "has surfaces that keep the good ions from washing to the river."

**Compaction** is the physical version of the same politics. A plough pan, a cattle track, a car park: bulk density up, pores down, roots sideways, denitrification in the wet bits. You can feel it with a probe or a boot. Texture you inherit. Structure and compaction you can ruin this season.

Anion exchange exists too, and phosphorus' whole personality is about not being a well-behaved dissolved ion. We will get there.

## Jenny's Factors: Soil As A Function, Not A Mess 🧮

In 1941, Hans Jenny wrote soil as an equation:

**S = f(cl, o, r, p, t, …)**

**Climate, organisms, relief, parent material, time**, and then the dots: fire, dust, humans, the rest. Dokuchaev had the idea. Jenny gave it a handle, **CLORPT**, and a research program: hold four factors still, vary one, see what soil you get. A climosequence, a toposequence, a chronosequence. The world is not a lab. The idea still organises the walk.

- **Climate**: temperature and water control weathering rate, leaching, organic-matter turnover. Tropical wet is a chemical furnace. Cold dry is a pause button.
- **Organisms**: plants pump carbon below; roots mine and glue; earthworms are ploughs with opinions; microbes run the cycles; humans are, Jenny said, the outstanding biological factor, which is a polite way of saying we can wreck a profile in a decade that took twelve thousand years to build.
- **Relief**: slope, aspect, position. Water runs downhill. So does soil. A ridge is thinner and drier than the gully. South-facing and north-facing, in this hemisphere, are different rooms.
- **Parent material**: granite is not basalt is not dune sand is not last week's alluvium. The rock is the mineral budget. Basalt weathers to something generous. Quartz sand is a sieve with low CEC and a blank look.
- **Time**: young soils remember the rock. Old soils have been leached, weathered, argued with, and often impoverished in the nutrients that walk. Australia has a lot of old. That is not a personality flaw. It is a clock.

None of the five is optional. A basalt in a freezer is not a tropical clay. A granite in the wet tropics is not a desert sand. The equation is a reminder to stop treating soil as a product you can order. It is a result.

The dots matter. Fire is a soil-forming factor in this country whether the equation wanted it or not. So is a plough. So is a city.

Jenny later wrote about the "humus capital" as a national asset. He was not being poetic. He was doing accounting.

A few global soil personalities, so the walk has types and not just factors:

- **Young soils** on fresh rock or sediment: remember the parent, may be fertile, may be shallow, have not had time to be robbed.
- **Old, highly weathered soils** (laterites, some ferralsols): iron and aluminium oxides, low CEC in the mineral fraction, phosphorus locked, the rainforest above them often a closed nutrient cycle wearing a lush disguise.
- **Podsols**: acid, leached, a bleached horizon, a humus or iron pan, a flora of specialists.
- **Vertisols**: high-activity clays that shrink and swell, crack in the dry, turn to glue in the wet, a farming problem and a landscape texture you can feel in a boot.
- **Calcareous soils**: pH up, phosphorus and iron in a different argument, a flora that can live with lime.
- **Organic soils / peats**: the mineral fraction lost the vote; carbon is the profile; drain them and they vanish into the air.

Australia collected a lot of the old, the sandy, the sodic, and the fire-edited. That is the bush companion's dialect. Here they are just to say: "soil" is not one brown. It is a family.

## A Nitrogen Atom's Bad Week 🔁

Follow one N, because cycles are easier as a character.

It starts as N₂ over a paddock, smug, triple-bonded. A *Rhizobium* in a clover nodule, paid in carbon, reduces it to ammonia. The clover builds an amino acid. A cow eats the clover — or a fungus does, or the clover dies. Mineralisation: NH₄⁺ in the soil solution. A plant might grab it. An ammonia-oxidising archaeon might turn it into nitrite, a bacterium into nitrate. Rain. The nitrate, being negatively charged and unwelcome at the CEC party, leaches toward a creek. Or the soil waterlogs, a denitrifier uses the nitrate as an electron acceptor, and our atom leaves as N₂O, which is a climate problem, or as N₂, back to smug.

Haber–Bosch can insert the atom at the ammonia step, from a factory, and then the same play runs with the volume up. The atom does not care. The creek does.

If that felt like a drum fill, good. The nitrogen cycle is a fill that never ends, and the band is microbial.

## Nitrogen: The Cycle That Runs The Band 💨

[Chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) left nitrogen hanging: the atmosphere is ~78% N₂, a triple bond so smug it barely reacts, and life needs the atom in amino acids and bases anyway.

**Nitrogenase** is the microbial enzyme that breaks the sulk, with molybdenum (sometimes vanadium) as jewellery, a ferocious energy bill, and a hatred of oxygen. Free-living fixers, symbiotic *Rhizobium* in legume nodules, actinorhizal partnerships, cyanobacteria: the biosphere's legal entry points for new reactive nitrogen, plus lightning, plus the **Haber–Bosch** process, which is industrial nitrogenase with a fossil-fuel habit. Natural fixation and industrial fixation are now in the same weight class. That is not a trivia item. That is a planetary intervention.

Once nitrogen is "fixed" as ammonia or ammonium, the soil theatre begins.

- **Ammonification / mineralisation**: organic N becomes NH₄⁺ as microbes eat the dead.
- **Nitrification**: ammonia oxidisers (bacteria and archaea) make nitrite; others make nitrate. Plants can take up both NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻. Nitrate is mobile. It leaches. It also denitrifies.
- **Immobilisation**: microbes keep the N for themselves if the carbon:nitrogen ratio of the meal is high. Sawdust on a garden is a nitrogen sponge until the microbes finish the carbs.
- **Denitrification**: under low oxygen, nitrate becomes N₂ and, along the way, **N₂O**, a greenhouse gas with no sense of proportion. Wet soils, compacted soils, the wrong irrigation: nitrogen leaves as a gas.
- **Anammox** and other plots exist: anaerobic ammonia oxidation, bacteria that pair NH₄⁺ with nitrite and skip the textbook, important in oxygen-minimum zones and some soils. **Comammox** organisms do the whole nitrification in one cell, which embarrassed the two-guild story. The textbook three-step version is the tour, not the map.

About half of fertiliser nitrogen, globally, does not end up in the crop. It ends up in water, air, or someone else's algae bloom. The cycle is not broken. It is overflowing. A later landscape chapter will treat a green drain as a sentence about this paragraph.

## Phosphorus: The Bottleneck That Does Not Do Interviews 🦴

Nitrogen at least has a sky. Phosphorus has **rock**.

The primary mineral is **apatite**, calcium phosphate. Weathering releases phosphate ions, which plants want, and which soil chemistry immediately tries to hide. In acid soils, phosphate binds to iron and aluminium oxides. In alkaline soils, it precipitates with calcium. Soil-solution phosphate is often micromolar. Inside a plant it is millimolar. That gradient is why roots spend ATP on uptake, and why a root alone is a short straw.

Phosphorus does not have a gaseous stage in any useful biological sense. It moves with dust, with erosion, with runoff, with the slow uplift of rock. Old, highly weathered landscapes — hello, large parts of Australia — are phosphorus theatres of scarcity. Young volcanic soils can be generous. Agricultural phosphorus is mined phosphate rock, which is a finite sentence.

This is why mycorrhizae exist as a lifestyle, not a curiosity. Hyphae are thinner, longer, cheaper per millimetre than roots. They get past the depletion zone a root makes around itself. They secrete organic acids and enzymes. Ectomycorrhizal fungi can weather apatite; one sterile-microcosm study found *Paxillus* on pine increasing P release from apatite nearly threefold, and the tree sending seventeen times more labelled carbon toward the apatite than toward quartz when P was scarce. That is not a web of talking trees. That is a mining contract.

Organic phosphorus is the other vault: phytate, nucleic acids, phospholipids, the leftovers of cells. Phosphatases from roots and microbes can unlock some of it. In young soils, the rock is the story. In old, humid soils, the organic pool and the oxide surfaces are the story. Lambers and colleagues have a useful cartoon: "rock-eating" versus "vegetarian" phosphorus economies along a climate and age gradient. Arid shrubland still weathers apatite. Wet old forest recycles the dead.

The bottleneck does not do interviews because it does not need to. Every leaf, every ATP, every DNA backbone from [chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) is a phosphorus story. When the rock is slow and the oxide surfaces are greedy, the fungus is the workaround.

## Mycorrhizae, Deeper Than The Wow-Piece 🍄

The [fungal network](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-fungal-network-beneath-our-feet.md) note is the documentary. This is the soil course.

Two big unions, plus specialists:

**Arbuscular mycorrhizae (AM).** Glomeromycotan fungi enter root cells and fold into arbuscules, exchange surfaces that look like they were named by a poet. Most herbs, grasses, tropical trees, a lot of everything. Excellent at scavenging inorganic phosphate from beyond the root's reach. The plant often down-regulates its own direct P uptake once the fungal path is on. Up to ~90% of a plant's P can come this way in some experiments; the number is a range, not a commandment.

**Ectomycorrhizae (ECM).** A fungal mantle around the root, a Hartig net between cells, no cell invasion. A minority of plant species — maybe 2% — and a majority of the trees that make temperate and boreal canopies look like canopies: pines, oaks, eucalypts, dipterocarps, the forest celebrities. Better at mining organic N and P, weathering rock, moving materials through extensive mycelium. The mushrooms you can eat, or not, are often the fruit of this.

Orchids cheat, or at least freelance. Ericoid mycorrhizae handle the sour, organic soils of heath. Nonmycorrhizal plants (proteaceae with cluster roots, brassicas, some others) took a different job. Banksias on sand are not failing to find a fungus. They brought their own mining kit.

The **common mycorrhizal network** — one mycelium touching more than one plant — is real as a possibility and oversold as a forest internet. Karst, Jones, and Hoeksema (2023) went through the claims: that such networks are widespread, that they boost seedlings, that mother trees preferentially succour kin. Field evidence is thin, mixed, and alternative explanations (soil pathways, not hyphal pipelines) were too often left standing. Citation culture ran ahead of the data. That is not a reason to sneer at Simard's 1997 door. It is a reason not to manage a forest as if the documentary were a methods paper.

The rest of the soil food web is not a cameo. Bacteria and archaea do the chemistry. Fungi do the filaments and a lot of the decomposition of the rude stuff (lignin, as [chapter 13](13-plants-are-not-furniture.md) said). Protozoa and nematodes graze the microbes and recycle nutrients in the classic "microbial loop" sense. Earthworms, ants, termites, mites, springtails: engineers. A teaspoon of healthy soil is a city. A teaspoon of sterilised potting mix is a suburb after the factory closed.

What you can take outside: most plants are in a carbon-for-nutrients contract with fungi. Tillage, fungicide, and fertiliser change the terms. A plant looking hungry on a high-P fertiliser regime may have sacked its fungus. A restoration without the right mycobionts is a housewarming without the plumbing. "Soil is alive" is not a bumper sticker. It is a headcount.

## The Unread Library 📚

A gram of soil can hold billions of microbial cells and thousands of species. The **great plate count anomaly** — Staley and Konopka, 1985, naming a problem everyone had — is that what you see under the microscope and what you grow on agar are not the same list. Classic slogan: **less than 1%** culturable. A modern meta-analysis put the median nearer **0.5%** of cells. Lloyd and colleagues (2018) estimated that the majority of Earth's microbial cells belong to lineages with no cultured representative; in soil, uncultured genera-to-classes were about half the 16S picture they compiled.

Metagenomics can read the books without opening the authors. It cannot yet tell you what most of them eat for a living. Auxotrophies, cross-feeding, the need for a neighbour's vitamin: some of the "unculturable" are just picky about company. Some are a different kind of metabolism than a petri dish knows how to host. The **rare biosphere** is real; so is the fact that a lot of the important work — nitrification, the slow grind of organic P, the production of the glue — is done by organisms we have never grown, named, or asked.

Soil is also a **carbon** vault, which is why climate policy has started speaking pedology with a sales accent. Living plants, dead plants, microbes, charcoal, the mineral-associated organic matter that can hang around for centuries: different pools, different clocks. "Soil carbon" is not one knob. A tillage pass, a drainage scheme, a peat fire, a restoration: different knobs. The fungal note's gigaton claims should be read with the same caution as the Wood Wide Web. The direction is right — soils hold a lot of carbon, fungi and roots are part of why — the merch is ahead of the measurement.

We are walking on an unread library. That is not a metaphor for flavour. It is the current state of soil microbiology.

If you want a sports version of this whole chapter: texture is the ground you were given. Structure is how the team is standing. CEC is the possession stats. Nitrogen is the passing game, fast and leaky. Phosphorus is the set-piece you only get a few of per match. The microbes are the players, most of them unsigned. Fire and a plough are the referee and a pitch invasion, depending on the week.

On a walk: wet a pinch. Ribbon it. Look at colour and crumbs. Notice whether water sits or vanishes. Ask what the rock was. Ask how long this surface has been a surface. Do not fertilise a heath because a vegetable garden liked it. Do not call a peat a mineral soil because it is brown. And do not, please, call it dirt once you have been introduced.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Most soil microbes have never been cultured.** Functions inferred from genes are a draft. Physiology is still mostly a rumour.
- **How much tree-to-tree transfer through common mycorrhizal networks actually matters in forests?** Partnership: yes. Internet: not proven at the scale of the story.
- **Where does the phosphorus come from in old, tight landscapes, year after year?** Organic P recycling, slow weathering, dust, fire: the mix is local and argued.
- **How do we put humans into Jenny's equation without the equation becoming a shrug?** We are a soil-forming factor with fossil fuels. The dots are doing a lot of work.
- **What is "healthy soil" when the native state is low-nutrient, fire-prone, and full of specialists?** Agricultural health and ecological fidelity are not the same exam.
- **How should we count soil carbon?** Living, dead, mineral-associated, charcoal: pools with different clocks. Policy that treats them as one knob will buy a surprise.
- **Is "soil" even a single thing?** Pedologists have been arguing about definitions for a century. A peat and a dune share a chapter. They do not share a life.
