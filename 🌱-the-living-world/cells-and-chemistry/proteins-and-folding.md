# Proteins: Shape, Movement and Molecular Work 🧩

Proteins do much of a cell's physical work. They catalyse reactions, carry substances, transmit signals, generate movement and hold structures together. Their versatility begins with a simple arrangement: a chain of amino acids whose sequence influences how it behaves in three dimensions.

The useful picture is a moving molecule in a crowded, watery environment. The colourful ribbon in a textbook is a still image of something considerably less cooperative.

## 🔤 A chain with chemical differences

Most proteins use a standard set of twenty amino acids. Each has a shared backbone and a variable **side chain**. Side chains can be charged, polar, hydrophobic, bulky or unusually flexible. Their arrangement determines which interactions the chain can make.

Amino acids are linked by peptide bonds. The peptide bond is relatively rigid, while adjacent backbone bonds allow rotation within limits imposed by neighbouring atoms. A chain therefore has many possible shapes, but not unlimited freedom.

Proteins are described at several levels:

| Level | What it describes |
| --- | --- |
| Primary | Amino-acid sequence |
| Secondary | Local structures such as alpha helices and beta sheets |
| Tertiary | The three-dimensional organisation of one chain |
| Quaternary | The assembly of several chains into a larger complex |

These are descriptions of the same molecule at different scales. A single protein may contain several independently folding **domains**, linked by flexible regions.

Chemical changes after synthesis also matter. Adding a phosphate can change activity or binding; adding sugars can affect stability and recognition; cutting a precursor can activate an enzyme. Sequence is the starting material, not the whole operational state.

## 💧 How folding happens

In water, many soluble proteins bury hydrophobic side chains and expose more water-compatible surfaces. Hydrogen bonds, electrical interactions, packing and sometimes disulfide bonds stabilise particular arrangements.

Christian Anfinsen's experiments showed that some purified proteins could regain their structure and activity after unfolding, under suitable conditions. The amino-acid sequence contained enough information for recovery in that environment.

But folding cannot involve trying every possible shape. Even a modest chain has too many possibilities. The modern **energy-landscape** picture explains how local interactions favour routes towards lower-free-energy states. There can be several routes, intermediates and traps. Folding may begin while the ribosome is still producing the chain.

Inside cells, **chaperones** help prevent inappropriate sticking and give proteins opportunities to fold or refold. They do not usually supply a separate blueprint for the final structure. They manage the conditions under which the chain finds a useful state.

## ⚙️ Why enzymes change everything

An enzyme speeds a reaction by lowering its activation barrier. It can bring reactants together, orient them, stabilise charge or favour a transition state. The enzyme is regenerated rather than consumed by the overall reaction.

Crucially, catalysis does not change the reaction's equilibrium or make an energetically unfavourable process favourable by itself. Cells often couple such processes to favourable reactions, including ATP hydrolysis.

An **active site** contains chemical groups positioned to act on a substrate. Those groups can be far apart in the sequence but close together after folding. Proteins can also change shape when molecules bind. In **allostery**, binding at one site alters behaviour at another, allowing feedback control.

These mechanisms explain why a small sequence change can have a large effect, while another change has almost none. Position and context matter more than the mere fact that a mutation occurred.

Enzymes connect this subject directly to [digestion and cellular energy](../bodies-and-energy/digestion-and-cellular-energy.md). Other proteins provide structure: collagen supports tissues, while actin and tubulin form dynamic cellular scaffolds.

## 🧶 When there is no single stable shape

Some proteins, and regions within proteins, remain **intrinsically disordered** under ordinary cellular conditions. They sample many conformations instead of adopting one stable fold.

Flexibility can be useful. A region may connect two domains, interact with several partners or fold only when it binds a target. Accessible flexible regions can also provide sites for regulatory modifications.

“Disordered” therefore does not mean broken. Function can depend on a range of shapes. This is why a structure alone rarely gives a complete explanation of what a protein does.

## 🦢 Misfolding and prions

Exposed sticky regions can lead proteins to aggregate. Cells respond through refolding systems and degradation, including the proteasome and lysosomal pathways. Maintaining this balance is called **proteostasis**.

A prion takes the problem further: a particular protein conformation promotes conversion of other copies into a similar state. Information is transmitted through shape rather than a changed DNA sequence. Mammalian prion diseases are the best-known examples, but heritable prion states also occur in fungi.

Other proteins can undergo seeded aggregation without sharing every property of infectious prions. Similar molecular behaviour is not enough to infer ordinary person-to-person transmission. The distinction matters more than the memorable phrase “contagious folding”.

## 🔍 What structure prediction can tell us

The 2021 AlphaFold2 work demonstrated a major advance in predicting protein structures from sequence. Predictions can help researchers identify folds, propose functions and design experiments.

A predicted structure still leaves important questions: how the protein moves, which partners it binds, whether a mutation changes stability, what chemical modifications matter and how the molecule behaves inside a cell. Confidence varies across a prediction, especially in flexible regions.

Solving a shape is a powerful step towards understanding a protein. It is not automatically a complete account of its chemistry. The related topics are [gene expression](../genetics-and-evolution/gene-expression-and-regulation.md), which produces the chain, and [immunity](../bodies-and-energy/immunity.md), where molecular recognition becomes especially consequential.
