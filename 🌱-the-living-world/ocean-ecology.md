# How Does the Dark Ocean Get Dinner? 🌊

Much of the ocean never receives sunlight. No sunlit leaves, no ordinary underwater vegetable patch. So what do animals down there eat?

Some wait for dinner to fall from above. Others move between depths. A few communities depend on microbes using chemical energy from the seafloor.

The sea contains many different **habitats**—places with different conditions for life—and several ways of getting fed.

## ☀️ Most of the food-making starts near the top

Sunlight fades as it passes through water. How far it reaches depends partly on how clear the water is. Cloudy coastal water may stop useful light much sooner than clear offshore water.

Near the surface, tiny drifting organisms called **phytoplankton** capture light. They use its energy to make living material from carbon dioxide and water. This is **photosynthesis**, the same broad process green plants use.

These organisms are small, but they grow and are replaced quickly. Their yearly production is enormous, even though they don't build lasting trunks like forests do.

Animals eat them, and other animals eat those animals. These connections form **food webs**. Much of the sea's food begins with something you could overlook in a glass of water.

## 🥣 Sunlight isn't the whole recipe

Phytoplankton also need nutrients, including nitrogen and phosphorus. Some regions have plenty of one ingredient but too little of another, such as iron.

Water movement can bring nutrients from deeper water towards the surface. This is **upwelling**. Mixing can also help, though it sometimes carries organisms down away from the light they need.

Warm surface water often forms a lighter layer above colder water. When the layers resist mixing, food-makers may stay in the light while running short of nutrients. Ocean productivity depends on supplies meeting in the right place.

## ❄️ It snows leftovers

Dead plankton, animal droppings and sticky clumps of material sink as **marine snow**. Microbes and animals eat much of it on the way down. Only part reaches the deep seabed.

Some animals travel upwards to feed at night and return deeper during the day. Their bodies and waste move food material between layers too.

Occasionally, something enormous arrives: a whale carcass. It can support changing communities as different parts are consumed. [Decomposition](death-and-decomposition.md) follows that unusual feast.

Sinking material also carries carbon into deeper water and sediments. How long that carbon stays away from the surface depends on where it ends up and how water circulates.

## 🔥 Dinner from chemical reactions

At **hydrothermal vents**, seawater heated by hot rock escapes through the seabed. Chemicals in the fluid can react with chemicals in surrounding water, releasing energy.

Some microbes use that energy to make living material. This is **chemosynthesis**. Animals can eat those microbes or house them as close partners.

The animals don't sit in the hottest jet. They live where fluids mix into tolerable conditions. A few centimetres can make a large difference.

These communities don't need sunlight arriving locally. But many still depend on chemicals, including oxygen, connected to processes nearer the surface. “No sunshine here” doesn't always mean “no connection to sunshine anywhere”.

## 🔦 The deep is more than dark

Pressure increases with depth. Deep-sea organisms need chemistry that works under those conditions. Oxygen supply varies too, depending on circulation and how quickly organisms consume it.

Some animals make their own light, called **bioluminescence**, for hunting, signalling or concealment. Red animals may be surprisingly hard to see where little red light remains to reflect.

A rocky underwater mountain, a muddy plain and a vent are different neighbourhoods. Calling all of them “the deep sea” is a bit like calling a desert and a rainforest “somewhere on land”.

For a version you can explore from shore, [reading a seascape](reading-a-seascape.md) starts with waves, rocks and the creatures you can actually see. The same questions apply: where are the supplies, how do they move, and who can use them?
