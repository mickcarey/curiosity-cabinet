# How Plants Make a Living 🌿

A plant obtains carbon from the air, water and minerals from its surroundings, and energy from light. It must balance all three while avoiding overheating, dehydration and being eaten.

Remaining rooted in one place does not make that passive. Plants respond through changes in growth, chemistry and transport, often on timescales that make an impatient animal miss the action.

## ☀️ Photosynthesis has two linked parts

In chloroplasts, **light reactions** use absorbed light to drive electron transfer. Water supplies electrons, oxygen is released, and the system produces ATP and NADPH. These molecules provide energy and reducing power for carbon assimilation.

The **Calvin–Benson cycle** uses them to incorporate carbon dioxide into organic molecules. Rubisco catalyses the addition of CO₂ to a five-carbon acceptor. Subsequent reactions generate carbohydrate precursors and regenerate that acceptor.

The oxygen released by oxygenic photosynthesis comes from water. The carbon incorporated into a plant comes largely from CO₂. Much of a tree's dry mass therefore arrived through its leaves, while roots supplied essential water and mineral nutrients.

Plants also respire. They use mitochondria to obtain usable energy from organic molecules during daylight as well as darkness. Photosynthesis does not exempt a plant from the need to maintain its cells overnight.

**Net primary production** is the organic material remaining after producers' own respiration. It supports growth and supplies other organisms through feeding, exudates and decomposition.

## 🍃 The carbon–water trade-off

Leaves admit CO₂ through adjustable pores called **stomata**. Water vapour escapes through the same openings. Guard cells regulate the aperture in response to light, carbon dioxide, water status and other signals.

Closing stomata can conserve water, but limits CO₂ uptake. During hot, dry conditions, that creates a difficult trade-off. Leaves also differ in thickness, angle, surface coatings and internal structure, reflecting different compromises among light capture, lifespan, water loss and defence.

Rubisco can react with oxygen instead of CO₂, initiating **photorespiration**, which consumes resources while recovering useful carbon from the products. Plants have evolved different ways to manage this:

- **C3 plants** use the Calvin cycle without a specialised C4-style concentrating system.
- **C4 plants** concentrate CO₂ around Rubisco, generally using spatial separation between initial fixation and the Calvin cycle.
- **CAM plants** commonly take up CO₂ at night and store it for daytime use, reducing water loss.

C4 and CAM involve costs and constraints. They are useful under particular conditions, not universal upgrades that other plants forgot to install.

## 🚰 Moving water without a heart

In the **xylem**, water moves through conducting cells whose functional mature forms are dead. Evaporation from leaves creates tension that draws water through connected columns. Cohesion between water molecules helps maintain those columns.

Drying increases the tension required. Air entry and cavitation can create embolisms that disrupt transport. Species differ in hydraulic efficiency, vulnerability and recovery, so drought tolerance involves internal plumbing as well as leaf appearance.

**Phloem** transports sugars and other compounds from sources to sinks. A mature leaf may be a source; a growing root, fruit or shoot may be a sink. Pressure differences drive transport in living conducting tissue. Different tubes can support different directions at the same time.

Roots provide absorption surfaces and interact with organisms in the **rhizosphere**, the soil zone strongly influenced by roots. Belowground investment varies enormously: grasslands may store much of their biomass below ground, while forests often have most biomass above it. There is no universal “half the tree is underground” rule.

## 🍄 Getting nutrients out of difficult ground

Many plants form mycorrhizal partnerships with fungi. Fungal hyphae explore soil beyond the immediate root surface and can improve access to nutrients. Plants supply carbon compounds in return.

Other strategies include nitrogen-fixing partnerships in root nodules, cluster roots that mobilise phosphorus, and carnivory in some nutrient-poor habitats. Carnivorous plants still generally obtain their carbon and energy through photosynthesis; captured animals chiefly supplement scarce nutrients.

These mechanisms make more sense when linked to [soil chemistry and nutrient cycling](soil-ecology.md). A lush canopy does not prove that the ground beneath it is rich in readily available nutrients.

## 🌱 Modular growth changes the possibilities

**Meristems** contain cells that sustain growth and generate new organs. Plants can add shoots, roots and leaves over time, with development responding to light, damage and resources.

This modular arrangement supports regrowth after some injuries and allows clonal spread through runners, rhizomes or other structures. One genetic individual can produce several apparently separate plants. Whether we count genetic individuals or independent shoots depends on the question.

Stored reserves and protected buds can support recovery after fire, but these capacities differ among species. Some depend mainly on seeds. [Fire and succession](forests/fire-water-and-succession.md) explains why “plants recover from fire” needs a species, a fire regime and a timescale attached.

## 🌸 Defence and reproduction are chemical work

Plants produce compounds that deter herbivores, inhibit microbes, attract pollinators or influence neighbours. Tannins, terpenes and alkaloids are examples of broad chemical families with many roles.

Eucalypt leaves contain complex mixtures that affect herbivore choices; scent alone does not give a complete measure of toxicity or food quality. Flowers similarly use combinations of shape, colour, scent and rewards to influence visitors. Wind-pollinated plants use a different strategy.

Seeds package an embryo with protection and resources, often allowing development to pause until conditions are suitable. Dispersal and dormancy influence where a species can establish, even when an adult plant could tolerate a site.
