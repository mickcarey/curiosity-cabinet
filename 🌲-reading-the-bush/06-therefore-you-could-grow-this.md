# Therefore You Could Grow This 🪴

This is the chapter where the walk tries to become a shopping list. It is allowed to be useful. It is not allowed to become a mood board.

If the remnant vegetation is X, the soil and water are probably Y, so these plants are *plausible*. That is the inference engine. The engine has a brake, and the brake is the whole point of the shelf: **could ≠ should.**

A suburban block in Keilor Downs is not Sherbrooke. Imported garden dreams die in that gap looking personally betrayed, which is a data set of sorts.

The bush is not a catalogue. "Could grow here" is not "the council will thank you," not "put it in the nature strip," not "replace the remnant with a tidier version of itself," and definitely not "forage it." Dinner is still a supermarket problem.

## The Inference Engine 🔧

You have been collecting cards.

- Climate and rock ([01](01-the-climate-and-the-rock.md))
- Soil you can see ([02](02-soils-you-can-see.md))
- Eucalypt type ([03](03-eucalypts-as-a-reading-key.md))
- Supporting cast ([04](04-the-supporting-cast.md))
- Fire and water rhythm ([05](05-fire-water-and-succession.md))

Now combine them the way a tennis player combines surface, wind, and opponent.

**If** the remnant is ironbark and red box over tough grass on stony ground, **then** you are in a dry, poor, open, fire-aware envelope. **Therefore** yellow gum, grey box, gold-dust wattle, and the local tussock grasses are plausible. Tree ferns are not. Mountain ash is not. A maple is a hostage.

**If** the remnant is messmate, peppermint, and a fern in the creek, **then** damp sclerophyll, winter-wet, acid loam. **Therefore** the local wet-end understorey shrubs, blackwood in the hollow, rough tree-fern if the soak is real. Coastal tea-tree is not asking to be here. A banksia from wallum might live and still be a lie.

**If** the remnant is tea-tree and moonah on pale dune, **then** calcareous, free-draining, salt-wind. **Therefore** coast beard-heath, drooping she-oak, the local saltbushes. Wet-forest ferns are theatre. Proteaceae from acid sand may hate the lime. Hydrangeas will look at you the way a surfboard looks at a shopping centre.

**If** the remnant is wallum banksia, scribbly gum, and heath on pale siliceous sand, **then** acid, low P, pan, boom-and-bust water. **Therefore** the local heath shrubs, grass trees, paperbark in the swale. A "native" mix from a big-box store, heavy on phosphorus, is how you kill the plot while flying the flag.

The engine has steps:

1. Read the remnant, not the nursery tag.
2. Infer soil, water, fire, exposure.
3. List plants that already succeed in *this* envelope, preferably local provenance if you are restoring.
4. Subtract anything that needs a different envelope, even if it is "Australian native."
5. Stop. "Plausible" is not a planting permit.

Australian native is not a habitat. A karri and a mallee are both native. One of them will look ridiculous in your suburb. "Native" is a passport, not a seating chart.

## Why Imported Garden Dreams Die Here 🍂

They die for reasons that feel personal and are not.

**The soil is poor, acid, and P-sensitive, and you fed it.** Proteaceae — banksias, grevilleas, hakeas, waratahs — evolved for soils in the lower part of the world phosphorus range. Cluster roots, conservative use, mycorrhizal or proteoid equipment. Handreck's review is the grown-up version: many Australian plants have a low P requirement per unit of biomass, and inorganic P in an ashbed is a pulse, not a lifestyle. A "complete" fertiliser is a northern-hemisphere assumption wearing a cheerful label. The plant yellows, burns, dies. You conclude natives are hard. The native concludes you are loud.

**The soil is alkaline sand, and you brought a mountain camellia.** Different wrong. Lime chlorosis, salt wind, drought between taps. The Dandenongs garden in your head was a 1200 mm acid loam with afternoon shade. Point Nepean did not sign that contract.

**The water rhythm is wrong.** You water like a person who owns a hose. The remnant lives through a winter-wet clay and a summer brick, or a sand that is dry two days after a storm. Constant moisture rots the dry-country plants. Neglect-plus-a-heatwave kills the wet-country plants you installed because they were on special.

**The fungi are missing.** A lot of "the soil is wrong" is "the handshake is missing." Eucalypts and most understorey species are mycorrhizal. The [fungal network note](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-fungal-network-beneath-our-feet.md) is already in the cabinet; believe it operationally. Sterile imported mix, fungicide, and a tree in a pot that never met its partner will sit there like a musician without a band. Sometimes it lives. Often it sulks. Restoration people inoculate for a reason.

**The fire is wrong, or the absence of fire is wrong.** Some plants need a gap, smoke, or an interval you cannot legally provide in a backyard. Grass trees may live without fire and still refuse to be the postcard. That is not a failure of character. That is a regime plant in a no-regime yard.

**The climate dialect is wrong.** A Sunshine Coast wet-forest plant in a Keilor summer is a different bet from a box-ironbark plant in a Nambour February. Seasonality, humidity, heat: the fixture list from chapter 01. People move house and bring the old garden in their head. The plants notice.

England is not the villain. England is just a different envelope that colonisers packed in their luggage, along with a belief that green lawn and deciduous autumn were the adult form of a garden. Maples in the Dandenongs villages work because the climate is a decent impersonation and the taps are on. Maples on the western basalt plains are a hobby. Both can be pretty. Only one is literacy.

## "The Soil Is Wrong" Is Often Not The Soil 🕵️

When a plant fails, the post-mortem likes a single villain. Soil is a convenient one. Sometimes it is even true: duplex waterlogging, sodic clay, coffee rock, lime, a builder's rubble profile that is not a soil so much as a crime.

More often the failure is a stack:

- Soil texture plus a hose rhythm that ignores it
- pH plus a fertiliser that was for tomatoes
- Missing fungi plus a species that cannot forage without them
- Exposure plus a wet-gully fern
- Fire absence plus a seeder that needed a gap
- A frost the tag did not mention
- A heatwave the 1990s catalogue did not mention

Ask the remnant what succeeds *without you*. That is the baseline. Anything you add is an experiment. Experiments are allowed. Calling them "what belongs" is not.

A useful gardener's rephrase of the field method:

> What is already living here unassisted, in the leftover bush, on the hardest corner of the block, in the cemetery, along the rail line? That is the envelope. Start there. Then cheat honestly — shade cloth, water, imported loam — if you want a different garden, but know you are cheating.

Cheating is horticulture. Literacy is knowing the difference.

## Plausible Lists, Not Shopping Lists 📝

These are sketches for the case-study envelopes. They are not complete, not a restoration prescription, and not an invitation to strip a remnant for cuttings.

**Dry box-ironbark (central Victorian foothills):** grey box, red box, red ironbark, yellow gum, red stringybark on the higher stony bits, gold-dust wattle and other local wattles, local tussocks, some hedge wattle if it is actually local. Not mountain ash, not tree fern, not wallum banksia, not a rainforest vine.

**Damp foothill mixed (messmate–peppermint):** messmate, narrow-leaf peppermint, mountain grey gum, blackwood in the wet line, local shrubs that already sit under that canopy, rough tree-fern only where a soak is real. Not moonah, not a desert grevillea, not an English woodland bulb package as "understorey."

**Wet sclerophyll / mountain ash:** the local wet-forest shrubs, tree ferns in gullies, myrtle beech only where the gully already holds it. In a suburban block, "I could grow mountain ash" is usually false by year twenty even if the rainfall map is kind. Height, wind, fire, and neighbours are constraints the forest does not have.

**Coastal calcareous tea-tree:** coast tea-tree *if you are in its range*, moonah, coast beard-heath, drooping she-oak, local saltbush and pigface on the exposed edge. Not wallum banksia as a default, not a fern gully, not a maple, not a pile of manure "to get them going."

**Wallum / siliceous sandplain:** wallum banksia, the local scribbly gums and bloodwoods, grass trees, heath shrubs, paperbark in the wet tiles. Low phosphorus. No lime. No "just a bit of chicken manure." Not a rainforest package because the hinterland is twenty minutes up the road.

**Sunshine Coast rainforest edge:** the local wet sclerophyll eucalypts and brush box, and rainforest species only if you actually have the moisture, shelter, and fire-rarity. A fig in a wallum yard is a tap and a lie.

Notice the pattern. The plausible list is mostly **what is already in the remnant.** That is not a lack of imagination. That is the definition of envelope.

## Could ≠ Should 🛑

Even when the plant would live.

- The remnant might be the last patch. Planting in it, "tidying" it, or "enriching" it can wreck the thing you admired.
- Provenance matters. A yellow box from a different bioregion is not the local yellow box. It may still grow. It may also hybridise, mis-time its flowers, or fail in the first real drought.
- Weeds are often plants that *could* grow here. That is the problem. Coastal tea-tree outside its range. Cootamundra wattle. A pretty South African that loves sand. Could is not a virtue.
- Fire. You probably cannot run a legitimate fire regime on a block. Some communities are not backyard-scale objects.
- Water you steal from a river to keep a wet-forest fantasy alive on a dry block is a value judgement. Make it as a judgement, not as "native gardening."
- Councils, covenants, Traditional Owners, and neighbours all have a say that a field inference does not override.

Should is ethics, law, neighbours, Country, and the next forty years of climate. Could is ecology. This chapter only sells could, and it sells it with a receipt.

If you want a sports analogy: reading the ground tells you which boots to bring. It does not tell you to play the match on a sacred oval, or to flood the court so you can surf it.

## A Honest Backyard Method 🏡

1. Walk the leftover vegetation within a kilometre: rail, creek, cemetery, park remnant. Read it with chapters 01–05.
2. Feel your own soil. Do not trust the house's imported topsoil as "the place." Dig a hole. Ribbon it. Note rubble.
3. Pick a goal: restore a local patch, or make a garden that cheats. Both fine. Label them.
4. If restoring: local plants, low P, no lime unless the remnant says alkaline, keep the fungi, tolerate the look of a woodland instead of a lawn.
5. If cheating: import mix, irrigation, shade, and species from a wetter envelope, and do not call it indigenous landscaping.
6. Never treat the remnant as a free nursery. Never eat a mushroom because a sentence felt confident.

The Svalbard vault is in the cabinet if this question starts feeling like a civilisation problem. Your block is not Svalbard. Your block is a small, specific envelope with a hose.

## Worked Failures, Because They Stick 💥

**The maple on the basalt.** Rainfall too low, summer too sharp, soil cracking, wind. Lives if you water. Dies when you go on holiday. The remnant creek is red gum and grassland. The maple was a postcard.

**The banksia with the tomato food.** Yellowing, then death. Phosphorus. The remnant down the road was fine without you.

**The tree fern in the courtyard.** Aspect wrong, humidity wrong, tap compulsory. Looks good for a summer. Becomes a brown stick with a story. The Otway gully it came from had a creek and a cloud.

**The "native garden" of WA grevilleas, NSW rainforest, and a Victorian ash, on fill.** Three envelopes in a trench. One of them might win. That is not literacy. That is a battle royale.

**The wallum block with lime "because vegies like 6.5."** You have cancelled the wallum. The banksias will tell you, quietly, by leaving.

Each failure is a missed chapter: 01 climate, 02 soil, 03–04 plants as text, 05 regime, and the brake in this chapter. Could looked available in the nursery. Should never got a vote. The envelope voted anyway.

## Local Provenance, Without The Halo 📍

"Plant local" is a good prior. It is not a religion.

Local plants match the photoperiod, the frost, the soil chemistry, and the pollinators more often than a random Australian. They also keep the genetic story of *this* ridge, which is a conservation reason, not a vibe. EVC benchmarks and indigenous-nursery lists are the grown-up plausible lists.

Climate change complicates it. Some people argue for planting slightly drier, hotter provenances as a hedge. That is an experiment at landscape scale, with hybridisation risks, and it is being argued by people who have data. Your nature strip is not the trial site unless you are in that conversation.

For a suburban block: start local. If you cheat, cheat on purpose. Do not mix a restoration of a remnant (local, careful, humble) with a courtyard experiment (whatever lives) and give both the same speech.

## Lawn, Mulch, And Other Invented Soils 🟢

A lawn is a decision to run a grassland with a hose and a mower, usually on a non-local grass. Fine as a decision. Not a biome reading.

Mulch is a wet-forest floor impersonation. On a dune it can be a kindness (temperature, evaporation) or a smothering of plants that wanted bare sand. On a clay it can be the difference between crust and life. Match the remnant: wet-forest sites have litter; some heaths have a thin, patchy floor; grassy woodlands have grass, not a woodchip sea.

Imported loam over a duplex clay is a pot sitting on a roof. Trees root into the loam, hit the clay, and fall over in the first real wind. Read the profile, not the top five centimetres the landscaper sold you.

## A Short Engine You Can Run In A Nursery 🛒

Hold the plant. Ask, before the card does:

1. Where does the remnant of this species actually live, in this state, on this soil?
2. What rainfall season does it expect?
3. Does it want fire, hate fire, or shrug?
4. Is it Proteaceae or a grass-tree? If yes, put the fertiliser down.
5. Is this provenance local, or a cultivar from a thousand kilometres away with a cute name?
6. If it lived, would I be glad, or would I have replaced a remnant with a preference?

If you cannot answer 1–3, you are not buying a plant. You are buying a hope. Hope is allowed in a courtyard. It is not restoration.

The living-world series will get to soil as a global chapter. This companion is the local application. Do not wait for every science chapter before going outside, or before walking past a nursery like a person with a brake. The bush will not mark your homework. The plant you killed with kindness already did.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Provenance and climate-shift planting** are an active argument. Local-is-best versus assisted migration. We do not have a finished answer. Humility is the interim policy.
- **Potting mix and nursery fertiliser** still quietly kill Proteaceae. Labels have improved. Not enough.
- **Urban soils are novel.** Rubble, fill, lead, compaction, imported loam over clay. The remnant down the road may not match the profile in your yard.
- **We do not know which "plausible" lists survive a hotter, sharper climate.** A plant that could grow here in 1990 is not guaranteed a 2050.
- **Could ≠ should** will keep being ignored, including by people who read this paragraph and then buy a mountain ash.
