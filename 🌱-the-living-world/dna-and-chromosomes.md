# DNA and Chromosomes 🧬

DNA stores information in the order of its chemical building blocks. Cells use parts of that information to make RNA and proteins, including the machinery that copies and repairs DNA itself.

That circular dependence is essential: DNA is not a self-executing blueprint. It works inside an already organised cell. An isolated genome is an impressive molecule with a serious staffing problem.

## 🔬 A sequence that can be copied

Each DNA strand is a chain of **nucleotides**. Every nucleotide contains a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and one of four bases: adenine, thymine, guanine or cytosine, abbreviated A, T, G and C.

The sugar–phosphate backbone links the chain together. Bases pair across two strands: A with T, G with C. Base pairing and stacking help stabilise the double helix. Because the strands are complementary, the sequence of either specifies the sequence of the other.

The strands run in opposite directions, called **antiparallel**. Their ends are labelled 5′ and 3′ after positions in the sugar. DNA polymerases extend a strand at its 3′ end, so new DNA is made in the 5′ to 3′ direction. That small chemical constraint shapes the entire copying process.

RNA differs in its sugar and generally uses uracil instead of thymine. Its chemical properties make it useful for temporary messages, recognition and catalysis. Some viruses use RNA as their genetic material; cellular organisms use DNA for their genomes.

## 🧵 Packing without losing access

A typical human diploid nucleus contains roughly two metres of DNA extended end to end, inside a space only a few micrometres across. Packing it is only half the challenge. The cell must still reach particular sequences.

In eukaryotes, DNA wraps around histone proteins to form **nucleosomes**. DNA, histones and associated proteins together form **chromatin**. Packaging changes access: tightly packed regions tend to be less available for transcription than more open regions, although the relationship is more complex than a simple on/off switch.

Chromatin forms loops and interacting regions within the nucleus. A textbook's neatly stacked levels of coils are an aid to visualisation, not an exact picture of a living nucleus.

A chromosome usually consists of one long DNA molecule with associated proteins. After DNA replication, the chromosome has two **sister chromatids**, each containing a DNA molecule. The familiar X shape is this duplicated, condensed state before separation. Chromosomes spend much of a cell's life less compact and less photogenic.

## 👯 Two kinds of pair

Humans usually have 23 pairs of nuclear chromosomes in diploid cells: 22 pairs of autosomes and a pair of sex chromosomes. Mature red blood cells lack a nucleus; eggs and sperm normally have one chromosome set; other cellular and individual variations occur.

Two distinctions prevent a great deal of confusion:

- **Homologous chromosomes** are the corresponding maternal and paternal chromosomes. They generally carry the same genes at corresponding locations, but can carry different versions of them.
- **Sister chromatids** are the products of copying one chromosome. They begin as nearly identical copies and separate during division.

Human X and Y chromosomes are a special case: they share only limited homologous regions. Chromosomal patterns also do not describe every aspect of sexual development by themselves.

Chromosome number is not a measure of complexity. Evolution can fuse or split chromosomes without a corresponding leap in the number of biological capabilities. The information and its organisation matter more than the count.

## 🧷 Handles, ends and copying

The **centromere** is the region where the kinetochore assembles, allowing spindle fibres to attach during cell division. **Telomeres** protect the ends of linear chromosomes from being treated as broken DNA. In humans, telomeres contain repeated TTAGGG sequences and specialised associated proteins.

Many cells lose telomeric sequence over repeated divisions. Telomerase can extend it using an RNA template. Telomere biology is one contributor to cellular ageing and cancer, not a single countdown clock for an entire person.

During replication, helicases separate the strands and each old strand serves as a template for a new one. This is **semiconservative replication**: each resulting double helix contains one old strand and one new strand.

Because polymerases work in only one direction, one new strand can follow the advancing replication fork relatively continuously. The other is made in short **Okazaki fragments**, later joined. Primers provide starting points; topoisomerases relieve twisting and tangling; proofreading and repair correct many errors.

The result is very accurate, not perfect. Mutations can arise from copying errors, damage or repair. Their significance depends on where they occur and what they change. [Inheritance](inheritance.md) explains what can pass to offspring; [evolution](evolution.md) explains how inherited variants change in frequency.

## 🔋 The mitochondrial exception

Human mitochondria retain a small circular genome with 37 genes. Most mitochondrial proteins are nevertheless encoded by nuclear genes and imported.

This combination reflects the bacterial ancestry of mitochondria and the transfer or loss of many ancestral genes. Mitochondrial DNA is generally inherited maternally in humans because the egg supplies the cytoplasm and paternal mitochondria are normally excluded or eliminated.

Cells can contain many copies of mitochondrial DNA, and the copies need not all be identical. The phrase “your genome” can therefore hide both multiple compartments and variation among cells. [Cell organisation](cells-and-organelles.md) explores the origin and workings of those compartments.

## 📖 What counts as a gene, and what else is there?

A gene is a DNA region whose expression produces a functional RNA or, through RNA, a protein. Regulatory sequences help control its use. Gene boundaries can be complicated by overlapping transcripts and alternative processing.

Only a small percentage of the human genome directly codes for protein. Other sequence includes regulatory regions, genes for functional RNAs, repeats, transposable-element remnants and structural regions. Some has no demonstrated organism-level function.

A stretch being transcribed or bound by a protein does not prove that natural selection maintains it for a useful role. Conversely, “noncoding” does not mean useless. The dispute about “junk DNA” often confuses those different claims. Genome size is not an intelligence test; onions would be insufferable if it were.

The complementary topic is [gene expression and regulation](gene-expression-and-regulation.md): how a cell uses a selected fraction of the DNA available to it.
