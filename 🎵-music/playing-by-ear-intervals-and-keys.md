# Recreating Songs by Ear 🎧🎹

*How to hear a tune, find the key, catch the intervals, and rebuild the magic*

## The Superpower Is Relative Pitch 🦸

Playing by ear is often treated like a mystical gift. Some people do have unusually strong natural pitch memory, but most practical "I can figure out that song" skill is **relative pitch**.

Relative pitch means:

- hearing how far one note moves to the next
- hearing whether a chord feels like home, away, or almost home
- recognizing common bass movements
- knowing enough theory to test good guesses quickly

It is less "lightning from the heavens" and more "musical detective with decent shoes."

## Step 1: Find Home 🏠

Most songs have a note or chord that feels like the floor. That is the **tonic**, the home base.

Try this:

1. Play the song.
2. Hum the note that feels most resolved.
3. Pause the song.
4. Find that note on your instrument.
5. Test whether the song feels finished when you play it.

If it feels like a full stop, you probably found home. If it feels like a comma, keep hunting.

Once you have home, you can build a likely key around it. A song that resolves to C and sounds bright may be in C major. If it resolves to A and has a darker color, A minor is a strong suspect.

## Step 2: Catch the Bass First 🧱

Melodies sparkle, but bass lines tell secrets.

The bass often reveals the chord progression. In lots of pop, film, game, and rock music, the bass moves through familiar shapes:

- I - V - vi - IV
- vi - IV - I - V
- I - IV - V
- i - VI - III - VII

You do not need to name every chord instantly. Start by finding whether the bass goes:

- up or down
- by step or leap
- back to home
- to the fifth above home
- to the relative minor

Once the bass is sketched, the harmony starts admitting things under questioning.

## Step 3: Learn Intervals Like Flavor Notes 👅

Intervals are the distances between notes. Ear training turns those distances into recognizable flavors.

A few useful associations:

- **Minor second**: tiny crunch, danger-close
- **Major second**: stepwise, casual walking
- **Minor third**: soft, shaded, bluesy
- **Major third**: bright opening of a major chord
- **Perfect fourth**: anthem lift, "here we go"
- **Tritone**: unstable, spicy, wants answers
- **Perfect fifth**: open, strong, horn-call energy
- **Octave**: same note wearing taller shoes

Song mnemonics can help at first, but they are training wheels. Eventually you want to hear "minor third" directly, not think "ah yes, that one song starts like this."

## Step 4: Use Solfege or Scale Degrees 🎶

If the key is home, scale degrees are the furniture.

Instead of thinking:

> C - E - G - A

Think:

> 1 - 3 - 5 - 6

That lets you move the idea to any key. The melody is no longer tied to one keyboard address; it becomes a shape.

Movable-do solfege does the same thing:

- do = 1
- re = 2
- mi = 3
- fa = 4
- sol = 5
- la = 6
- ti = 7

When you can hear a melody as "do mi sol la," recreating it becomes far easier than poking random keys like a confused vending machine.

## Step 5: Slow the Recording Down 🎛️

There is no shame in slowing audio. Professionals do it. Jazz transcribers do it. Film-score nerds do it. Everyone with ears and deadlines does it.

Listen in loops:

1. Grab two seconds.
2. Hum the phrase.
3. Find the first note.
4. Work interval by interval.
5. Confirm against the recording.

Do not try to swallow a whole chorus at once. Take tiny bites. Music is made of little cells, and little cells are much easier to catch.

## Step 6: Guess Chords by Function 🧭

Most tonal chords have jobs:

- **I / i**: home
- **V**: wants to go home
- **IV**: open, warm, pre-home
- **vi / VI**: emotional side room
- **ii**: setup chord, often heads to V
- **bVII**: rock/movie-magic door to somewhere bigger

If a chord feels like it is leaning hard toward home, test V. If it feels like a lift before the chorus, try IV. If it suddenly gets tender without fully leaving the key, vi might be waving from the corner.

This is not guessing wildly. It is using the fact that songs often reuse the same gravitational physics.

## A Practical Noob-to-Pro Path 🚀

### Week 1: Single-Note Melodies

Pick nursery rhymes, game themes, simple vocal hooks. Find the key and melody only.

### Week 2: Bass Notes

Ignore fancy chord color. Find the root motion under simple songs.

### Week 3: Triads

For each bass note, test major or minor. Most songs become shockingly playable at this stage.

### Week 4: Chord Color

Add sevenths, suspensions, inversions, borrowed chords, slash chords. This is where "close enough" starts turning into "wait, that is actually it."

### Ongoing: Transcribe Tiny Things

A two-bar movie motif. A guitar riff. A game menu loop. A choir line hiding in the background. Tiny transcriptions sharpen the ear without turning practice into a mountain climb.

## Why This Matters 🌟

Playing by ear changes listening. Songs stop being sealed objects and become buildable machines. You hear a chorus and notice the bass lift. You hear a movie cue and catch the minor third that made your heart sit up. You hear a modulation and think, "sneaky little key change, I see you."

The goal is not perfect pitch.

The goal is **musical x-ray vision**.
