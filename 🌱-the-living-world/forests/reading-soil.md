# Reading Soil: Texture, Water and Chemistry 🥾

Soil determines where roots can grow, how long water remains available and which nutrients plants can obtain. It also contains organisms that continually transform those resources.

A useful field description starts with behaviour: gritty or sticky, deep or shallow, freely drained or seasonally wet. Formal soil names become easier to understand once those properties are visible.

A dark handful is not automatically fertile. A pale sandy profile is not automatically dry. Soil enjoys making shortcuts expensive.

## ✋ Texture: what the mineral particles feel like

**Texture** describes the proportions of sand, silt and clay. It differs from **structure**, which describes how particles are arranged into aggregates and pores.

Where sampling is permitted, moisten a small amount of soil and knead it. Sand feels gritty. Silt contributes a smooth, silky feel. Clay makes the sample more cohesive and plastic; a clay-rich sample can often be pressed into a longer ribbon between finger and thumb.

This is an estimate, not a laboratory measurement. Organic matter, moisture and experience affect the result.

**Loam** is a texture containing a useful mixture of particle sizes. The word does not establish nutrient levels, pH or drainage. Equally, two clay soils may behave very differently if one has stable, porous aggregates and the other disperses and seals.

## 🧱 The surface is only one layer

A soil profile may contain an organic surface layer, a darker mineral topsoil, a paler leached layer and a subsoil with different texture or chemistry. Not every soil has every layer.

A sharp change from sandy or loamy topsoil to clay-rich subsoil is often called a **texture-contrast** or duplex profile. Water may accumulate above a slowly permeable subsoil, especially during wet periods. Later, the surface can dry markedly.

Deep sand usually drains readily, but groundwater or a restrictive layer can keep it wet. Cracking clays may admit water rapidly through open cracks, then swell and behave differently after wetting.

Read an existing exposure where possible. A creek bank or disturbed edge can reveal information unavailable from the top centimetre, without excavating an intact habitat.

## 🎨 Colour is evidence with several possible causes

Darkness often indicates organic matter, although minerals and charcoal can contribute. Red and yellow colours commonly reflect different iron oxides; yellow does not by itself prove waterlogging.

Grey, low-chroma zones can indicate prolonged saturation and reducing conditions, especially when associated with contrasting mottles. Orange or reddish patches within greyer material may record fluctuating oxidation conditions. Parent material and past conditions can complicate that interpretation.

A pale layer can reflect strong leaching. On some sandy profiles, dissolved organic compounds and metals accumulate deeper down, forming a dark layer that may become cemented. **Coffee rock** is a familiar example in parts of coastal Queensland.

Colour is most useful alongside texture, depth, roots and the site's water regime. It cannot replace all four merely by being dramatic.

## 💧 Plants need both water and oxygen

Water in large pores drains relatively easily. Smaller pores retain it more strongly. Some water held by soil is too tightly bound for roots to extract readily.

Clay can therefore hold much water without making all of it available. Sand may provide good aeration but limited storage between rain events. Compaction reduces larger pores and can restrict both roots and gas exchange.

Waterlogging fills pores that would otherwise contain air. Oxygen diffusion becomes much slower, affecting roots and changing microbial chemistry. A plant adapted to seasonal flooding may have structures or physiological responses that a well-drained-soil species lacks.

Return in another season if possible. One dry afternoon cannot reveal how long a hollow stays saturated in winter.

## 🧪 Chemistry changes what roots can obtain

Soil pH affects nutrient availability and metal solubility. Calcareous sand contains carbonate and may be alkaline; old, strongly leached quartz sand may be acidic. Both can look pale and feel gritty.

Clay minerals and organic matter can retain positively charged nutrient ions. This contributes to **cation exchange capacity**, but the amount varies with mineral type and chemistry. It is not a complete fertility score.

Phosphorus is especially important in many Australian landscapes. It can be scarce in available forms even where total phosphorus is appreciable. Some plants, including many Proteaceae, have highly effective phosphorus-acquisition mechanisms and can be harmed by excessive phosphorus supply. [Woodland and rainforest plants](woodland-and-rainforest-plants.md) explains cluster roots and other strategies.

Vegetation provides clues, but no plant is a universal pH test strip. Species tolerances overlap, and a planted specimen may be receiving irrigation or growing in imported material.

## 🗺️ Several Australian soil patterns

| Setting | What to investigate |
| --- | --- |
| Basalt plains | Clay content, cracking, stony rises, seasonal wetness and land-use history |
| Granite slopes | Shallow coarse material versus deeper pockets and accumulated sediment |
| Wet forest gullies | Organic layers, rooting depth, drainage and oxygen availability |
| Calcareous coastal dunes | Carbonate, alkalinity, rapid drainage and salt exposure |
| Old sandy wallum landscapes | Acidic nutrient-poor profiles, groundwater and subsurface organic accumulations |
| Ironstone or deeply weathered terrain | Restricted rooting, mineral chemistry and limited available nutrients |

Australian soil classifications such as Vertosols, Podosols and Sodosols formalise combinations of profile properties. [Queensland's soil overview](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/land/management/soil/soil-explained/types) is a useful introduction. The categories are descriptions to investigate, not substitutes for looking at the site.

## 🧂 Salinity and sodicity are different problems

**Salinity** is the presence of substantial soluble salts. It can make water uptake difficult even when soil appears wet.

**Sodicity** concerns the proportion of sodium on soil exchange sites. Under susceptible conditions, it promotes clay dispersion, reducing structure and permeability. A saline soil is not necessarily sodic, and vice versa.

Some coastal sediments also contain iron sulfides that can generate acidity when exposed to oxygen. Identifying or managing these acid sulfate materials requires more than noticing an orange drain or an unusual smell. The visible signs are a reason to investigate, not a diagnosis.

## 🏡 A garden has its own history

Around Keilor Downs, the broader volcanic-plain setting is useful context. A particular garden may nevertheless contain imported fill, compacted subsoil, altered drainage and decades of additions.

Compare the actual rooting conditions with nearby remnant vegetation, then verify important assumptions with suitable measurements. [Choosing plants](choosing-plants.md) applies that reasoning to a garden without assuming that every local species suits every local bed.

For the organisms behind nutrient release and soil structure, see [soil ecology](../soil-ecology.md). Texture tells you what the material is like; ecology helps explain what is happening inside it.
