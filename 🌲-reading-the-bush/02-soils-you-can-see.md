# Soils You Can See 🥾

Soil is not dirt. Dirt is what you call soil when you have not been introduced.

The previous chapter handed you climate and rock as the first two cards. This one is the substance those cards actually make: a living, layered, slightly rude body that plants have to drink from, lean on, and negotiate with. If [the fungal network](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-fungal-network-beneath-our-feet.md) is the underground internet, this chapter is the suburb the internet is installed in.

You can see more of it than a lab would like to admit. Colour. Texture-by-feel. Whether water sits or vanishes. Whether the plants are behaving like the pH is sour, sweet, or just empty. A test kit in the glovebox is useful. A fern, a banksia, and a handful of soil are already talking.

## What Soil Is, When It Is Being Honest 🧱

Soil is mineral grains plus organic matter plus pores plus water plus air plus a workforce of organisms, arranged in layers, over time. Take any of those away and you have a different object. Pure sand in a dune is barely a soil yet. A peaty gully floor is almost a compost heap with a mineral habit. A cracking basalt clay is a mineral argument that swallows fences.

Hans Jenny's factors still earn their keep: **climate, organisms, relief, parent material, time.** The cabinet's landscape method next door is this paragraph with more scenery. For a walk, compress it:

- **Parent material** sets the mineral diet. Granite gives sand and kaolin. Basalt gives clay and more bases. Quartz sand gives... quartz. Limestone gives calcium whether you asked or not.
- **Climate** decides how hard the leaching works. High rainfall plus time equals acid, hungry, iron-stained profiles. Low rainfall leaves salts and carbonates closer to the surface.
- **Relief** decides whether the soil stays or leaves. Ridges shed. Gullies catch. South faces keep moisture. North faces bake.
- **Organisms** include the plants you came to read, the fungi you cannot see, the worms, the termites, and you.
- **Time** is why a fresh dune and an old sandplain are not the same sand.

CSIRO's Australian Soil Classification is the grown-up naming system: Chromosols, Sodosols, Kurosols, Ferrosols, Podosols, Calcarosols, Vertosols, and friends. You do not need the order name on a Saturday. You need the behaviour. Duplex, cracking clay, deep sand, red loam, peat. Those are field sentences.

## Texture-By-Feel, Which Is Not A Personality Test ✋

Ribbon it. That is the whole trick.

Take a pinch of moist soil. Not wet mud, not dust. Knead it. Try to roll a ribbon between thumb and finger.

- **Sand** feels gritty, will not ribbon, falls apart, and makes a noise if you chew a grain (you can skip the chewing). Water falls through it unless a pan downstairs says no.
- **Loam** is the compromise: some grit, some silk, some stick, a short ribbon. This is the texture garden books pretend is normal.
- **Clay** is sticky, stains, ribbons long, and polishes. It holds water and then refuses to share it at the wrong moment. It cracks in basalt country. It sits yellow-grey and sullen under a sandy top in duplex country.

**Loam** is a mix, not a moral victory. Australian "loam" under wet forest can still be acid and phosphorus-poor. Do not hear "loam" and reach for a maple.

A **duplex** profile — sandy or loamy top over a sharp clay subsoil — is one of Victoria's signature moves. Chromosols if the clay is not strongly acid; Kurosols if it is; Sodosols if sodium is making the clay dispersive and hostile. In winter the water perches on the clay and your boots make that sucking sound. In summer the top dries and the clay downstairs can still be a brick. Plants that live here have opinions about wet feet and drought in the same year. Many Victorian paddocks are this story with the trees removed.

## Colour Is A Gossip 🎨

Colour is not a soil type. It is a clue.

- **Black or very dark topsoils** often mean organic matter. Wet forest floors, peaty soaks, and some volcanic soils do this. Dark is not automatically fertile. It is often "at least someone has been photosynthesising here."
- **Red** usually means iron oxides in a well-drained profile. Deep red Ferrosols on basalt — the old name was krasnozem — can be friable, acid, and better structured than they have any right to be. They were also the first soils people cleared, because the forest on them looked like money.
- **Yellow and grey** often mean the iron has been reduced or removed. Prolonged wetness. Poor drainage. A yellow duplex subsoil is a winter-waterlogging confession.
- **Pale, bleached, almost white** sand or loam under a thin dark top is a leaching story. Podosols (podsols in the old language) send organic matter and aluminium, sometimes iron, down the profile. In coastal sand that downstairs layer can harden into **coffee rock**.
- **Cream, pale brown, maybe a fizz with acid:** carbonate. Coastal calcareous sand. Alkaline. Different universe from wallum.

Mottles — orange blobs in grey clay — are the soil saying "I wet and dry for a living." Treat them as a drainage diary.

## Drainage Is Personality 💧

Ask what happens to a bucket of water.

**Free-draining sand** is a drought machine with excellent manners after rain. Roots have to chase water. Nutrients wash. Coastal tea-tree, banksia, she-oak, and a lot of heath live here because they can, not because they enjoy being poor.

**Perched wetness** is the duplex and the wallum pan. Water sits. Then it goes. Plants must tolerate both, or live on the rises.

**Gully peat and wet-forest organic soils** stay moist because rainfall, shade, and organic matter conspire. Tree ferns are a wetness meter with extra theatre. If the ferns are happy and the soil feels springy, you are not in box-ironbark country even if a map says you are "in Victoria."

**Cracking clay** on basalt plains swallows water down the cracks, then swells shut. Vertosols. They grow grass and argument. They are not a fern gully, and they are not a dune.

Drainage is why "I will just water it" fails as landscape literacy. The remnant vegetation is adapted to a *rhythm*, not a mean. A plant from a winter-wet duplex is not asking for a pot of constant moisture. A plant from deep sand is not asking for a clay bowl and a saucer.

## The Cast Of Soils This Shelf Actually Needs 🎭

### Sand

Coastal dunes, granite wash, old sandplains. Low water-holding, low nutrient, often acid if siliceous, alkaline if calcareous. Texture is the easy part. Chemistry is the fork in the road.

### Clay

Basalt plains, some alluvial flats, duplex subsoils. Holds nutrients better, holds water too well or too meanly, depending. Structure matters more than the word "clay." A friable red clay and a sodic grey clay are not cousins you should invite to the same party.

### Loam and clay loam

Wet and damp sclerophyll on sheltered hillsides often sit on deeper loams. Viridans and Victorian ecosystem notes put wet sclerophyll on deep loamy soils, rainfall above about 1100 mm. That is mountain ash country, not a sand dune with ambition.

### Laterite and ironstone

Old, deeply weathered surfaces. Iron and aluminium concentrated into nodules or a duricrust. A pallid zone underneath, bleached and kaolinised. These are hungry, acid, ancient. Box-ironbark country often involves stony, auriferous, thoroughly used-up ground — gold-bearing rock is a human story on top of a soil story about not much left to eat. Lateritic gravel underfoot is the continent showing its age.

### Peaty wet forest

Otway and Central Highlands gullies: high organic matter, acid, springy, smelling like a wet dog and a mushroom shop. This is not "rich" in the fertiliser sense. It is carbon sitting in the cold. Drain it and you lose the plot as well as the carbon.

### Coastal alkaline sand

Calcareous dunes. Calcium carbonate in the parent sand. pH nearer 7–8.5. Coastal Moonah Woodland and tea-tree scrub live here. Phosphorus and iron chemistry are different from the acid sand two bays over. A banksia that loves wallum may not love this, and a hydrangea that loved a Dandenongs garden will look at you as if you have personally lowered the tone.

### Wallum

The Sunshine Coast's sandy lowlands, named from the Kabi Kabi word for *Banksia aemula* and then stretched to mean the whole poor, wet-and-dry sandplain. Classic profile, from older survey work: dark sandy humus over white sand over a hard black organic pan — coffee rock — then more sand, then sandy clay. Very acid. Very low nutrients. Excessive drainage above the pan, wetness on it. Selectors called it useless. That was an agricultural judgement, not an ecological one. Wallum is a specialist system. Treat it as "needs compost and lime" and you have not read it. You have decided to replace it.

## pH Clues From The Plants, Who Are Ruder Than A Kit 🧪

A pH kit is fine. The remnant vegetation is already a kit that cannot lie as easily.

**Acid, low-nutrient, often sand or highly leached:** banksias, many heath shrubs, wallum species, a lot of stringybark country, wet-forest floors. Proteaceae (banksia, grevillea, hakea) are famous for **phosphorus sensitivity**. They evolved cluster roots and fungal deals to scavenge P from soils that would make a northern-hemisphere farmer weep. Give them "complete fertiliser" and you can kill them with kindness. This is not a vibe. It is a well-documented Australian native plant problem.

**Neutral to alkaline, often calcareous:** moonah, some she-oaks, coastal tea-tree on limestone sands, plants that do not flinch at calcium. If the remnant is moonah on dune calcarenite, the soil is not asking for sulphur and peat as a personality transplant.

**Better-structured volcanic reds:** still often acid. Krasnozems / Ferrosols can sit around pH 4.5–5.5 and grow wet forest, then dairy, then strawberries, then an argument about what the soil "really wants." Acidity is not infertility, and fertility is not a licence to import a woodland from somewhere wetter.

**Sodic, hostile subsoils:** the plants that persist are the ones that do not need a deep, friendly clay. Or they live on the better-structured A horizon and sulk when you rip the profile.

Cross-check. One garden camellia proves a tap and a truckload of mix, not a biome. A hundred metres of remnant banksia, grass tree, and pale sand is a pH and nutrient statement.

## What Your Boots Are Already Telling You 👢

- **Squelch in winter, brick in summer:** duplex or clay. Look for yellow-grey mottles if you dig.
- **Squeak and drain, pale dust on the carpet:** sand. Ask acid or alkaline next.
- **Springy, dark, mushroomy, ferns:** organic wet forest. Do not "improve" it.
- **Red and friable on a slope:** volcanic or deeply weathered; check the vegetation before you assume rich.
- **Stony, shallow, light through the trees:** ridge, granite, or box-ironbark. Fertility is not the headline. Survival is.
- **Black cracking paddock:** Vertosol country. The remnant, if any, was grassland or open woodland, not a gully fantasy.

The [fungal note](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-fungal-network-beneath-our-feet.md) is the handshake under this. Most Australian plants are mycorrhizal. Many Proteaceae skip the usual mycorrhiza and build cluster roots instead. If you import soil, sterilise it, fertilise it, and then wonder why the remnant species fail, the soil is not "wrong." The relationship is missing.

## Organic Matter, Which Is Not "Rich" 🍂

Garden language loves "rich soil." The bush is ruder. A wet-forest floor can be dark, deep, and still phosphorus-poor. A box-ironbark ridge can be pale, thin, and still doing a complete ecosystem. Organic matter is carbon in the soil, a sponge, a slow pantry, and a housing estate for fungi. It is not a NPK label.

In the Otway gully, litter and roots build a springy mat because the climate is cool-wet enough that decomposition does not win every week. In a hot sandplain, organic matter lives in a thin dark lid over white sand, or it is parked downstairs as coffee rock. Same word. Different career.

If you scrape the litter "for tidiness," you have removed the pantry and the mulch. If you dump manure on a banksia heath, you have thrown a party the guests cannot metabolise. Organic matter is a rhythm, not a product you add until the colour looks northern-hemisphere.

Smell is allowed. Fungal, sour, and clean-wet is a wet forest. Dusty and not much is a dry sand. Rotten-egg in a drain is a different problem (acid sulfate, waterlogging) and a reason to stop digging like a hero.

## Salt, Sodic Clay, And Other Hostilities 🧂

Not every difficult soil is "poor sand."

**Sodosols** have a sodic clay subsoil. Sodium makes the clay disperse, seal, and sulk. Water sits. Roots hit a wall. Victorian duplex paddocks do this at industrial scale. The remnant vegetation on the worst of it is not a fern gully waiting to happen.

**Saline** edges — estuaries, some western-plain soaks, the last line before mangroves if you wander into [Reading The Water](../🌊-reading-the-water/01-tides-salt-and-the-shape-of-the-shore.md) — need a different cast: saltbush, some she-oaks, paperbarks that can take wet salt. Do not "fix" them with a wet-forest shopping list.

**Acid sulfate** soils sit quiet in coastal drains and then oxidise when you drain them, producing actual acid. This is a management horror, not a pH-kit curiosity. If you are on a coastal flat and someone wants to dig a pond because the brochure said wetlands, that is a specialist conversation.

The field handle: if plants look scorched without fire, or the clay slakes into soup when you wet a crumb, or the drain runs orange, you are not in "add compost" territory. You are in chemistry.

## A Keilor Downs Aside, Because The Human Lives Somewhere 🏠

Western basalt plains. Rain shadow. Cracking clays and stony rises. Remnant grassland and open woodland if anything is left. This is not the Dandenongs, and it is not a moral failing of the suburb. A tree fern here is a hose. A red ironbark here might actually be having a conversation with the climate, depending on the exact patch. A mountain ash is a cameo from a wetter postcode.

The imported garden mix on top of the basalt is a third soil, invented last Tuesday. Read it separately from the remnant down at the creek. The creek is the landscape. The mix is a pot. [Chapter 06](06-therefore-you-could-grow-this.md) will get stern about the difference. For now: your boots on the nature strip are not a soil survey of the bioregion.

If you want a sports analogy: texture is the court surface, drainage is how fast the ball comes through, pH is the indoor-versus-outdoor lighting. You can play on all of them. You cannot play the same way. A clay-court plant on a sand court is not "failing to try." It is wearing the wrong shoes.

One last field habit: after rain, come back. Duplex country and wallum pans tell the truth when they are wet. Sand tells the truth when it is dry. A single visit is a snapshot. Soil is a process, like the rest of life in this cabinet.

## A Handful, A Guess, A Check 🔬

Method, same as the expedition map:

1. Feel texture. Grit, silk, stick, ribbon.
2. Look at colour and at whether water would sit.
3. Look at the remnant plants as a pH and nutrient gossip.
4. Infer: sand or clay, acid or alkaline, free-draining or perched, hungry or less hungry.
5. Check later. A lab test is a check, not a personality.

Worked guesses:

- **Otway fern gully:** dark, organic, acid, moist, moderately heavy on Cretaceous sediments. You could grow wet-forest understorey species. You could not grow a dry-country ironbark and expect it to feel understood.
- **You Yangs slope:** coarse, shallow, granite-derived, free-draining, low water-holding. Tough woodland. A tree fern would be a performance art piece.
- **Nepean dune:** pale, calcareous, alkaline, drought-prone, wind-pruned. Tea-tree country. Not wallum, not a krasnozem, not a place for mountain ash.
- **Sippy Downs wallum:** siliceous sand, acid, pan, boom-and-bust water. Banksia country. Lime and phosphorus are not a kindness.

Inference is a first draft. A filled gully, a scalped ridge, and a nature strip are all capable of lying. [Chapter 06](06-therefore-you-could-grow-this.md) is where the horticultural temptation gets a hearing, and a warning. Could ≠ should. The soil is not a mood board.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Most soil microbes have never been cultured.** We walk on an unread library. Texture we can feel. The workforce we mostly cannot.
- **Available phosphorus is a swamp of methods.** Total P, Colwell P, organic P, microbial P: different questions. Heathlands in particular do not care about your agriculture test.
- **Coffee rock, sodicity, and acid sulfate** are easy to miss until a drain, a drought, or a subdivision finds them.
- **Mapping is coarse.** Victoria has a soil-type map built from seventy years of surveys. Paddock-scale reality can still be a different soil from the polygon.
- **We do not know how fast these soils will track a changing climate.** Organic wet-forest floors and wallum pans are not obliged to persist because a 1980s profile description was tidy.
