# How Can a Biscuit Power a Bicycle? 🍪🚲

Eat a biscuit, climb onto a bike, and some of the energy from that biscuit can eventually help turn the pedals. Some also leaves as heat. Your legs are very good at reminding you of the second part.

A **calorie** measures an amount of energy. Energy lets things change: move, warm up or carry out chemical reactions. Food supplies chemicals whose reactions help our cells—the tiny living units in our bodies—do their work.

## 🔥 Why the packet has such odd numbers

The “calories” on food labels usually mean **kilocalories**, or **kcal**. One kilocalorie is a thousand small calories. The name has become a little untidy through everyday use.

Another energy unit is the **joule**. Food labels often use thousands of joules, called **kilojoules**, or **kJ**.

**One food Calorie = 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ.**

These are two ways of measuring the same quantity, like metres and feet measure distance. The calorie isn't a mysterious ingredient hidden between the flour and butter.

## 🧪 Scientists can actually burn the biscuit

A device called a **calorimeter** can measure heat released when a food sample burns. That tells scientists how much energy complete burning releases.

Your body doesn't burn a biscuit in one fiery burst. It breaks food down and releases usable energy in many controlled chemical steps.

It also can't use everything a laboratory burner can. Some material passes through without being digested or absorbed. More energy leaves in other waste. Food-label estimates try to account for these differences, rather than simply reporting all the heat from a fire.

## 🧮 A little food arithmetic

Three familiar groups supply much of our food energy:

- **Carbohydrates**, including sugars and starch: roughly 4 kcal per gram the body can use.
- **Proteins**, which supply building pieces for many body parts: roughly 4 kcal per gram.
- **Fats**, useful as fuel and building material: roughly 9 kcal per gram.

These are practical averages. For an imaginary food with 10 grams of usable carbohydrate and 2 grams of fat, the estimate would be about **40 + 18 = 58 kcal**.

Real foods need more careful accounting. The numbers are useful estimates, not an exact prediction of what every person will obtain from every mouthful.

## 🌰 Food can hide some of its fuel

Plant cells have walls. If chewing and digestion leave some of those walls intact, material inside can remain difficult to reach.

In a controlled almond study, researchers measured less usable energy than a standard calculation predicted. That doesn't give every nut a magical calorie discount. It shows that structure matters.

Grinding and cooking can expose material to digestive **enzymes**, molecules that speed up food breakdown. Heating starch with water can make it more accessible; cooling some cooked starch makes a portion harder to digest.

Gut microbes—microscopic living things—can break down some material our own enzymes leave behind. Some of their products become fuel for us too. [Digestion](digestion-and-cellular-energy.md) follows that journey.

## 🛌 You use energy even while asleep

Your heart keeps pumping, your brain remains active and cells keep repairing and replacing parts. Staying alive is ongoing work.

Movement, growth, temperature control and processing meals add other demands. Those demands change with the body and its circumstances. You aren't a bicycle that burns a perfectly fixed amount of fuel for every kilometre.

A calorie tells us about energy quantity. It doesn't tell us how much a food contributes to growth, how filling it is or which vitamins it supplies. Food has several jobs, and bodies need fuel even on days when the bicycle stays in the shed.
