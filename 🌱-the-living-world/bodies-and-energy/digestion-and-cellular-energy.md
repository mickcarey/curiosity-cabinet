# From Food to Cellular Energy ⚡

A muscle cannot spend a piece of toast. Food must be broken down, absorbed and processed before cells can capture some of its chemical energy in a usable form.

Much of that work ultimately supports **ATP production**. ATP is continually made and consumed as reactions are coupled to movement, transport and synthesis. It is a rapidly replenished intermediate, rather than the body's main long-term energy store.

## 🍽️ Digestion is selective disassembly

Chewing increases surface area. Salivary amylase begins starch breakdown. In the stomach, acid unfolds many proteins and supports the activity of pepsin, a protein-digesting enzyme.

Most nutrient digestion and absorption occur in the small intestine. Pancreatic bicarbonate helps neutralise acidic contents arriving from the stomach. Pancreatic enzymes digest carbohydrates, proteins and fats, while enzymes on the intestinal surface complete many final steps.

Bile helps disperse fats and supports the formation of micelles that carry lipid digestion products towards the intestinal surface. Bile is not an enzyme; its physical chemistry helps enzymes and absorption do their jobs.

The lining has folds, villi and microscopic projections called microvilli. Together they provide a large absorptive surface. Nutrients cross through specific transporters and other mechanisms, rather than simply soaking through a passive sponge.

## 🩸 Nutrients take different routes

Most absorbed sugars and amino acids enter blood vessels that drain through the **hepatic portal vein** to the liver. The liver can store, modify or redistribute them. For example, it stores glucose as glycogen and processes amino-acid nitrogen for excretion.

Most long-chain dietary fats take a different route. Intestinal cells rebuild triglycerides and package them into **chylomicrons**, particles containing lipids and proteins. These enter intestinal lymph vessels and later reach the bloodstream. Their remnants are subsequently taken up by the liver.

Shorter-chain fatty acids can travel more directly through portal blood. The distinctions follow solubility, particle size and transport machinery, not a single universal route for “food”.

Liver glycogen helps support blood glucose. Muscle glycogen mainly supports the muscle containing it. Fat can provide substantial stored energy, while amino acids also supply material for proteins and other nitrogen-containing molecules.

## 🔋 How glucose supports ATP production

In an oxygen-using human cell, glucose oxidation can be divided into connected stages:

1. **Glycolysis**, in the cytosol, converts one glucose into two pyruvate molecules. It yields a net two ATP and two NADH molecules.
2. **Pyruvate oxidation**, in mitochondria, produces acetyl-CoA, carbon dioxide and additional NADH.
3. **The citric acid cycle** oxidises acetyl groups, regenerates its starting acceptor and produces electron carriers, carbon dioxide and a small amount of directly generated nucleotide triphosphate.
4. **Oxidative phosphorylation** uses electron transfer to support most of the ATP yield.

NADH and related carriers are important because oxidation transfers electrons. Capturing those electrons in carriers lets the cell couple their subsequent transfer to useful work.

Fatty-acid breakdown supplies acetyl-CoA and electron carriers through **beta-oxidation**. Amino-acid carbon skeletons enter metabolism at several points after their nitrogen is handled. The pathways intersect; cells do not have entirely separate machinery for every meal ingredient.

## 🌀 Why a membrane is essential

The mitochondrial electron-transport chain passes electrons through carriers towards oxygen, which is reduced to water. Several complexes use the released free energy to move protons across the inner membrane.

This produces an **electrochemical gradient**: a difference in both proton concentration and electrical potential. Protons returning through **ATP synthase** drive rotation and conformational changes that support ATP formation from ADP and phosphate.

The energy has changed form: chemical differences support an electrical and concentration gradient, which supports chemical synthesis. A membrane prevents the gradient from immediately dissipating. A punctured membrane is rather like a battery with an enthusiastic short circuit.

An idealised accounting often gives roughly 30–32 ATP per glucose in eukaryotic cells. Actual yields vary with transport costs, tissue, proton leak and how intermediates are used. The mechanism is more informative than memorising one exact total.

## 🫁 Oxygen, lactate and heat

Glycolysis does not directly require oxygen. It does require NAD⁺. Converting pyruvate to lactate regenerates NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue when the rate of glycolysis exceeds the capacity to oxidise its products through mitochondria.

Lactate can be produced even when oxygen is present. It can be used as fuel by other cells or converted back towards glucose by the liver. It is a transferable metabolite, not merely waste, and it does not explain delayed muscle soreness.

Oxygen enables efficient mitochondrial respiration, but reactive oxygen compounds also form in cells. They participate in signalling and can cause damage when poorly controlled. Cells have several protective systems; ageing cannot be reduced to a single story of biological rust.

Energy conversion also releases heat. Some tissues deliberately increase heat production through uncoupling: proton movement generates heat without producing the corresponding ATP. Brown adipose tissue uses this mechanism.

## 🦠 What reaches the colon

Gut microbes ferment some compounds that human enzymes leave undigested. Products include acetate, propionate and butyrate, which can be absorbed and used by the host. Other products support microbes or leave as waste and gas.

The amount and outcome depend on food, microbial community and intestinal conditions. This is a real metabolic contribution, but it does not make the effect of a particular probiotic predictable from a general diagram.
