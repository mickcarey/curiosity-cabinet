# Barcodes: Tiny Striped Passports 🧩

## The Gist

A barcode is not the price. It is usually an identity tag.

At the supermarket scanner, the black-and-white stripes are read as a number. That number points the register toward a product record: name, size, price, tax category, discounts, stock rules, and whatever else the retailer has wired into the system.

The barcode is the little passport. The shop's database is the border officer with the clipboard.

## How The Trick Works

- The stripes encode digits using different widths of bars and spaces.
- A scanner measures the reflected light pattern and turns it back into a number.
- The number often includes a product identifier such as a GTIN.
- A check digit helps catch scanning or typing errors.
- The register looks up the current price locally or through the store's systems.

## Why It Is Interesting

Barcodes are boring in exactly the way civilization needs things to be boring. A cashier does not need to know whether a tin of tomatoes came from a warehouse, a truck, or a promotional display. The stripe says: "I am this item." Everything else can click into place.

That tiny standard made retail faster, stock control less mystical, and global supply chains a little less like everyone shouting numbers across a loading dock.
