# A Fallen Tree Becomes Everybody's Lunch 🍂

A tree falls and stops living as a tree. For the fungi, beetles and other creatures moving in, a new opportunity has opened.

The wood becomes food, shelter and a place that holds moisture. As it breaks down, its ingredients move into soil, air and other bodies. **Decomposition** is the breakdown of dead material into simpler substances.

The tree's life has ended. Its atoms still have a busy schedule.

## 🪱 Who starts clearing the table?

Different creatures tackle different parts. Scavengers eat chunks of dead animals. Worms and insects break up material and mix it with soil. Fungi and bacteria release **enzymes**, molecules that speed up reactions, onto material outside themselves.

Those enzymes help cut large molecules into smaller pieces that the decomposers can absorb. Other creatures eat the decomposers. The result is a **food web**, a set of connected relationships showing who feeds on whom.

Wood is particularly tough. It contains strong sugar-based fibres called **cellulose**, reinforced by other material including **lignin**. Different fungi can break down different parts.

Some leave pale, stringy wood; others leave brown material that crumbles into blocks. A rotting log's texture can be a clue to which chemical jobs its occupants have done.

## 🌧️ Why doesn't everything rot at the same speed?

Decomposers need suitable conditions. Cold can slow their activity. Dryness can deprive them of needed water. Too much water can fill air spaces and restrict oxygen.

That means both a very dry place and a waterlogged place can slow some kinds of decay, for different reasons.

A standing dead trunk may stay fairly dry. The same trunk lying against damp soil gives fungi a different opportunity. Its size, bark and kind of wood also matter. There is no universal “best before” date for a log.

## 🔁 Atoms go around; energy keeps coming in

Decomposers use some dead material to build their own bodies. They release other ingredients into forms that plants and microbes can take up again.

Some carbon returns to the air as carbon dioxide as organisms use food for energy. Plants can later take in that gas to build more living material.

The atoms are reused, but usable energy doesn't circulate forever. Living activity releases heat, so the system needs continuing energy supplies—mostly sunlight.

Even nutrient release isn't instant. Decomposers need nutrients for themselves. While breaking down carbon-rich wood, they may temporarily take up nitrogen that nearby plants could otherwise use. Recycling includes a processing stage.

## 🪵 A log does more than feed things

A fallen trunk makes shade, traps material and helps hold moisture. Its cracks shelter small animals. Standing dead trees may provide nesting hollows and perches.

Removing every dead branch can therefore remove homes and alter the conditions that living things need. A tidy-looking forest isn't automatically a better-functioning one.

Fire transforms dead material too, but much faster and with different results. Ash, charcoal and a sudden change in shelter create a different future from gradual decay. [Fire and regrowth](forests/fire-water-and-succession.md) follows that story.

## 🌊 The ocean gets falling dinners too

Dead drifting organisms, droppings and other particles sink through seawater as **marine snow**. Much is eaten on the way down. What reaches deeper water helps feed life in the dark.

A sunken whale provides a much larger feast. Different animals and microbes use it as its soft tissues and then harder remains change. Even fats in the bones can support chemical reactions that feed later communities. [Ocean ecology](ocean-ecology.md) explores these routes to dinner.

## 🧬 What happens inside a living body?

Individual cells—the tiny living units of a body—die throughout life. Some carefully dismantle themselves when damaged or no longer needed. This helps shape a developing body and maintain tissues.

Death of the whole organism is different: the coordinated activity keeping it alive has failed. Not every cell and associated microbe stops at the same instant.

Ageing involves many interacting changes to repair, maintenance and regulation. Species age differently, and there is no single universal death switch. The [open questions](open-questions-in-biology.md) remain fascinating. Meanwhile, outside the body, decomposition turns yesterday's living material into part of tomorrow's world.
