# Chi Xu: Making A Screen Something You Can Wear 👓

A large screen normally arrives with a large object attached. XREAL founder Chi Xu has built a company around a different proposition: let the image appear large while the device carrying it resembles glasses.

That makes his story particularly relevant to pairing a handheld keyboard such as the Twiddler 4 with XREAL One Pro display glasses. The keyboard can leave the desk, but the display needs somewhere to go too.

## 🛠️ From Engineering To Nreal

Xu earned a doctorate in electrical and computer engineering and worked at Nvidia and Magic Leap before the creation of Nreal. In a 2020 interview, he discussed his route into the company and the choices behind its early glasses. [The AR Show interview](https://www.thearshow.com/podcast/086-chi-xu)

Nreal was founded in 2017 and later became XREAL. The company describes its mission as bringing augmented reality to a broad consumer audience. **Augmented reality**, often shortened to AR, adds digital imagery to a view of the physical world. [XREAL’s company history](https://www.xreal.com/us/about)

The useful distinction is between that broad ambition and any individual product’s capabilities. Showing a virtual monitor, tracking its position and understanding a room are different engineering tasks.

## 📱 Why Leave Part Of The Computer In Your Pocket?

In his 2020 AR Show interview, Xu discussed making Nreal’s glasses a companion to a smartphone, along with the path from wearable displays towards fuller augmented reality. That division is central to understanding the early product idea. The glasses did not have to be an entire independent computer balanced on a nose. [Xu’s interview with Jason McDowall](https://www.thearshow.com/podcast/086-chi-xu)

The engineering logic is straightforward: wherever processing and power consumption occur, heat and battery requirements follow. Moving work to a phone can reduce what the head-worn part must contain. The cost is dependence on another device, its connection and its software. You have redistributed the problem around the body.

This makes Xu’s story a useful study in choosing boundaries. A self-contained headset and phone-connected glasses can both show digital imagery, but they make different demands on the wearer. The product decision starts before anyone draws a prettier frame.

## 🔬 The Interesting Bet

In a January 2020 interview, Xu emphasised getting early hardware into consumers’ hands and using their feedback to improve it. That places everyday use at the centre of the development problem: a compelling demonstration still has to survive being worn, connected and used for something ordinary. [GamesBeat’s interview with Xu](https://gamesbeat.com/nreal-ceo-chi-xu-interview-advancing-ars-future-one-step-at-a-time/)

This is where wearable displays become especially interesting. A product can impress someone for five minutes yet lose them over text clarity, fit or the effort of connecting it. The face is a rather unforgiving place to install a peripheral.

## 🖥️ A Practical Window Into The Ambition

The One Pro illustrates one approach taken by Xu’s company: glasses with local spatial-display processing that receive content from a connected host. The host supplies the applications; the glasses handle the view. This is a company and engineering-team achievement, rather than evidence that Xu personally designed each component. [One Pro product information](https://www.xreal.com/one-pro)

For the wearer, that division suggests a useful possibility: existing software can remain on an existing computer while the display changes form. The arrangement still has to work as a whole, with image, input, cables and power included.

## 🪟 A Floating Screen Is Only One Rung Of The Ladder

Picture three increasingly demanding tasks. First, show a film in front of someone’s eyes. Second, keep its virtual screen facing a particular direction as they turn their head. Third, place a virtual object on a real table so they can walk around it and see it from another side.

The first needs an image. The second also needs an estimate of head rotation and a matching change in the rendered view. The third needs position as well as orientation; making the object convincingly meet the table introduces knowledge of the surroundings. These are different layers of capability, even though marketing may place them beneath the same AR umbrella.

This distinction helps explain the appeal of the One Pro’s host-driven display approach. A conventional application already has something useful to show. It can benefit from a wearable screen without first being rebuilt to understand tables, walls and furniture. That is an interpretation of the product strategy, rather than a claim about Xu’s private intentions.

The unresolved tension is adoption. A display accessory has familiar uses, but users must still find those uses valuable enough to tolerate another thing to wear and connect. Xu’s early emphasis on consumer feedback acknowledges this gap between an impressive prototype and a habit. A successful demo gets applause; a successful everyday object gets picked up again tomorrow.

## 🧭 Why He Belongs In This Cabinet

[Douglas Engelbart](douglas-engelbart.md) explored how tools could amplify skilled work. [Thad Starner](thad-starner.md) explored computation worn through everyday life. Xu’s connection is the attempt to turn wearable-display ambitions into products people can buy and use.

Those are related questions, rather than a single straight line of invention. Together they make an excellent rabbit hole: once the computer’s screen and controls stop needing a desk, how much of our idea of “sitting down to work” changes with them?
