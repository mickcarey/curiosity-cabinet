# What Is Life? 🫧

A bacterium is alive. A flame is not. Both consume fuel, grow and respond to their surroundings, so the difference takes a little explaining.

Biology has no universally accepted definition of life. That is not because biologists cannot recognise a squirrel. Definitions become difficult at the edges: dormant spores, viruses, sterile organisms and the first chemical systems that could evolve. Those edges reveal what ordinary life is actually doing.

## 🔋 Staying organised takes energy

A cell continually replaces damaged molecules, regulates its internal chemistry and maintains a boundary. Its apparent stability comes from activity. Stop that activity for long enough and the organisation falls apart, even though the atoms remain.

This does not violate the second law of thermodynamics. Organisms are open systems: they take in energy and materials, release heat and waste, and increase the total entropy of themselves and their surroundings. A tidy cell has an energy bill.

Erwin Schrödinger made this relationship central to his 1944 book *What Is Life?* His language of “negative entropy” was influential, but **usable free energy** is the clearer biological idea. Sunlight or chemical reactions can drive processes that would not proceed on their own. The details appear in [cellular energy](digestion-and-cellular-energy.md).

Energy use alone is insufficient. Hurricanes also persist by dissipating energy. They do not pass an inherited system of biochemical instructions to hurricane offspring.

## 🧬 Maintenance, heredity and evolution

Three features work together in familiar cellular life:

- **Metabolism:** linked chemical reactions obtain materials and energy, build components and remove waste.
- **Compartmentalisation:** a membrane helps maintain conditions different from the surroundings.
- **Heredity:** information is copied, with variation that can affect survival and reproduction.

These features support one another. Membranes keep useful molecules together; enzymes build and maintain membranes; inherited sequences help specify the enzymes. None operates as an independent starter kit.

**Autopoiesis**, developed by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela, emphasises the self-maintaining network. A living cell helps produce the components and boundary that make its continued existence possible. This is a useful distinction between a cell and a machine assembled and maintained entirely from outside. It does not, by itself, settle how evolution belongs in a definition.

A widely used astrobiology working definition describes life as a chemical system able to sustain itself and undergo Darwinian evolution. “Self-sustaining” does not mean independent of an environment: every organism needs resources, and many depend on other organisms. “Capable of evolution” concerns populations across generations. An individual mule does not have to reproduce to qualify as alive. Biology can spare the mule an identity crisis.

The definition is a research tool, not a universally binding verdict. Different definitions foreground different parts of the same system.

## 🦠 Why the awkward cases matter

| Case | What makes it life-like? | What is missing or disputed? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fire | Growth, fuel consumption, persistence through energy flow | An inherited information system supporting cumulative evolution |
| Crystal | Ordered growth; some defects can propagate | Integrated metabolism, self-maintenance and open-ended biological evolution |
| Dormant seed or spore | Retains an organised cellular system that can resume activity | Detectable metabolism may be extremely low during dormancy |
| Virus | A genome, replication in hosts and Darwinian evolution | Independent cellular machinery and metabolism |
| Prion | A protein conformation that can template further copies | A cell, a genome and a self-maintaining metabolism |

Viruses are especially informative because their dependence is so complete. A viral particle does not make its own proteins or generate its own ATP. Inside a suitable host, its genome redirects cellular machinery into producing more virus. Whether the word *alive* applies depends partly on whether we classify the particle, its entire reproductive cycle or the infected system.

Prions demonstrate something subtler: transmissible biological information need not always be a DNA sequence. A protein's shape can influence the shape of other copies. That does not make a prion a miniature organism; it makes [protein folding](proteins-and-folding.md) much more consequential than “getting the right shape” sounds.

## 🌳 Common ancestry is not the origin of life

All known cellular life shares deep biochemical similarities, including ribosomes and a nearly universal genetic code. These support common ancestry. **LUCA**, the last universal common ancestor, names the ancestral lineage or population from which surviving cellular lineages descend.

LUCA was not necessarily the first organism, the only organism alive at the time or one uniquely identifiable cell. Earlier lineages could have existed and disappeared without leaving descendants we can recognise.

A [2024 reconstruction by Moody and colleagues](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-024-02461-1) inferred a surprisingly complex LUCA around 4.2 billion years ago. That estimate depends on molecular clocks, fossil calibrations and assumptions about gene histories. It is a model-based reconstruction, not a dated fossil of the ancestor. The stronger general lesson is that common ancestry and life's first emergence are different questions.

## 🔭 What the uncertainty lets us ask

If we search only for DNA, we are searching for life with familiar machinery. If we search only for chemical complexity, we risk mistaking nonliving chemistry for biology. A useful search therefore combines evidence: energy use, organised chemistry, environmental context and signs of reproduction or sustained disequilibrium.

We do not yet know which features of Earth life are universal necessities and which are historical choices. That is the connection between the everyday question “what is alive?” and [astrobiology](astrobiology.md). The uncertainty is productive when it helps us design better observations.
