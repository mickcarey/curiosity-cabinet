# Inheritance: Why Families Resemble Each Other 🧬

You have your mother's eyes, your father's nose, and apparently your grandfather's talent for losing keys. Which of those actually came through your genes? Family resemblance is easy to notice and surprisingly hard to untangle.

**Biological inheritance** means passing genetic information from parents to offspring. That information travels in **DNA**, short for **deoxyribonucleic acid**, a long molecule with information stored in its chemical sequence. A **gene** is a region of DNA used to make a working molecule, often a protein. Proteins build structures and carry out many of the jobs inside cells, the small living units that make up your body.

DNA is packaged into **chromosomes**. Most human body cells carry two corresponding copies of each chromosome, originally supplied by the two biological parents. Eggs and sperm, called **gametes**, usually carry one set each. When they join during **fertilisation**, the two sets come together.

The interesting part is the shuffle. While eggs and sperm form, corresponding chromosomes exchange sections and separate. Each reproductive cell receives a different combination. You inherit material from your parents, but not a little ready-made parent.

The underlying machinery is explained in [DNA and chromosomes](dna-and-chromosomes.md).

## 🟢 What Mendel's peas actually explain

An **allele** is a version of a DNA sequence at a particular location. An individual's combination of alleles is part of their **genotype**; an observable characteristic is a **phenotype**.

A **trait** is a characteristic, such as a flower colour. For a simple trait, call its two alleles A and a. A parent labelled Aa has one of each. Two Aa parents can produce AA, Aa or aa offspring because each passes on one allele. Across many offspring, the expected proportions are one AA, two Aa and one aa: a 1:2:1 ratio. This follows the separation of allele pairs described by Gregor Mendel, a monk whose pea plants turned out to have rather a lot to say about heredity. If A is completely dominant, AA and Aa have the same measured phenotype, giving a 3:1 phenotypic ratio.

Those are probabilities across many offspring. Four children are not obliged to organise themselves into a tidy textbook diagram.

**Dominance describes the relationship between alleles for a particular phenotype.** It does not mean an allele is stronger, better or more common. A working gene copy may produce enough of an **enzyme**, a molecule that speeds a reaction, to mask the effect of a non-working copy. For another trait, both copies may contribute visibly.

In incomplete dominance, an organism with two different alleles—a **heterozygote**—has an intermediate result. In codominance, both contributions can be detected: the A and B alleles of the ABO blood-group system provide a familiar example.

## 🧩 Most traits involve more than one switch

Height, pigmentation and many physiological traits are influenced by numerous genetic variants. Each may have a small effect, with the final outcome also depending on development and environment.

Several complications matter:

- **Linkage:** nearby DNA variants tend to be inherited together unless recombination separates them.
- **Pleiotropy:** one gene can influence several traits.
- **Epistasis:** the effect of one variant can depend on variants elsewhere.
- **Penetrance:** people carrying a variant do not always show its associated phenotype.
- **Variable expressivity:** those who show it may do so to different degrees.

Genes act through molecules and cells. Whether a variant changes a protein, alters when a gene is active, or affects a developmental signal matters more than calling it “the gene for” a whole human characteristic. [Gene regulation](gene-expression-and-regulation.md) explains why the same DNA can behave differently in different tissues.

## 📊 Heritability is about variation in a population

**Heritability** is a statistical way of asking why members of a group differ. How much of the variation in their height, for example, is associated with genetic differences? The answer applies to that population—the group studied—in those conditions.

Broad-sense heritability includes all genetic contributions to that variation. Narrow-sense heritability focuses on additive genetic effects, the contributions estimated by adding the effects of individual variants: the component especially useful for predicting resemblance across generations and responses to selection.

Neither measure divides an individual into percentages. A height heritability estimate of 80% would not mean that 80% of your centimetres came from genes.

The environment changes the calculation. If everyone receives similar nutrition, nutritional differences explain relatively little of the remaining height variation. Genetic differences can then account for a large share, even though nutrition remains essential for everyone. Change the range of environments and the estimate can change.

High heritability therefore does not mean a trait cannot respond to an intervention. Nor does heritability within one population explain the causes of a difference between populations. Those are separate questions requiring separate evidence.

## 🌦️ Genes work in environments

A **reaction norm** describes the observable outcomes the same genetic makeup can produce in different environments. A plant might grow tall in moist soil and remain short in dry soil. Another genotype might respond differently, even reversing which plant grows taller.

That is a genotype–environment interaction. It is one reason “nature versus nurture” is such an awkward question: development is an interaction from the beginning. Cells need both inherited machinery and conditions in which to use it.

Family and twin studies can estimate components of resemblance, but their interpretation depends on assumptions about shared environments, mating patterns and the populations sampled. Families pass down meals, housing, habits and resources as well as chromosomes. The statistical problem is separating things that arrive together.

## 👪 Why siblings differ

Biological full siblings share, on average, about half their **autosomal** DNA **identical by descent**: those stretches came from the same copies in their parents. Autosomes are the chromosomes other than sex chromosomes. This does not mean half their total DNA sequence is different; humans share the overwhelming majority of their sequence anyway.

Recombination makes the inherited segments a mosaic, and the exact shared fraction varies. Each sibling also develops in a different sequence of conditions and experiences.

Some inheritance patterns depart from the simplest autosomal model. Variants on sex chromosomes have their own transmission patterns. Mitochondria, compartments that help cells use food energy, have their own DNA, usually inherited through the egg. For **imprinted genes**, chemical marks make gene activity depend partly on which parent supplied the copy. These mechanisms are specific, not a general licence to claim that every parental experience is genetically passed on.

## 🔎 Finding variants is not the same as explaining a trait

A **genome** is a complete set of genetic material. Genome-wide association studies compare DNA variation with measured traits across many people, looking for versions that tend to occur alongside a particular characteristic. They can identify regions associated with a trait, including regions controlling gene activity.

An associated marker may be travelling alongside the causal variant rather than causing anything itself. Groups with different ancestry may also have different environments. Researchers need to account for those connections and for how the trait was measured.

Polygenic scores combine effects from many variants. Their predictive performance depends on the trait, the study population and the setting in which they are used. A score trained in one ancestry group or environment may transfer poorly to another.

The useful picture is neither destiny nor randomness: inherited differences influence probabilities through biological mechanisms.
