# From Mouth to Mitochondria ⚡

Breakfast is not fuel. Not yet. It is a kit of parts, still wearing the architecture of a plant or an animal, and your job is to take it apart far enough that a mitochondrion can be interested.

A calorie, as [chapter 07](07-what-a-calorie-actually-is.md) argued, is a physics unit with a food-label day job. This chapter is what happens after the number: the disassembly line, the two postal services, the customs house, and the little rotary engines that turn a sugar into the only currency a muscle will actually spend.

A body hydrolyses peptide bonds thousands of times a day, usually while thinking about something else. The machinery does not need anyone's attention. It does need this tour, because “I ate some food” is about as informative as “the band played some notes.”

If you came here for training zones, VO₂ max, or how not to bonk at kilometre thirty: that is already in [The Long Game](../📰-articles/health-fitness-endurance.md). This is the chemistry underneath, not a training plan. The sport chapter tells you which engine you are in. This one tells you what an engine is.

## The Disassembly Line 🏭

Digestion is not a cauldron. It is a factory with stations, and each station is picky.

**Mouth.** Teeth increase surface area, which is the entire game in slow motion. Saliva wets the bolus and adds **salivary amylase**, which starts nicking starch into shorter sugars. **Lingual lipase** tags along for fats, a small opening act. You could skip this station with a smoothie. You would still have to pay later.

**Stomach.** A muscular bag that is also a chemical plant. Hydrochloric acid unfolds proteins and kills a lot of what should not have been lunch. **Pepsin** starts cutting proteins into peptides. **Gastric lipase** continues the fat work. Churning is not a personality trait of the stomach. It is emulsification with extra violence. What leaves is **chyme**: acidic, half-demolished, ready for the small intestine to complain about.

**Small intestine.** This is the main hall. The pancreas dumps a toolkit into the duodenum: **pancreatic amylase**, the proteases **trypsin** and **chymotrypsin** (shipped as zymogens so they do not digest the pancreas on the way out), **carboxypeptidase**, **pancreatic lipase** plus its sidekick **colipase**, nucleases. The liver contributes **bile**, stored in the gallbladder, which is not an enzyme. Bile salts are amphiphiles: greasy on one face, wet on the other. They break fat droplets into **micelles**, which is how a water-based gut gets a purchase on an oil. Without bile, lipase is a drummer with no kit.

**Brush border.** The last cuts happen on the lining itself. **Maltase**, **sucrase**, **lactase** split the remaining disaccharides. Peptidases finish proteins into amino acids. If lactase is off-duty, lactose continues downstream and the microbiome throws a party you will hear about.

**Large intestine.** Water reclamation. Fibre. Microbes. The station the Atwater factors keep underestimating. Transit time here is measured in hours to a day-plus, not minutes. Slow is the point. Fermentation needs a residence.

Notice the pattern. You almost never absorb a steak, a starch grain, or a triglyceride. You absorb **monosaccharides, amino acids, fatty acids, monoglycerides, vitamins, salts**. Digestion is controlled demolition. Absorption is customs. Everything after that is metabolism.

Gastric emptying is the tempo. Liquids leave faster than solids; fats linger; fibre and protein slow the queue. That is not a moral ranking. It is plumbing. A glucose drink and a bowl of lentils can contain similar labelled carbohydrate and produce very different blood-sugar stories because one of them is still in the waiting room. The disassembly line has a speed setting, and the food, not just the calorie, turns the knob.

## Enzymes Are The Band 🎸

[Chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) already said enzymes are how chemistry happens in time for dinner. Digestion is that claim with a seating chart.

Each enzyme is a specialist. Amylase wants α-1,4 links in starch. Pepsin wants proteins in acid. Trypsin wants different peptide bonds from chymotrypsin. Lipase wants the outer esters of a triglyceride, and it wants them at an oil–water interface that bile has already made reasonable. Lactase wants one disaccharide that a lot of adult humans have stopped bothering to keep.

They are not “digestive juices” in the Victorian sense, a vague vitality poured on food. They are **proteins that hold a shape**, often with metal jewellery from chapter 02, that make one reaction fast and leave the neighbouring reaction alone. Heat, pH, and the wrong partner unfold them. That is why the stomach is acid and the duodenum is immediately alkalised by pancreatic bicarbonate: different bands, different rooms.

Bile deserves a second look because it is the exception that teaches the rule. It does not cut bonds. It **changes the physical state of the substrate** so that an enzyme can reach it. Cooking does a related trick before the food even arrives. Wrangham’s fire, from the previous chapter, is an enzyme you keep in the kitchen.

Bile is also recycled with a stinginess that would impress a finance department. Bile salts, having done their emulsifying, are reabsorbed in the ileum and sent back to the liver — **enterohepatic circulation**. A pool of a few grams cycles multiple times per meal. Lose the terminal ileum, or bind the bile with a resin, and fat absorption drops, fat-soluble vitamins start missing appointments, and the colon gets a detergent it did not order. The factory reuses its soap.

A last enzyme fact worth stealing: they are shipped inactive on purpose. Trypsinogen becomes trypsin only when **enteropeptidase** on the duodenal lining nicks it. Trypsin then activates the others. The cascade is a safety interlock. Pancreatitis is, in part, what happens when the interlock fails and the band starts playing inside the green room.

## A Gut The Size Of A Small Suburb, Not A Tennis Court 🏘️

Textbooks love to say the inner surface of the gut is a tennis court, 200 to 300 square metres. It is a great image. It is also, according to the people who actually measured biopsies, wrong.

[Helander and Fändriks (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24694282/) put the total mucosal surface of the adult digestive tract at about **32 square metres**. Most of that is small intestine. The colon is about 2. Mouth, oesophagus, and stomach together are less than 1. Their punchline: not a tennis lawn. **Half a badminton court.**

Still enormous. A simple tube a few metres long and a couple of centimetres across would be a fraction of a square metre. Folds (**plicae circulares**), finger-like **villi**, and the **microvilli** of the brush border multiply the area by something like sixty to a hundred and twenty in the small intestine. That is the suburb: lots of street frontage on a small block.

Why the correction matters: a tennis-court gut would be a gigantic exposed surface for toxins and microbes. The real number is still plenty for absorption and a bit more modest about risk. The image you want is not Wimbledon. It is a dense postcode of one-cell-thick streets, each street with a blood capillary and a lymphatic capillary running behind the shops.

Those shops have doors with names. Glucose rides **SGLT1**, a sodium-coupled cotransporter, which is why a pinch of salt in an oral rehydration mix is not folklore: sodium and glucose pull each other across. Fructose uses **GLUT5**. Many of the sugars then leave the cell into the blood via **GLUT2**. Amino acids have their own families of transporters, often shared in overlapping, slightly chaotic ways. The brush border is not a sponge. It is a set of turnstiles. If a turnstile is missing — lactase, or a rare amino-acid transporter — the cargo continues downstream and becomes someone else’s problem.

## Two Postal Services: Blood Versus Lymph 📬

Here is the split the school diagram usually mumbles.

**Water-soluble cargo** — glucose, galactose, fructose, amino acids, small peptides, minerals, water-soluble vitamins, short- and medium-chain fatty acids — crosses the enterocyte and drops into **blood capillaries**. Those capillaries drain to the **hepatic portal vein**, which is a dedicated road to the liver. Almost everything you absorb as “food” reports to customs first.

**Long-chain fats and the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K** cannot loiter in watery blood as naked oil. Inside the enterocyte they are rebuilt into triglycerides and packed, with cholesterol and phospholipid, into **chylomicrons**: lipoprotein particles with a protein wrapper, notably apoB-48. Chylomicrons are too big for blood capillaries. They enter **lacteals**, the lymphatic capillaries in the villi. The milky lymph is called **chyle**. It rides the intestinal lymphatics to the **thoracic duct** and dumps into the **left subclavian vein**, near the heart.

Fats, in other words, **skip the liver’s first pass**. They go systemic, get nibbled by **lipoprotein lipase** in the capillaries of muscle and adipose tissue, and only the remnants report to the liver later. That is why a fatty meal can turn plasma cloudy. It is also why some drugs are designed to hitch a ride on chylomicrons: same loophole, on purpose.

Short- and medium-chain fatty acids are the exception among fats. They are small enough and soluble enough to take the portal road like respectable glucose. Coconut-oil folklore lives in that chemical distinction and then usually overstays.

If you like a sports picture: glucose and amino acids go down the tunnel and through the dressing room. Long-chain fats sneak out the loading dock, join the main road by the stadium, and get stopped by security later.

Cholesterol and plant sterols hitch on the same fat ride, which is why a bile-acid binder or a cholesterol-absorption blocker can change blood lipids without you “burning” anything extra. The postal service is the policy.

## The Liver As Customs 🛃

The portal vein is not a courtesy. It is a policy.

The liver sees a pulse of glucose and decides whether to burn it, stash it as **glycogen**, or turn the overflow toward fat. It sees amino acids and decides whether they become liver protein, plasma proteins, glucose (gluconeogenesis), or urea. It sees fructose, which skips some of the checkpoints glucose uses, and handles it with a slightly different, more “just metabolise this” attitude that is interesting and easy to oversell. It detoxifies, conjugates, stores iron, makes bile, and generally behaves like a border post that also runs a factory.

First-pass metabolism is why some things you swallow barely reach the rest of you, and why a nutrient in the portal blood is not the same event as a nutrient in an artery in your calf. The liver is allowed to skim. That is not a conspiracy against your breakfast. That is the organ that has to keep blood glucose in a band narrow enough that your brain does not have a public incident.

Glycogen in liver is the communal store, released as glucose for the whole body. Glycogen in muscle is a private lockbox: muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase, so it cannot export free glucose. The endurance note already uses that fact when it talks about bonking. Here it is just geography. Two stores, two keys.

The liver also runs the **Cori cycle**: lactate in from muscle, glucose out. That is not waste disposal. That is a courier service between a tissue that needed ATP in a hurry and an organ that can afford the gluconeogenic bill. Alcohol, arriving from the gut in the same portal blood, jumps the queue. Ethanol metabolism hogs NAD⁺, which is why a drinking session can stall other liver chemistry and why “empty calories” is almost the right insult: seven Atwater calories, plus a customs house that has to drop its other clipboards.

## Glycolysis, Krebs, And The Chain: How A Sugar Becomes A Charge 🔋

Now the cargo is inside a cell. The calorie still is not spent. Spending means making **ATP**.

ATP is a nucleotide with three phosphates in a row that would rather not be a row. [Chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) called phosphorus the energy clerk. The useful trick is not that the bonds are magic. It is that the cell keeps ATP/ADP far from equilibrium, so that snipping the terminal phosphate can be coupled to work: pumping, assembling, contracting, thinking. You store almost none of it. Muscle has a couple of seconds of ATP on hand. **Phosphocreatine** can refill that pool for another handful of seconds by donating a phosphate, which is the phosphagen system in the [endurance note](../📰-articles/health-fitness-endurance.md) — the sprinter’s friend, the first two strokes of an all-out paddle. After that you are in glycolysis, then, if there is oxygen and time, in the mitochondria. Same ATP. Different refill desks. This chapter stays at the desks. The sport chapter stays on the track.

### Glycolysis: the anaerobic opening act

In the cytosol, glucose is split to two **pyruvate**. Net yield: **2 ATP** and **2 NADH**. No oxygen required. Ancient. Fast. Rude about efficiency. If oxygen is scarce, pyruvate becomes **lactate** so that NADH can be recycled to NAD⁺ and glycolysis can continue. Two ATP per glucose is a poor wage, but it is a wage you can collect in seconds.

Lactate is not a toxin and not a dead end. It is a shuttle. Other fibres, the heart, and the liver can take it and oxidise it or rebuild glucose. The burning in a hard interval is metabolism at high throughput, with hydrogen ions in the mix; “lactic acid” as the villain is a story that has had a good run and should retire. The endurance article already made that correction for sport. Same chemistry, quieter setting.

### The link reaction

Pyruvate enters mitochondria, loses a carbon as CO₂, and becomes **acetyl-CoA**, attached to coenzyme A. Another NADH. Two pyruvates per glucose, so two acetyl-CoA. This is the turnstile. From here, the carbons are heading for CO₂.

### Krebs (citric acid cycle, TCA): the roundabout

Acetyl-CoA (two carbons) joins **oxaloacetate** (four) to make citrate (six). Around the cycle, two CO₂ come off, oxaloacetate is regenerated, and the take per turn is **3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, and 1 GTP** (as good as ATP). Two turns per glucose.

Krebs is not “where the energy is made.” Krebs is where the remaining electrons are loaded onto carriers, and where a lot of other metabolism plugs in: fats enter as acetyl-CoA after **beta-oxidation**; some amino acids enter as cycle intermediates. It is a hub, not a shrine. Hans Krebs worked it out in the 1930s with minced pigeon muscle, which is a sentence that should be on a plaque. The cycle is older than pigeons. LUCA, back in [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md), was already in this neighbourhood: a chemistry that could run a loop, dump CO₂, and keep a carrier charged.

The CO₂ you breathe out is, in large part, this cycle plus the link reaction. A calorie that was a sandwich becomes, if you fully oxidise it, warm water, carbon dioxide, and ATP. The sandwich does not contain ATP. It contains the *possibility* of ATP, if the factory runs.

### The electron-transport chain: the waterfall

NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The electrons fall toward oxygen. The complexes use that fall to pump **protons** (hydrogen ions, the crowd surfers from chapter 02) from the matrix into the intermembrane space. Oxygen sits at the bottom as the final acceptor and becomes **water**. That is what breathing is for. Not “air into the blood” as a vibe. **O₂ as the electron sink** so the chain can keep running.

The chain is four complexes and a couple of shuttles, not a single gadget:

- **Complex I** takes electrons from NADH and pumps protons. FADH₂ skips it, which is why FADH₂ is worth less ATP.
- **Complex II** is also a Krebs enzyme (succinate dehydrogenase). It feeds FADH₂-level electrons in without pumping.
- **Ubiquinone** (coenzyme Q) and **cytochrome c** are the mobile posties between complexes.
- **Complex III** pumps. **Complex IV** pumps, and is the one that reduces oxygen.

A typical accounting: about ten protons pumped per NADH, about six per FADH₂, and about four protons’ worth of work to make and export one ATP. Divide and you get the modern **~2.5 and ~1.5**, not the old 3 and 2. The fractions are a confession that this is chemistry, not a vending machine.

Peter Mitchell’s **chemiosmotic** idea, which looked like heresy in 1961 and won him a Nobel in 1978, is that the energy is stored as a **proton gradient**, not as a high-energy chemical sprayed between the chain and ATP. The membrane is a dam. The protons are the reservoir. **ATP synthase** is the turbine: protons flow back in, the machine rotates, ADP plus phosphate becomes ATP.

School numbers still say 36 or 38 ATP per glucose. Modern accounting, with real proton stoichiometry and the cost of shuttling cytosolic NADH into mitochondria, is about **30 to 32** in an idealised eukaryotic cell, and less in a living one that leaks protons on purpose. The exact integer is a teaching convenience. The architecture is the point: **substrate-level phosphorylation** (the handful of ATP made directly in glycolysis and Krebs) versus **oxidative phosphorylation** (the majority, made by the turbine).

Most of the energy in the sandwich still becomes **heat**. ATP is a high-grade slice, not the whole pie. You are a warm animal because the factory is inefficient on purpose, in the thermodynamic sense: you stay out of equilibrium by dumping disorder into the room. Schrödinger’s radiator, from chapter 01, is this paragraph in a coat. A hard run makes you hot because the engine’s waste heat has nowhere else to go. That is not a bug in endurance. That is the bill being paid in the original unit: a calorie, heat, water, a degree.

Fats skip glycolysis. A fatty acid is activated (that step *costs* ATP), carted into the mitochondrion as an acyl-carnitine, then chopped two carbons at a time — **beta-oxidation** — into acetyl-CoA, plus a NADH and an FADH₂ per chop. Those acetyl-CoA units join Krebs like anyone else. More reduced carbons, more reducing equivalents per gram, which is why the bomb and Atwater both give fat about 9. You need more oxygen per ATP from fat, which is one reason high-intensity work leans on carbohydrate: the chain can only run as fast as oxygen arrives. Again: sport systems live next door. This is why those systems have the shapes they have.

When carbohydrate is scarce, the liver can divert acetyl-CoA into **ketone bodies** and export them as a water-soluble fat-like fuel, especially for brain. That is a physiological state, not a personality, and not a chapter of commandments. The chemistry is just: Krebs needs oxaloacetate; oxaloacetate can be drained toward glucose; leftover acetyl-CoA has to go somewhere. Somewhere is acetone, acetoacetate, and β-hydroxybutyrate, which is a sentence that has launched a thousand cookbooks and one actual metabolic pathway.

## Oxygen As Gift And Rust 🫁

[Chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md) called oxygen the greedy one. Here is the deal in the engine room.

**The gift.** With oxygen, a glucose that was worth 2 ATP in glycolysis is worth on the order of 30. Fat becomes a vast slow tank instead of an awkward grease. The electron-transport chain can run. Eukaryotic life got loud. Brains got expensive. Endurance became a hobby.

**The rust.** Oxygen wants electrons so much that it sometimes grabs them in the wrong place. The chain, especially at complexes I and III, leaks **superoxide** and other **reactive oxygen species**. Mitochondria are a major source of that mess. Antioxidant enzymes mop it up; they do not get a perfect score. This is one of the leading suspects in ageing, inflammation, and the general category of “the engine that burns also scorches the paint.”

Aerobic life made a deal with a pyromaniac: you can have our leftovers if you power the house. The house sometimes burns. We call that ageing, among other words.

There is a safety valve. Inner membranes leak protons on purpose, uncoupling oxidation from ATP synthesis as heat (brown fat is the celebrity; **UCP1** the named protein) and as a way to keep the gradient from sitting so high that ROS production spikes. Inefficiency is not always a bug. Sometimes it is how you stop the dam from cracking.

Copper sits in cytochrome c oxidase, the last complex, the one that actually reduces oxygen. Iron sits in the haem of the cytochromes and in the iron–sulfur clusters that pass electrons along. Chapter 02’s jewellery is not decoration. Take the copper or the iron away and the chain is a guitar with no strings: it still looks like an engine. It is not, in any useful sense, an engine.

Cyanide and carbon monoxide are interesting here for the worst reason: they bind that last complex, or the haem that delivers oxygen to it, and the waterfall stops. You can have air in the room and still asphyxiate at the mitochondrion. Breathing is only the delivery. The chemistry is the point.

## The Microbiome As A Second Organ 🦠

You are not the only one eating.

The colon is a fermenter. Bacteria, archaea, and their friends take the fibre, resistant starch, and leftover peptides the small intestine declined, and they turn them into **short-chain fatty acids** — mostly acetate, propionate, and butyrate, often in a rough **60:20:20** ratio — plus gas and heat. Butyrate is the preferred fuel of colonocytes, perhaps **60–70%** of their energy. Propionate is a gluconeogenic substrate at the liver. Acetate travels further, even, in small amounts, across the blood–brain barrier.

Estimates for how much of *your* daily energy this is usually sit around **5–10%** on a typical Western diet, more if you eat like a gorilla. That is the “cut” from chapter 07, now with a face. It is also not just a cut. SCFAs bind receptors (FFAR2, FFAR3), prod L-cells to release **GLP-1** and **PYY**, talk to the vagus, and change immune tone. The microbiome is a metabolic organ that also runs a signalling network. Calling it a second brain is poetry. Calling it a second organ is increasingly just anatomy.

The germ-free mouse is the unkind thought experiment. Animals raised without microbes need more food to maintain the same weight; put a conventional microbiome in and energy harvest rises, body fat can rise, and the metabolic conversation changes. Turn that observation into “eat this yoghurt and become a new person” and you have left the evidence. The direction of the effect is real. The magnitude in a given human, on a given Tuesday, is not a number you should buy from a start-up.

We do not know how to rank “which microbes, exactly, for which human, for which outcome” without a lot of handwaving. The industry of stool tests and proprietary powders would like you not to notice that. What is solid: **fibre that reaches the colon is not wasted by default**, the host–microbe contract is real, and a calorie that becomes butyrate in a colonocyte is a different event from a calorie that became plasma glucose at the portal vein.

Antibiotics, unsurprisingly, are a metabolic intervention wearing an infection-control hat. They change the fermenter. Sometimes that is a rounding error. Sometimes it is a month of unusual stool and a shifted SCFA profile. We are still bad at predicting which.

Holobionts — we are ecosystems wearing a nametag — get a proper sitting in a later arc. Here they are just sitting in the last station of the disassembly line, taking a legitimate wage.

## What The Fights Are Usually About 🥊

Once you have this map, a lot of internet nutrition is two true facts having a knife fight.

One camp says energy is energy, eat less, move more, the first law will collect. They are right that you cannot violate conservation, and they are wrong when they talk as if the label were the mitochondrion.

The other camp says insulin, or seed oils, or “carbs,” or the hour on the clock, is the real lever, and calories are a distraction. They are right that **route and hormone and food form change intake, storage, and the next meal**, and they are wrong when they talk as if joules had been repealed.

A third camp wants a microbial villain or hero. Sometimes a microbe is doing something. Usually it is doing something *and* you are still eating food.

The evidence, as opposed to the merch, looks like this: metabolizable energy is a decent first approximation; protein and fibre change the approximation; cooking and grinding change it again; hormones and the brain change how much of the approximation you reach for tomorrow; adaptation changes how much you spend. That is several knobs. It is not a reason to throw the stereo out, and it is not a reason to insist there is only a volume dial.

[Chapter 07](07-what-a-calorie-actually-is.md) was the unit. This chapter is why the unit has to go through a factory. The factory has opinions. Physics still wins at the end of the day, because physics always does. It just wins after a lot of biochemistry has taken a cut.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **The tribal food fights are louder than the evidence.** Carbs versus fat versus protein as moral teams; “insulin is the only lever”; “a calorie is a calorie” as a shutting-up device. The chemistry is more boring and more interesting: different macronutrients take different routes, pay different thermic costs, and feed different signals. Insulin is a real hormone that does real work after carbohydrate (and some amino acids) arrive; it is not a demon and not the only clerk in the building. Ketosis is a real pathway; it is not a personality. Energy still counts. The route still counts. Neither side gets the whole stadium. If a claim needs you to forget the first law *or* forget that a nut is not a flask, it is a campaign, not a mechanism.
- **We do not know the true ATP yield in a living human cell.** Thirty-two is a model. Proton leak, shuttle choice, and tissue type all move the number.
- **How much of gut-brain signalling is SCFAs, hormones, immune tone, or the vagus** is a live argument, not a TED-talk conclusion.
- **Lactate’s full job list is still growing.** Fuel, signal, gluconeogenic precursor. The old “waste” story is dead. The new story is not finished.
- **Personalised digestion is real and badly mapped.** Enzyme copy number (amylase), lactase persistence, bile acid pools, transit time, and microbiome composition all change extraction. Two humans, same meal, different receipts: we can measure that more often than we can explain it.
- **We still do not know how to put this into a label.** Chapter 07’s useful lie remains the supermarket’s official truth.

If the calorie chapter was the bill, this one is the kitchen, the plumbing, and the fuse box. Food becomes molecules. Molecules become a proton gradient. The gradient becomes ATP. ATP becomes a muscle fibre twitching, a neuron gossiping, a liver deciding what to do with Tuesday.

The slogan on the yoghurt never had to survive any of that. You do. That is the whole joke, and the whole science: a unit invented to warm water, printed on a snack, asked to explain an animal that is a process, not a flask. The process has a mouth, a suburb of villi, two postal services, a customs house, and a turbine older than bones. Once you can see the process, the tribal fights get quieter. Not gone. Quieter. Which is as much as chemistry usually manages.
