# Life in the Ocean 🌊

Most of the ocean is dark. So if much of its food begins with sunlight, how does anything down there get dinner?

**Ocean ecology** asks how marine organisms interact with one another and their surroundings. A **habitat** is a place that provides living conditions and resources. In the sea, moving down can change that habitat as dramatically as travelling across a continent: light fades, pressure rises and food becomes harder to find.

Most marine **food webs**, the linked paths of who eats whom, ultimately depend on **photosynthesis** near the surface. This is the use of light energy to build sugars and other biological materials from carbon dioxide and water. Much of the ocean lies below that illuminated layer, so understanding how food reaches the dark is central to understanding ocean life.

## 💡 Light creates a shallow productive layer

Water absorbs and scatters light. The depth receiving enough light for substantial photosynthesis depends on clarity, season and location. In cloudy, particle-filled coastal water it may be shallow; in clear open water it can extend much deeper.

Conventional depth zones provide useful orientation:

| Zone | Approximate depth | Broad conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epipelagic | Surface–200 m | Includes much of the sunlit water |
| Mesopelagic | 200–1,000 m | Diminishing sunlight; generally insufficient for net photosynthetic production |
| Bathypelagic | 1,000–4,000 m | No sunlight; usually cold |
| Abyssopelagic | 4,000–6,000 m | Deep, dark open water |
| Hadal | Below about 6,000 m | Trenches and their associated environments |

These boundaries classify depth, not perfectly uniform biological conditions. The **euphotic zone** is the layer with enough light to support substantial photosynthesis; its lower boundary moves with water clarity and light conditions. “Pelagic” in the table refers to the water column, rather than the seabed.

Red wavelengths disappear relatively quickly. In dim deep water, a red animal can be difficult to see because little red light remains to reflect. **Bioluminescence**, light produced by chemical reactions in organisms, supplies another source of light, used in signalling, concealment and hunting. The deep sea has excellent lighting effects and very little general illumination.

## 🌿 Small producers, rapid turnover

**Plankton** are organisms carried along by currents. **Phytoplankton** are the photosynthetic members: they include **diatoms**, microscopic algae with silica shells, and **cyanobacteria**, light-using bacteria.

Together they make roughly as much new biological material each year as land vegetation does, after subtracting what they use for their own energy needs. That is **net primary production**. Their total living mass, or **biomass**, is much smaller than that of land plants. Small workforce, astonishing output.

The difference is turnover. Trees retain large amounts of carbon in wood for years or decades. Many plankton populations grow, are consumed and are replaced quickly. A small standing population can therefore support a large annual flow of production.

Light is only one requirement. Nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and, for diatoms, silicon can limit growth. Large regions with abundant nitrate still have limited phytoplankton production because another requirement, often iron, is scarce.

The biochemical process is explained in [plant biology](plant-biology.md), although many marine producers are not plants in the usual biological classification.

## 🌡️ Mixing controls access to nutrients

Sunlight warms the surface, often creating a less dense layer above colder water. A **thermocline** is a region where temperature changes rapidly with depth; **salinity**, the amount of dissolved salt, also affects density. Layers of different densities can resist mixing: this is **stratification**.

Stratification can keep phytoplankton near light while limiting the upward supply of nutrients. Mixing can replenish nutrients, but can also carry cells into darker water. Productivity depends on the balance, not simply “more mixing is better”.

**Upwelling** brings deeper water towards the surface and can support highly productive regions. Seasonal changes in light and mixing help explain spring **blooms**, rapid increases in phytoplankton, in many temperate seas.

Currents also distribute heat, oxygen and **larvae**, the young stages of many marine animals. A suitable habitat may lack a species because its offspring rarely arrive. That is one reason [predicting marine habitats](coasts/predicting-marine-habitats.md) requires more than matching temperature.

## ❄️ Food reaches the dark in several ways

Dead plankton, faecal pellets, mucus and other particles sink as **marine snow**. Microbes and animals consume and transform this material during descent. Only a fraction reaches the deep seafloor.

Many organisms also migrate vertically, feeding nearer the surface at night and retreating during daylight. Their movement and waste transport carbon between depths. Large carcasses provide more concentrated, occasional food supplies.

These processes contribute to the **biological carbon pump**. Carbon incorporated into biological molecules near the surface can be transported into deeper water and sediments, with storage times depending on where it is transformed and how circulation returns it.

## 🔥 Vents provide another primary energy source

**Hydrothermal vents** release seawater heated by hot rock beneath the seabed. **Cold seeps** release fluids containing chemicals such as methane without the same heating. At both, microbes can obtain energy when chemicals in those fluids react with substances in the surrounding water.

Some use this energy to build biological material from carbon dioxide: **chemosynthesis**. Animals can eat the microbes or house them as **symbionts**, close living partners. A tube worm with chemical-feeding partners has access to a food supply no salad could provide down here.

Vent animals inhabit tolerable mixing zones, not the hottest fluid emerging from the seafloor. The changes in temperature and chemistry create a patchwork of conditions over short distances.

This is primary production without local sunlight. It does not mean every vent ecosystem is entirely independent of surface photosynthesis: many depend on oxygen or other oxidants ultimately linked to surface processes. These reactions transfer **electrons**, tiny charged particles, from one substance to another. Both a suitable source and a suitable recipient must be available.

Vents are also studied as possible environments for life's origins. Their existence today demonstrates a functioning habitat; it does not prove that life first arose there. [Open questions in biology](open-questions-in-biology.md) keeps those claims separate.

## 🧱 Pressure and oxygen shape the inhabitants

Pressure rises by roughly one atmosphere—the pressure exerted by air at sea level—for every ten metres of seawater depth. Deep-living organisms have molecular and physiological adaptations that allow **proteins**, molecules that perform much of a cell's work, and **membranes**, the boundaries enclosing cells and compartments, to function under those conditions.

Gas-filled spaces create particular difficulties, but pressure is not simply an external crushing force acting on an otherwise empty animal. The chemistry of cells and the behaviour of gases are central constraints.

Oxygen varies with circulation, temperature and biological consumption. Where decomposition uses oxygen faster than circulation replenishes it, low-oxygen zones can develop. They have their own communities and important effects on nutrient cycling.

A vent, a muddy plain, a rocky seamount and a trench are consequently distinct habitats. “Deep sea” locates a place vertically; it does not describe its full ecology.
