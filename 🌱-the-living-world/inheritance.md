# Inheritance: Why Families Resemble Each Other 🧬

Relatives resemble one another because they inherit some of the same DNA, often share environments, and influence one another's lives. Untangling those contributions is harder than noticing a family nose.

Inheritance begins with chromosomes. Humans usually carry two copies of each autosome, one from each biological parent. During egg and sperm formation, chromosome pairs exchange segments and separate. Each gamete receives a reshuffled set. Fertilisation brings two sets together again.

The result is a new combination, not an average of two people. The underlying machinery is explained in [DNA and chromosomes](dna-and-chromosomes.md).

## 🟢 What Mendel's peas actually explain

An **allele** is a version of a DNA sequence at a particular location. An individual's combination of alleles is part of their **genotype**; an observable characteristic is a **phenotype**.

For a simple trait with alleles A and a, two parents with genotype Aa can produce AA, Aa or aa offspring. Under ordinary Mendelian segregation, the expected proportions are 1:2:1. If A is completely dominant, AA and Aa have the same measured phenotype, giving a 3:1 phenotypic ratio.

Those are probabilities across many offspring. Four children are not obliged to organise themselves into a tidy textbook diagram.

**Dominance describes the relationship between alleles for a particular phenotype.** It does not mean an allele is stronger, better or more common. A functional copy of an enzyme gene may provide enough activity to obscure the effect of a nonfunctional copy. For another trait, both copies may contribute visibly.

In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype. In codominance, both contributions can be detected: the A and B alleles of the ABO blood-group system provide a familiar example.

## 🧩 Most traits involve more than one switch

Height, pigmentation and many physiological traits are influenced by numerous genetic variants. Each may have a small effect, with the final outcome also depending on development and environment.

Several complications matter:

- **Linkage:** nearby DNA variants tend to be inherited together unless recombination separates them.
- **Pleiotropy:** one gene can influence several traits.
- **Epistasis:** the effect of one variant can depend on variants elsewhere.
- **Penetrance:** people carrying a variant do not always show its associated phenotype.
- **Variable expressivity:** those who show it may do so to different degrees.

Genes act through molecules and cells. Whether a variant changes a protein, alters when a gene is active, or affects a developmental signal matters more than calling it “the gene for” a whole human characteristic. [Gene regulation](gene-expression-and-regulation.md) explains why the same DNA can behave differently in different tissues.

## 📊 Heritability is about variation in a population

Heritability asks how much of the **variation in a measured trait, in a particular population and environment**, can be attributed to genetic differences under a statistical model.

Broad-sense heritability includes all genetic contributions to that variation. Narrow-sense heritability focuses on additive genetic effects: the component especially useful for predicting resemblance across generations and responses to selection.

Neither measure divides an individual into percentages. A height heritability estimate of 80% would not mean that 80% of your centimetres came from genes.

The environment changes the calculation. If everyone receives similar nutrition, nutritional differences explain relatively little of the remaining height variation. Genetic differences can then account for a large share, even though nutrition remains essential for everyone. Change the range of environments and the estimate can change.

High heritability therefore does not mean a trait cannot respond to an intervention. Nor does heritability within one population explain the causes of a difference between populations. Those are separate questions requiring separate evidence.

## 🌦️ Genes work in environments

A **reaction norm** describes the phenotypes a genotype can produce across different environments. A plant might grow tall in moist soil and remain short in dry soil. Another genotype might respond differently, even reversing which plant grows taller.

That is a genotype–environment interaction. It is one reason “nature versus nurture” is such an awkward question: development is an interaction from the beginning. Cells need both inherited machinery and conditions in which to use it.

Family and twin studies can estimate components of resemblance, but their interpretation depends on assumptions about shared environments, mating patterns and the populations sampled. Families pass down meals, housing, habits and resources as well as chromosomes. The statistical problem is separating things that arrive together.

## 👪 Why siblings differ

Full siblings inherit, on average, about half their autosomal DNA **identical by descent** from the same parental copies. This does not mean half their total DNA sequence is different; humans share the overwhelming majority of their sequence anyway.

Recombination makes the inherited segments a mosaic, and the exact shared fraction varies. Each sibling also develops in a different sequence of conditions and experiences.

Some inheritance patterns depart from the simplest autosomal model. Variants on sex chromosomes have their own transmission patterns. Mitochondrial DNA is usually inherited maternally. For imprinted genes, expression depends partly on which parent supplied the copy. These mechanisms are specific, not a general licence to claim that every parental experience is genetically passed on.

## 🔎 Finding variants is not the same as explaining a trait

Genome-wide association studies compare DNA variation with measured traits across many people. They can identify regions associated with a trait, including regions controlling gene activity.

An associated marker may be travelling alongside the causal variant rather than causing anything itself. Population structure, environmental correlations and measurement choices also need careful handling.

Polygenic scores combine effects from many variants. Their predictive performance depends on the trait, the study population and the setting in which they are used. A score trained in one ancestry group or environment may transfer poorly to another.

The useful picture is neither destiny nor randomness: inherited differences influence probabilities through biological mechanisms. [Embryonic development](embryonic-development.md) shows those mechanisms building an organism; [evolution](evolution.md) follows what happens when inherited variation changes across generations.
