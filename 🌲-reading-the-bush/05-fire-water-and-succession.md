# Fire, Water, And Succession 🔥💧

Australia does not look like a postcard of England having a nervous breakdown because the climate is rude, the soils are often poor, and fire is not an accident. Fire is a process with a calendar. Water is the other process. Succession is what people call the conversation when they want it to have a last chapter.

It does not have a last chapter.

The honest reason the bush looks like this is that it is a conversation that keeps getting interrupted — by flame, by flood, by drought, by a tree falling, by a person with a match or a tractor. "Climax forest" is a tidy Victorian idea (the other Victorian) that this continent never signed.

## Four Switches, Not A Vibe 🎚️

Ross Bradstock put Australian fire regimes in a useful box: fire happens when four switches are on at once.

1. **Biomass** — is there enough stuff to burn?
2. **Availability** — is that stuff dry enough?
3. **Fire weather** — is the air in a mood?
4. **Ignition** — lightning, or us.

In dry woodlands, the limiter is often **growth**. Grass has to exist before it can burn. A drought can suppress fire by starving the fuel. In wet forests, the limiter is often **dryness and weather**. The fuel is always there. It just spends years too wet to take. Then a summer like 2009 arrives, and the cathedral burns.

That is why "wet forest never burns" is a cartoon, and "the bush always burns" is also a cartoon. The Otway gully and the box-ironbark ridge are not on the same switchboard.

Murphy and colleagues mapped the trade-off: frequent, lower-intensity fires in the monsoonal north; infrequent, high-intensity fires in the southern tall forests. Victoria's wet ash forests are in the second club. Victoria's grassy woodlands and much of the dry foothills are closer to "fuel-limited, more often." The Sunshine Coast sits in a humid subtropical argument: rainforest that hates fire, wet sclerophyll that uses it rarely, wallum heath that expects it, paperbark swamps that complicate the map.

Season is part of the switch. Southern summers are the danger months. Northern Australia burns in the dry. A Victorian prescribed-burn calendar and a Queensland one are not the same playlist.

## Resprouters Versus Seeders 🌱

When the fire has gone, the plants divide into two broad careers. Real species mix them. The cartoon is still the best field handle.

**Resprouters** keep buds in protected places: **epicormic** strands under bark, **lignotubers** at the base, sometimes buried shoots. Most eucalypts do this. The tree can look like a charcoal sculpture in September and a green bottlebrush in November. Stringybarks, ironbarks, boxes, peppermints: generally in this club. Grass trees. Many banksias. Many wattles, not all.

**Seeders** (obligate seeders, if we are being precise) are killed by a severe fire and come back from seed. The seed may be in the soil, waiting for smoke and heat, or in the canopy — **serotiny** — waiting for the capsules to open over an ashbed. Mountain ash is the celebrity seeder. Some banksias and hakeas are too. Some wattles flood the soil with hard seed and then die on the parent.

Each career has a vulnerability.

- Resprouters can be cooked if fires are too frequent and too hot, or if they are grazed to death on the regrowth. They are not immortal. They are persistent.
- Seeders can be eliminated if a second fire arrives before they have made a new seed bank. For mountain ash, that window is on the order of 15–20 years to first seed, with historical stand-replacing intervals much longer. Enright and others called the climate-change version **interval squeeze**: fires more often, growth slower, the safe window narrowing until the species falls out.

A post-fire walk is a census of careers. Green shoots on black trunks: resprouters clocking on. A carpet of seedlings, dead parents: seeders taking the bet. Nothing but weeds and a bare ridge: something went wrong, or you are too early, or the seed bank was already gone.

## Wet Gully Refuges 🪵

Fire does not paint a landscape one colour. It follows weather, slope, and fuel.

Gullies stay wetter, cooler, less windy. Rainforest and fern floors live there because fire is a visitor, not a landlord. Ridges dry first, carry fire faster, and host the sclerophyll that is built for it. After a big fire you can walk from a black ridgeline into a green gully in a hundred metres and feel like you changed planets. You changed position.

This is why "the forest burned" is a bad sentence. *Which* forest. *How hot*. *Which slope*. A low-intensity burn through box-ironbark litter is not a crown fire through stringybarked mixed species is not a stand-replacing ash-forest event. The plants know the difference. Your inference has to as well.

Water is the other refuge. Creeks, soaks, the Otway cloud-catching ridge, a wallum swale that sits wet even in a dry month. Moisture is fire's off-switch until the day it is not. Then the wet forest, having stacked decades of fuel because it could, burns in a way that makes the evening news.

Bradstock's point, again: in wet climates the limiter is availability and weather. Stack enough drought and a bad day, and the refuge fails. Myrtle beech does not resprout from that. Its seed does not want an ashbed. Cool temperate rainforest in the Otways is a fire-interval museum. Treat it that way.

## What A Post-Fire Walk Is Telling You 🚶

Besides "that was a lot of smoke."

Look for:

- **Epicormic shoots** along trunks: eucalypt resprouters, fire not quite stand-replacing for those individuals.
- **Lignotuber mops** at ground level: the plant coppicing from the base. Mallee is this as a lifestyle. Many woodland eucalypts do it as a backup.
- **Seedling carpets** of ash, acacia, or banksia: a seeder event. Note whether the parents are dead. Note the age you would guess. A second fire in this cohort is the nightmare scenario.
- **Grass-tree skirts** gone, spikes coming: fire was recent enough to cue flowering.
- **Serotinous cones** open, valves gaping: banksia or hakea has cashed the bank.
- **Unburnt gully tongues:** the refuge. Rainforest species, ferns, wet-forest herbs. This is the seed source and the memory.
- **Weeds in the ashbed:** disturbance is a job vacancy. Fire did not invent that. It opened the counter.

Time since fire is a first-class variable, as real as rainfall. A "typical" box-ironbark understorey at two years and at forty years are different rooms. A wet forest at six years after 2009 and at eighty years after 1939 are different cathedrals. If you do not ask "when did this last burn?", you are reading a sentence with the date torn off.

VicFlora's fire notes are blunt about mountain ash: without fire in a few centuries, regeneration from seed on the forest floor fails — shade, fungus, ants, browsers. The magnificent wet forest is a forest *born* of fire, and also a forest that can be killed by the wrong fire. Both are true. The continent is not obliged to be cute.

## Succession, And Why Climax Is A Bit Of A Myth 📖

School ecology liked a story: bare ground → herbs → shrubs → trees → climax. The end. Credits. A beech forest in Europe, a wet sclerophyll that "wants" to be rainforest, a grassland that "wants" to be forest.

Sometimes you can watch a drift. Wet sclerophyll understorey fills with rainforest taxa if fire stays away. Eucalypt recruitment stalls in the dark. Given a long enough peace, the eucalypts die of old age and the closed forest holds the lease until a fire resets it. On the Sunshine Coast, flooded gum over vine-forest understorey is this sliding door in action. In the Otways, myrtle beech in the gully and ash on the slope are a mosaic held by fire frequency and wetness, not a queue.

But "climax" suggests a destination the system is trying to reach. A lot of Australian vegetation is **disturbance-dependent**. Heath that is never burnt can senesce. Ash forest that is never burnt can fail to replace itself. Grassland that is never burnt or grazed can thicken. Box-ironbark that is never burnt can accumulate a shrubby understorey that is a different habitat, not a higher one.

Jackson's ecological drift and Mount's sharpening switch were Tasmanian arguments about rainforest versus button-grass versus sclerophyll. The details are local. The useful export is this: **mosaics can be stable without being a climax.** Fire frequency, soil, and water can hold alternative states next to each other for centuries. The gully is not "ahead" of the ridge. It is wetter.

Indigenous fire knowledge is the other half of the honesty. This is Country. People have been handling fire here for tens of thousands of years, with purposes that are not a hazard-reduction prescription written in 2020. A paragraph is not a substitute for that knowledge, and it should not flatten it into "they burnt everything every year" or "nobody burnt the wet forests." The regime was local, skilled, and is still being recovered, argued, and practised. If you want the real story, that is a relationship, not a citation.

## Water, Which Is Not Only Rain 💧

Fire gets the posters. Water writes the daily script.

- **Drought** turns wet forest available to burn and kills rainforest edges from the inside.
- **Flood** sorts river red gums, paperbarks, and swamp species better than any textbook.
- **Water table** tiles wallum into rises and swales.
- **Aspect** is water-by-another-name: south faces keep it, north faces spend it.
- **Drainage** from [chapter 02](02-soils-you-can-see.md) decides whether a "wet" rainfall total is actually available to roots.

A fern gully is a water story. A tea-tree dune is a water-leaving-as-fast-as-it-arrives story. Ironbark country is a water-rationing story. If you only learn fire, you will stand in a drought-killed gully and call it a fire regime.

Succession after *flood* is a real thing, just quieter on this shelf. After *clearing*, too. After *dieback*. The method is the same: what was interrupted, who has the career for this interruption, what would not recruit here now.

## Interval Squeeze, Without The Lecture ⏳

The unsolved-and-urgent bit.

If the climate makes wet fuels available more often, and ignitions are not shy, the tall seeder forests get fires too close together. Seedlings die before they seed. The stand flips toward wattle, or mountain grey gum, or something that resprouts, or weeds, or a shrubby mess that is not a cathedral. Lindenmayer's long-term work in the Central Highlands has been ringing this bell for years, along with possums, gliders, and the hollows that take a century to build.

If the climate dries the woodlands, Bradstock's model says fire may actually *decline* there because the grass does not grow. Different problem. Not no problem.

Wallum and heath have their own windows: too frequent, and the slow seeders drop out; too rare, and the canopy closes or the senescent shrubs stop replacing. There is no universal "right interval." There is a set of species with clocks, on a site with a fuel array, in a climate that is moving.

Could ≠ should applies to lighting fires, too. This chapter is literacy, not a burn plan.

## Fuel, Bark, And Why Stringybark Country Feels Different 🪵

Not all canopies carry fire the same way.

Stringybark and ribbon-bark gums are ladder fuels: fire in the litter can climb the tatters into the crown. That is one reason mixed-species foothill forest can go from "surface fire" to "everyone is on the news" faster than a tidy box woodland with grass and hard bark. Ironbark armour is not a gimmick. Smooth wet-forest ash, once the understorey dries, is a different monster because the fuel load is huge and the strategy is stand replacement.

Heath and wallum can carry fire in the shrub layer even when the trees are short. Grasslands carry it in grass, fast, when the biomass switch is on. Rainforest often will not carry it until the day the drought has emptied the sponge.

So when you read a site, ask **what would burn, and how.** A fern gully with wet litter is a fire break until it is not. A tea-tree thicket is a fuel array. A grazed box paddock with no grass is a switch off. Fuel is not a moral quality. It is physics wearing plants.

CSIRO and state fire agencies spend careers on this. Your job on a Saturday is the first inference: this vegetation is built to burn often, rarely, or almost never. Then check. Do not start a fire because a paragraph was vivid. Do not assume a city edge with no fire for forty years is "now safe." Absence of fire is a regime too.

## Water After Fire, Fire After Drought ♻️

The two interruptions talk to each other.

A hot fire on a steep Otway slope can be followed by rain and then by the soil leaving. Ash and hydrophobic sand are a bad couple. Wet-forest soils with an organic mat can still erode if the mat burned off. The first winter is part of the fire.

A drought that kills rainforest edges is a fire-pre-game. The dead fuel sits. The next ignition is not starting from the same script as a normal year. Mountain ash that regenerated after 2009, then copped another fire too soon, is this conversation gone wrong.

Floods after fire dump charcoal and sediment into creeks. That is [Reading The Water](../🌊-reading-the-water/01-tides-salt-and-the-shape-of-the-shore.md) receiving a parcel from this shelf.

For the walker: do not read a black landscape as "finished." Do not read a green gully in a black ridge as "untouched forever." Read them as different times since the last interruption, on different parts of the switchboard.

## A Post-Fire Sentence You Can Practise ✍️

> "Ridge: stringybark, epicormic, grass-tree spikes, seeder shrubs in the ash. Gully: ferns unburnt, myrtle beech intact. Inference: mixed-severity fire, wet refuge held, interval for the ridge species looks survivable if the next fire stays away long enough for the seeders."

That is a first draft. A scientist would want dates, severity maps, and species. You have a walk and a notebook. Good. Check later. The bush is patient. Your certainty does not have to be.

## Mosaics, Not A Queue 🧩

Stand on an Otway slope and you can see the argument in one view: wet gully, damp midslope, drier ridge. That is not succession-in-progress, like a band still sound-checking. It is a mosaic held by water and fire. The gully is not "more advanced." The ridge is not "degraded gully."

Same on the Sunshine Coast sandplain: paperbark swamp, wet heath, dry banksia rise. Tiles, not a ladder.

Box-ironbark does this on shorter legs: ironbark ridge, box flat, a cheat of yellow box in the hollow.

The school-story queue (herbs to climax) makes people "help" a site toward a forest it is not trying to be. A grassy woodland does not need to be filled with shrubs to graduate. A heath does not need trees to be finished. A rainforest gully does not need to be extended up the ridge to be valid.

If you remember one thing from the climax argument: **ask whether you are looking at time-since-disturbance, or at a different envelope next door.** Age and type are easy to confuse when both are green. The codebook plus the supporting cast plus the wetness underfoot will usually split them. If they will not, say "I do not know yet." That is a first-class result, not a shrug.

## Season, Intensity, Type: The Rest Of The Word "Regime" 📅

"Fire" is a blunt noun. A regime is at least:

- **Frequency** — how often
- **Intensity / severity** — how hot, how high, how much of the canopy died
- **Season** — a spring burn and an autumn burn are different gifts to different species
- **Type** — ground, shrub, crown; peat fire is another animal again

A mild autumn burn through box-ironbark litter can keep it open and still spare the hollows. A summer crown fire in stringybark mixed species is a different letter to the same postcode. A peat smoulder in a wet swamp can kill what a fast grass fire would have skipped.

Walkers over-read charcoal. Charcoal says "fire has been here." It does not say when, how hot, or whether that was good for the plants you like. Pair it with living clues: epicormic, seedlings, skirts, unburnt tongues, the age-structure of the canopy. Then go home and look up the last mapped fire if you can. Forest Fire Management Victoria and Queensland's fire history are not vibes. They are dates.

Indigenous burning, again, was not one regime. Different Country, different fuel, different purpose. If you want to understand it, that is listening, not a bullet list at the end of a chapter.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Climax is a myth with occasional local truth.** We still argue how much rainforest would expand without fire, and how much soil and climate forbid it anyway.
- **Pre-colonial fire regimes are incompletely known** at the slope scale you are standing on. Do not fake a number.
- **Interval squeeze** is a well-posed risk, not a completed prophecy. Where it has already happened, we have patches. Where it will happen, we have models.
- **Severity versus frequency** both matter, and extreme weather can override fuel age. A one-year-old prescribed burn is not a forcefield on a 47-degree day.
- **We do not know what "recovery" means** if the climate the plants recovered into is not the climate they adapted in.
