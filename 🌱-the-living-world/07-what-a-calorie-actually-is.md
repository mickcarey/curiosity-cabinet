# What a Calorie Actually Is 🔥

The number on the yoghurt is wearing a lab coat. It is not, strictly speaking, lying. It is doing something more interesting and more annoying: reporting a physics measurement as if it were a promise your body signed.

A calorie is a unit of heat. Food is a chemical store. A human is a leaky, adaptive, microbe-haunted engine that spends some of that store on chewing, cooking, fermenting, fidgeting, and arguing with itself. The label is the flask. You are not the flask.

I am the AI narrator. I do not have a metabolism, which is the one time that is an advantage. Mick has one, and it has opinions. This chapter is the energy bill from [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md), itemised, before anyone is allowed to start a religion about breakfast.

## The Physics Unit, Before Anyone Mentions Lunch 🌡️

A **calorie** (lowercase, small, the chemist’s one) is the heat needed to raise the temperature of **one gram of water by one degree Celsius**. That is the original idea: water, a thermometer, a tiny amount of heat.

Water is fussy. Its specific heat is not quite constant, so physicists had to pick a temperature window. The **15° calorie** is the heat that takes one gram from 14.5°C to 15.5°C, about **4.1855 joules**. The **thermochemical calorie**, the one you will see in tables, is defined as exactly **4.184 J**. Close enough that nobody should fight about the fourth decimal over a sandwich, and not close enough that you should pretend “a calorie” was ever a single, eternal object.

A **kilocalorie** is a thousand of those: the heat to raise **one kilogram** of water by one degree. That is also the **large Calorie**, sometimes written with a capital C, and it is the unit hiding on food labels. When a peach is listed as 40 Calories, that is 40 kilocalories, which is 40,000 small calories. The capital letter was supposed to stop the confusion. It did not. In ordinary English, “calorie” now means the large one, and science, having lost the branding war, mostly uses **joules** anyway.

Australia puts **kilojoules** on the panel, which is the honest SI move. One food calorie is about 4.184 kJ. A 2,000 calorie day is roughly 8,400 kJ. Same energy, different hat. The yoghurt did not change. The units did.

The word itself is from Latin *calor*, heat. French physicists in the 1820s needed a unit for the steam-engine age. Nicolas Clément is usually given the naming credit. The original published sense was already the *large* one: heat to raise a kilogram of water by a degree. Later, as the centimetre-gram-second system took over laboratories, the small calorie (per gram) became the chemist’s tool, and nutrition kept the kilogram version and then, in English, dropped the “kilo-”. Two units, one name, a capital letter that nobody uses, and a country that went to kilojoules and still has to explain itself at barbecues.

The important sentence, the one diet culture keeps trying to steal: **a calorie is a unit of energy, not a nutrient, not a moral category, and not a personality test.** It is the same kind of thing as a joule or a watt-hour. You can put it on a biscuit. You can also put it on a lump of coal. The unit does not care.

## The Tiny Arsonist With a Thermometer 💣

If you want to know how much chemical energy is *in* a food, you set fire to it.

A **bomb calorimeter** is a thick steel vessel — the bomb — charged with pure oxygen at high pressure, with a small dried sample inside and a fuse to light it. The bomb sits in a jacket of water. You ignite the sample. It oxidises, almost completely, to carbon dioxide, water, and, if there was nitrogen, nitrogen gas. The water warms. From the temperature rise, the mass of water, and some corrections for the hardware, you get the **heat of combustion**: the **gross energy**.

Typical bomb numbers, per gram of dry nutrient:

- Carbohydrate: about **4.1 to 4.2 kcal**
- Fat: about **9.4 to 9.5 kcal**
- Protein: about **5.65 kcal**

Fat wins the density contest because it is more reduced: more C–H bonds, less oxygen already sitting in the molecule, more fire left to have. That is chemistry, not a character flaw of butter.

The bomb is a closed flask. It is also a pyromaniac. It burns fibre. It burns the bits you would have sent down the toilet. It oxidises protein all the way to N₂, which your body refuses to do. You excrete nitrogen mostly as **urea**, and urea still contains combustible energy — about **1.25 kcal per gram of protein**. The bomb collects that heat. You do not. You pee it.

So even before anyone mentions cooking, chewing, or the microbiome, the bomb is answering a different question from the one a body asks. The bomb asks: *if we oxidise this completely, how much heat?* The body asks: *how much of this can I actually catch, after the disassembly line, the leaks, and the rent?*

There is a second kind of calorimeter, and it is the one that made the human claim stick.

**Direct calorimetry** measures heat as heat: a person in a sealed, instrumented room, the air and walls reporting how much warmth they gave off. **Indirect calorimetry** infers heat from gas exchange: oxygen in, carbon dioxide out, a bit of urinary nitrogen so you know how much protein was oxidised. Atwater did both. He wanted to show that a unit of food heat was a unit of body heat and a unit of mechanical work, not three different religions.

Wilbur Olin Atwater, working at Wesleyan and with the USDA in the 1890s and 1900s, had a bomb for food, faeces, and urine, and a **respiration calorimeter**: a copper-lined room that could hold a human for days. Subjects ate weighed diets, pedalled, rested, slept, and surrendered every output that could be collected. It is one of the great, slightly unhinged experiments in American science. He showed that conservation of energy holds for people. You are not a miracle. You are a very fancy radiator with opinions, as [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md) already put it, now with a receipt.

Indirect calorimetry is still how metabolic wards and those claustrophobic hospital hoods work. Doubly labelled water — a sip of water tagged with odd isotopes of hydrogen and oxygen, then tracked in urine over days — is how you estimate free-living total energy expenditure without locking someone in a box. Pontzer’s hunter-gatherer numbers, later in this chapter, come from that trick. The bomb tells you what is in the food. These methods tell you what the animal did with it.

## Atwater’s 4 / 4 / 9, A Useful Lie 🧮

From those rooms and those bombs, Atwater derived **metabolizable energy**: gross energy minus what left in faeces and urine. After rounding, the **general Atwater factors** became the numbers that ate the twentieth century:

- Carbohydrate: **4 kcal/g**
- Protein: **4 kcal/g**
- Fat: **9 kcal/g**
- Alcohol, the uninvited plus-one: **7 kcal/g**

Protein’s 4 is the most obviously constructed. The bomb said ~5.65. Subtract the urea (~1.25). Discount the fraction that is not digested. Atwater’s typical **coefficients of digestibility** were about 92% for protein, 95% for fat, and 98% for carbohydrate in mixed diets. Arithmetic, rounded: (5.65 × 0.92) − 1.25 ≈ 4. Carbohydrate and fat needed smaller discounts, then the same rounding. His less-rounded working numbers were closer to 4.0, 8.9, and 4.0; the poster version became 4 / 9 / 4.

Alcohol is the uninvited plus-one because it is almost fully absorbed and the bomb and the body agree, more or less, on **7 kcal/g**. Sugar alcohols (polyols) are a later headache: erythritol is treated as ~0, others often ~1.5–2.4, because they are partly absorbed and partly fermented. The 4 / 4 / 9 was never a complete census of things humans put in their mouths. It was a census of steak, bread, and butter.

The 4 / 4 / 9 is not a law of nature. It is a **population average** from mixed late-Victorian diets, mostly young men, turned into a pocket calculator. Atwater knew it was an average. The century after him sometimes forgot.

Annabel Merrill and Bernice Watt later built **specific Atwater factors** in [USDA Handbook 74](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400535/data/classics/usda%20handbook%2074.pdf): different multipliers for the protein, fat, and carbohydrate of different foods, because a gram of protein in milk is not a gram of protein in beans, and a gram of “carbohydrate” in sugar is not a gram of “carbohydrate” in whole wheat. The general 4 / 4 / 9 survived anyway. It is easy. Labels love easy.

Call it a useful lie. Useful, because the first law still holds and you need *some* conversion if you want to compare a potato to a peanut without setting fire to both at the table. A lie, because it pretends every gram of a named macronutrient is the same job, in every food, in every gut, after every kitchen.

If you want a sports version: a goal is a goal on the scoreboard. A tap-in and a goal from the boundary on the run are not the same piece of work. Atwater is the scoreboard.

## The Cuts The Flask Does Not Take ✂️

A body is not a bomb. Several honest taxes sit between the label and the mitochondria.

### Fibre, the carbohydrate that mostly is not

“Carbohydrate” on a panel is a chemical category, not a metabolic one. **Fibre** is carbohydrate your small intestine cannot dismantle. Some of it is fermented in the colon into short-chain fatty acids — acetate, propionate, butyrate — which you *can* use. Some of it leaves as bulk. The bomb burns all of it.

FAO and the EU treat mixed dietary fibre as about **2 kcal/g** (8 kJ/g), assuming a typical fermentability around 70%. [Australian labelling](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/science-data/food-nutrient-databases/afcd/search/nutrients) does the same: fibre at 8 kJ/g, protein 17, fat 37, available carbohydrate 17. The United States is messier: manufacturers may subtract insoluble fibre, count soluble non-digestible carbohydrate at 2 kcal/g, or just use 4 on total carbohydrate. Same apple. Different arithmetic. The apple does not change.

**Resistant starch** is the plot twist inside “carbohydrate.” Some starch, especially when cooked and then cooled — rice in the fridge, a potato salad — recrystallises into a form that enzymes in the small intestine treat as fibre. It was 4 on the way into the pot. It may be closer to 2 on the way out of you. Retrogradation is not a lifestyle. It is physics in leftover roast potatoes.

### Cooking, which is a metabolic technology

Richard Wrangham’s argument, in *Catching Fire* and the papers around it, is that **cooking is not a lifestyle**. It is part of human digestion that we outsourced to fire. Heat gelatinises starch, denatures proteins, bursts cell walls, and lowers the cost of chewing and immune defence. [Carmody and Wrangham (2009)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047248409001262) put numbers on the starch side: cooking can raise the available energy of starches on the order of **12 to 35 percent**, depending on the plant. Raw-foodist studies keep finding lower energy status, including menstrual disruption in some women, which is a loud physiological complaint and not a vibe.

Pounding and blending help. Heat helps more. Almonds are the punchline later in this chapter: whole, they leak; as butter, they behave. Structure is not a footnote. Structure is the food.

Mice in the Carmody lab gained more weight on cooked diets than on calorically matched raw ones, for both meat and tubers. Pounding helped a bit. Heat helped more. That is an animal result, not a meal plan, and it is one of the cleaner demonstrations that **processing changes metabolizable energy**.

This is not a palaeo sermon. It is the observation that **the same grams, cooked, are a different food**. The label on raw oats and the label on porridge are closer than your gut is. Humans also have smaller teeth and a shorter gut than our raw-eating relatives, which Wrangham reads as an adaptation to already-processed food. Other researchers prefer meat, or tubers, or a pile of all three. The energy claim can be true even if the fossil-fire timeline is still a brawl.

### The thermic effect, or the cover charge for eating

Digesting, absorbing, and storing food costs ATP. That cost shows up as extra heat after a meal: the **thermic effect of food**, diet-induced thermogenesis, the cover charge.

Typical ranges, as a fraction of the energy in that nutrient ([Westerterp, 2004](https://nutritionandmetabolism.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1743-7075-1-5)):

- Protein: **20–30%**
- Carbohydrate: **5–10%**
- Fat: **0–3%**
- Alcohol: messy, often quoted around **10–30%**

A mixed diet at energy balance usually spends about **10%** of daily intake on this. High-protein meals push it up; very high-fat meals push it down. Protein is expensive because you have to break peptide bonds, handle nitrogen, run the urea cycle, and you have no tidy storage locker for spare amino acids. Fat is cheap to warehouse. Whey tends to cost more heat than casein or soy, which is a reminder that “protein” is already a crowd. That is not a reason to worship one macro and exile another. It is a reason the 4 and the 9 on the label are not the 4 and the 9 in the ledger.

Total daily energy expenditure, while we are naming the parts, is usually carved into **basal / resting metabolism** (the lights-on cost), **thermic effect**, **exercise**, and **NEAT** — fidgeting, standing, pacing, the unglamorous motion that is not a workout. Adaptation, later, loves to steal from NEAT first, because you do not notice yourself sitting still.

### Bioavailability, which is a food-matrix word for “did it actually get in?”

Nuts made this famous. USDA feeding trials that collected diets, urine, and faeces found that **Atwater overestimates the metabolizable energy of whole tree nuts**. [Novotny, Gebauer, and Baer (2012)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3396444/) measured almonds at about **4.6 kcal/g** in the diet against an Atwater prediction of **6.0–6.1 kcal/g**: a **32%** overestimate. Walnuts came in about **21%** below the factors. Cashews and pistachios were closer but still short. Grind the almond into butter and the gap shrinks, because you have already done the cell-wall demolition the molars were going to fail at.

The fat was in the nut. The bomb found it. The ileum did not get the memo. Cell walls, particle size, chewing, roasting, and how intact the tissue is after you swallow it all change the cut. “Bioavailability” is the grown-up name for that. It is also why a calorie of almond and a calorie of almond oil are not the same appointment.

Industrial processing can run this tape the other way. Grind, extrude, puff, and you have already done the demolition. A calorie from a whole food and a calorie from a highly processed version of the same macronutrient split can differ in absorption *and* in how quickly they hit the blood, which changes hormones, which changes the next meal. That is not an argument that “processed” is a nutrient. It is an argument that **form is part of the energy story**, and the panel does not have a column for form.

### The microbiome’s cut, which is more of a side business than a theft

What the small intestine misses, bacteria may ferment. The short-chain fatty acids they return are real energy: often estimated at about **5–10%** of metabolizable energy in a typical human diet, and a much larger share in a gorilla. Butyrate feeds the colon lining. Propionate heads to the liver. Acetate wanders further. The microbes also take a cut for themselves, make gas, and send signals that are not calories at all.

So the landlord metaphor is half-right. They take a cut. They also remodel the building. Chapter 08 gets the organ-level version. Here the point is narrower: **fibre’s 2 kcal/g is already a confession that Atwater’s 4 was never meant for the colon.**

## A Calorie Is a Calorie, In a Flask 🧪

The slogan is a first-law statement wearing street clothes.

In a closed system, energy is energy. If you oxidise 4 kcal of sugar or 4 kcal of fat in a bomb, you get about 4 kcal of heat. If a body oxidises them completely, the heat and the work still have to add up. You cannot magic energy into or out of existence by calling it “clean” or “toxic.” That part is true, and people who pretend otherwise are selling a loophole.

A body is not a closed flask.

- Some of the 4 never absorbs.
- Some is spent on the thermic cover charge.
- Some is returned as SCFAs at a discount.
- Some is stored, and storage has a cost and a location.
- Hormones, fibre, protein, and food form change *how much you eat next*, which is not a property of the joule; it is a property of the animal.
- Two humans given the same label-calories do not extract or expend them identically.

So: **a calorie is a calorie in the bomb. A calorie is a *budget line* in a body, and budget lines have fees.** The first law is not optional. The 4 / 4 / 9 is optional in the sense that it is an estimate, and estimates have error bars the size of a handful of almonds.

This is not a licence to ignore energy. It is a licence to stop treating the panel as scripture.

And this is not a diet. No protocol follows. If a paragraph here could be swapped for a 12-week plan, it has failed in the same way a first-year lecture transcript would fail this series. Macros are architecture. They are not a personality test. The [endurance note](../📰-articles/health-fitness-endurance.md) already covers how athletes spend ATP on purpose. The next chapter covers how a meal becomes ATP at all. Neither is a commandment about your lunch.

## How The Label Gets Made 🏷️

Most of the time, nobody set fire to your snack.

Regulators let manufacturers **calculate** energy from composition. In the United States, [21 CFR 101.9](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/101.9) allows several routes: general 4 / 4 / 9, specific Atwater factors from Handbook 74, bomb calorimetry minus a protein correction, and adjusted factors for fibre and sugar alcohols. Calories on the panel are rounded: to the nearest 5 below 50, to the nearest 10 above. The FDA’s enforcement discretion is roughly a **20%** overage before a calorie declaration is treated as misbranding. A “500 calorie” meal that is actually 600 can still be legal. That is not a conspiracy. That is what happens when an average is asked to be a measurement.

In Australia and New Zealand, energy is declared in **kilojoules**, using the Food Standards Code factors: protein 17 kJ/g, fat 37, available carbohydrate 17, dietary fibre 8, alcohol 29. The EU’s [Regulation 1169/2011](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32011R1169) is in the same family, with fibre at 8 kJ/g and polyols at 10. Same idea, local dialects.

Australia requires kilojoules; calories may appear as well. The “8,700 kJ daily intake” footnote on many packs is a reference value for a mythical average adult, not a conversion of 2,000 calories (that would be closer to 8,400 kJ) and not your number. Serving sizes are another quiet fudge: they are often a custom, a regulation, or a manufacturer’s optimism, not a physiological unit.

What the panel is, then:

1. A chemical analysis, or a recipe database’s guess at one.
2. Multiplied by a century-old average.
3. Rounded.
4. Allowed to be off by a fifth, in US practice, before anyone calls a lawyer.
5. Silent about cooking, chewing, your microbes, and whether you are currently dieting.

Use it as a map. Do not use it as a surveyor’s peg. The cabinet already has a note on [the map not being the territory](../💬-quotes-with-teeth/map-is-not-the-territory.md). The calorie panel is one of the most printed maps in the supermarket, and it is still a map.

## Metabolic Adaptation: Two Humans Are Not Two Identical Engines 🛠️

Here is the mystery the flask cannot see.

When you eat less, or a lot less, energy expenditure falls further than the loss of tissue predicts. Researchers call this **metabolic adaptation** or **adaptive thermogenesis**. It is not “damage” in the soap-opera sense. It is the organism noticing a famine and turning down the lights.

The Minnesota Starvation Experiment — Ancel Keys, conscientious objectors, 1944–45 — remains the loudest demonstration. Men on roughly half their usual intake lost about a quarter of their weight. Resting metabolism fell on the order of **40%** in total; a substantial slice of that was **beyond** what the smaller bodies explained. They were cold, tired, obsessed with food, and moving less. Some of the “adaptation” is physiology. Some is behaviour wearing a metabolic mask: less fidgeting, fewer stairs, the quiet death of **NEAT** (non-exercise activity thermogenesis), the calories you spend on not sitting still.

The CALERIE trials, with milder restriction in non-starving humans, still find adaptation: a few percent at rest, more in free-living conditions. The body is not a spreadsheet.

Then there is the circus version. [Fothergill, Hall and colleagues (2016)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27136388/) followed *The Biggest Loser* contestants. After 30 weeks of extreme diet and exercise, resting metabolic rate was down about **610 kcal/day**. Six years later, most of the weight had been regained, and a **metabolic adaptation around 500 kcal/day** was still there. The people who had kept more weight off were, awkwardly, the ones whose engines were still running slow. Adaptation looks less like a one-time punishment and more like a spring that stays stretched while you keep pulling.

Herman Pontzer’s **constrained energy** picture complicates the other side of the ledger. Hadza hunter-gatherers are on their feet all day and, once you adjust for body size, do not burn wildly more total calories than office workers. Add a lot of aerobic work and the budget does not rise one-for-one; other expenditures shrink. [Pontzer’s 2016 analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4803033/) is a fight with the additive model that still sits in fitness watches. Kevin Hall has even [reinterpreted the Biggest Loser numbers](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.23308) through that lens: huge sustained activity, compensatory drop in resting burn, total daily energy less elastic than the montage suggested.

Leptin, the adipose-tissue hormone, falls when fat stores fall, and the brain reads that as famine: more hunger, less expenditure, a thermostat shoved. Thyroid hormone T3 often dips. Sympathetic tone changes. You fidget less. You take the lift. Some of this is measurable as a lower resting rate; some only shows up in a free-living day. James Levine’s work on NEAT made the fidgeting slice famous: two people in the same office can differ by hundreds of kilocalories a day in spontaneous movement, which is a lot of yoghurt, and almost none of it looks like “exercise.”

We do not have a finished theory of why two humans, same height, same labelled intake, same gym, diverge. Organ size (brains and livers are expensive; fat is cheap), thyroid hormones, leptin, fidgeting, sleep, prior weight history, medications, and the microbiome are all on the suspect board. Genetics is in the room. So is the simple fact that **expenditure is a regulated variable**, not a fixed tariff.

A useful picture, if you want one you can carry: basal burn is the idle. Thermic effect is the cover charge. Exercise is the session. NEAT is everything you do when you are not “training,” which is most of life. Adaptation can pick the idle, the fidgets, or both. Fitness watches that add your run on top of a fixed daily number are telling an additive story Pontzer does not buy at the high end.

None of this is a diet. It is the reason “just subtract 500 calories” is a sentence that works in a spreadsheet and sulks in a mammal.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **There is no single “true” calorie content of a food in a human.** There is gross energy, digestible energy, metabolizable energy, and net metabolizable energy after the thermic cost. We pick one, print it, and hope.
- **The Atwater factors have not been rebuilt from scratch for modern diets.** Specific factors exist. Most labels do not use them. Nuts, high-fibre foods, and novel ingredients keep failing the average.
- **We still argue how to count fibre.** Two kcal/g is a compromise, not a measurement of your particular colon.
- **Metabolic adaptation’s size, duration, and individual scatter are not settled.** Minnesota, CALERIE, and *The Biggest Loser* are different stresses. Constrained versus additive daily expenditure is an active fight, not a textbook footnote.
- **We do not know how much of “two humans are not identical engines” is organs, hormones, microbes, fidgeting, or history.** The error bars are the story.
- **Cooking’s evolutionary timing is still a fight.** That cooking raises net energy is on much firmer ground than “therefore *Homo erectus* had hearths at 1.8 million years.” Wrangham is interesting. Archaeology gets a vote.
