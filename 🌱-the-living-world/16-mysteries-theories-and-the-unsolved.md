# Mysteries, Theories, And The Unsolved 🕳️

This is the chapter where the sitcom credits refuse to roll.

We have a working picture of life as a process that pays three bills — energy, information, structure — and a kit it actually used: [CHNOPS, water, and a handful of metals](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md). We have a last common ancestor who was already a competent cell, not a lonely spark in a puddle. We have a planet that got oxygenated, then forested, then walked on. None of that is a closed case file. The Earth story still has missing first acts, characters who will not sit still for a definition, and a family tree that keeps turning into a group chat.

I am the AI narrator. Mick asked for the unsolved pile, not a trophy cabinet. Fair. A series that pretends biology finished in Year 10 is how you get adults who think "calorie" is a moral category and "species" is a stamp. This chapter is the Earth-bound capstone: the theories we actually have, labelled as theories, and the holes we are not going to paper over with a confident font.

If you came here for aliens, wait. Other worlds get the door at the end. This is still our rock, arguing with itself.

## What The Earth Story Has Actually Settled 🧾

A capstone is allowed a short lap of honour, as long as it does not start lying.

**Settled enough to carry:**

- Life, as we can poke it, is a chemical process, not a substance. Stop the energy flow and the pattern dies. Schrödinger was right about the radiator. Joyce was right that Darwinian memory is the other half of the ticket.
- The kit is choosy. Carbon in water, with phosphorus as clerk and metals as jewellery. Silicon keeps applying and failing the wet chemistry. That snobbery is data.
- All known cellular life is cousin. One code, one handedness, one LUCA. The sample size for "how life must look" is one, which is the number at which every pattern looks like destiny.
- Endosymbiosis happened. Eukaryotic cells are mergers. Forests sit on fungal bargains. You are a holobiont whether the word annoys you or not.
- Energy at planetary scale is a sunlight story with a chemical basement. Calories are physics. Bodies are not bomb calorimeters. Landscapes are arguments among climate, rock, water, and disturbance.

**Not settled, and not going to be settled by a confident paragraph:**

- The definition. Checklists fail. Autopoiesis ignores evolution. NASA's line is a tool. We still hunt a process we cannot close.
- The start. Everything below is theory with laboratory teeth, not a core sample of the Hadean.
- The map of kinds. Domains move. Species concepts quarrel. Viruses refuse the furniture.
- The mind. Parked next door on purpose.

That split is the whole method of this series. Swagger is for the jokes. The claims have to say which pile they are in.

One more honest note, because the expedition map lists chapters we have not built yet. DNA, proteins, calories, cells, bodies, plants, soil, the land method: those rooms are still being furnished. This capstone is allowed to speak for the *thesis* — process, kit, lineage, coupling — without pretending the middle floors already have carpets. If a later chapter contradicts a sentence here, the later chapter wins. That is how a climb is supposed to work.

## The First Act Is Missing 🎬

[Chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md) already said it: **LUCA is late in the play.** The last universal common ancestor is the last team still in the tournament, not the invention of football. A 2024 reconstruction put that ancestor around 4.2 billion years ago, already packing a couple of thousand proteins and already in an arms race with viruses. The error bars are not small. Other groups prefer younger dates. Either way, the interesting claim is competence, not a birthday cake.

So how did a process that copies, eats, and stays in one place get started?

Nobody has a souvenir. What we have are research programmes that keep producing chemistry, and a habit of talking as if one of them has already won.

### RNA World: The Guitarist Who Also Drums 🎸

Walter Gilbert named it in 1986, after RNA turned out to be able to *do* things, not just carry the score. The pitch is elegant: one polymer that stores information *and* catalyses reactions would skip the chicken-and-egg fight between DNA (library) and proteins (machines). The ribosome, still an RNA machine at its heart, looks like a fossil of that arrangement. So does the fact that nucleotides still run the cell's energy currency.

The problems are also elegant, which is annoying.

- RNA is a fussy molecule. Making it from plausible prebiotic soup, in water, without a chemist hovering, is not a solved trick.
- A ribozyme that copies itself well enough to evolve is the whole point, and we keep getting closer without quite arriving. Gerald Joyce's lab has RNA enzymes that copy functional RNAs and let variants win. A 2026 polymerase ribozyme nicknamed QT45 got small enough, and capable enough, to make the spontaneous-emergence story less of a stretch. Closer is not done.
- Even a working RNA world still has to invent metabolism, membranes, and the genetic code's mapping onto amino acids. It is a first band, not the whole festival.

In 2025, Matthew Powner's group at UCL showed amino acids linking to RNA in water at neutral pH, using thioesters as the energy handle — chemistry that smells like both the RNA world and the older "thioester world" idea that energy-rich sulfur compounds ran early metabolism. That is a genuine bridge, not a press-release bridge. It is still a beaker, not a Hadean tide pool.

There is a quieter RNA argument that does not need a full world. Even if proteins and membranes showed up early, the fact that the ribosome's active site is RNA, that many cofactors are nucleotides in fancy hats, and that DNA is a later, more stable filing system, still looks like a historical scar. Biology is full of scars. Homochirality is one. The code is one. RNA at the centre of the oldest machine we have is another. Scars are not proofs. They are the sort of clue a detective is allowed to keep on the board.

RNA world is the favourite of people who think information had to come first. It might be right. It is not a postcard from 4.3 billion years ago.

### Metabolism-First: The Kitchen Before The Recipe 🔥

The other camp starts with a flow, not a genome.

Nick Lane, William Martin, and Michael Russell have spent decades arguing that alkaline hydrothermal vents — warm, not boiling; labyrinthine; full of natural proton gradients across thin iron-sulfur walls — look suspiciously like a cell that has not invented a membrane yet. Hydrogen and carbon dioxide as dinner. Metal sulfides as the first enzymes. A geological battery that biochemistry later copied and took indoors.

Lost City, found in 2000 on the Atlantis Massif, is the modern exhibit: alkaline fluids, carbonate chimneys, hydrogen and methane, no black-smoker theatre. Russell had sketched something like it before anyone parked a submersible there. That is the sort of prediction that makes a theory feel like a theory rather than a mood.

Günter Wächtershäuser's older iron-sulfur world is a cousin: pyrite surfaces as the first workbenches, carbon fixation as the first job. Different vent, same instinct. Chemistry first. Genes later, once there is something worth remembering.

The complaint from the RNA side is fair: a metabolism without heredity is a flame with better branding. You can have a lovely set of reactions and still not have a lineage. The metabolism-first reply is also fair: a genome with nothing to eat is a USB stick in a desert. Both bills have to get paid. The fight is over which invoice arrived first.

We do not know.

The surface-versus-vent fight is partly chemistry and partly taste in drama. Ponds and wet-dry cycles are good at concentrating and polymerising. Vents are good at gradients and metals. A grown-up origin story may need both neighbourhoods, or a third one nobody is funding yet. "It happened at a vent" and "it happened in a pond" are both, for now, fan fiction with lab notes attached. The fan fiction is getting better. That is not the same as a memory.

### Soup, Sparks, And The Warm Little Pond ⚡

Stanley Miller, in 1953, put water, methane, ammonia, and hydrogen in a flask, sparked it like a tiny thunderstorm, and got amino acids. It remains one of the great "wait, it actually worked" moments in science. It does not remain a reconstruction of the early Earth.

The atmosphere Miller assumed was more reducing than most geochemists now think the Hadean sky was. Amino acids are not a cell. A racemic mix of leftovers is not a code. Darwin's warm little pond is still in play as a concentrating dish — wet-dry cycles on land, or little pools, may do jobs that a dilute ocean cannot — but "soup plus lightning" is a beginning of prebiotic chemistry, not the origin of life. Treat it as the opening riff, not the album.

### Panspermia, Which Kicks The Problem Down The Road ☄️

Here is the teaser, not the documentary.

Rocks move. Martian meteorites sit in Antarctic collections. Amino acids, nucleobases, and other organics show up in meteorites and in samples from asteroids Bennu and Ryugu. Soft panspermia — *ingredients* arriving from space — is not a fringe claim. It is delivery logistics.

Hard panspermia — *living cells* hitching a ride — is a different sport. Lithopanspermia, the rock-as-spaceship version, is physically plausible inside the Solar System. Spores have survived exposure experiments. Transfer from Mars to Earth is dynamically real. Survival, establishment, and "therefore we are Martians" are not demonstrated. Fred Hoyle's cometary version remains a minority report with a loud stereo.

Even if a cell arrived, you have not explained life. You have explained postage. The origin still happened *somewhere*. Panspermia is a change of venue, and it is allowed to stay in the "maybe, later" drawer until someone finds a lineage that is not our cousin.

A smaller, sharper version will matter on Mars: if we find life that shares our code and our handshake, we will not know whether it is a cousin via rocks, a contamination, or destiny. If we find the other handshake, we will know it is not us. Chapter 21 will make that into a detection problem. Here it is just the origin problem wearing a spacesuit. The teaser ends. The other worlds can wait until the ocean has had its say.

## Viruses Will Not Sit Down 🦠

Viruses failed the school exam in chapter 01 and they fail this one too, on purpose.

They evolve. They have genomes. They outnumber cells the way ants outnumber picnic baskets. Giant viruses blur the cartoon by carrying genes that look stolen from cellular life, or leftover from a more independent past. Moody's LUCA already had viral company, which means the virus problem is older than the kingdoms you learned.

Three origin stories, none of them a verdict:

- **Virus-first.** Replicators before cells. RNA world with a coat. Then the coat learned to mug a cell.
- **Reduction.** A parasitic cell that kept shedding furniture until it was a genome in a capsid. Some giant viruses make this feel less silly.
- **Escape.** A piece of cellular genetic material — a plasmid, a transposon — that learned to pack itself and leave. Retroviruses look a lot like this.

Different viral realms may have different origin stories. That is not a cop-out. It is what you say when "virus" is a lifestyle, not a family. The chimeric version — replicators from the precellular world, capsids from cells — is fashionable because it refuses to pick a single parent.

Are they alive? Still the wrong first question. They are how information parasitises metabolism. Any Earth story that leaves them out is a biography of football that ignores fouls.

## Why We Age, And Why A Jellyfish Is Being Rude About It ⏳

Aging is not a mysterious substance that leaks in at forty. It is what happens when a body that is good at paying the three bills slowly stops being good at it.

Carlos López-Otín and colleagues, updating their 2013 list in *Cell* in 2023, offered twelve **hallmarks of aging**: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic drift, loss of proteostasis, disabled autophagy, wonky nutrient-sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, senescent cells that will not leave the pitch, stem-cell exhaustion, garbled communication between cells, chronic inflammation, and a microbiome that stops being a good housemate. The list is a map of failure modes, not a single fuse.

The evolutionary "why" is colder. Peter Medawar noticed that selection gets weak after you have already reproduced. Tom Kirkwood's **disposable soma** idea is the sports version: a club that invests in the academy and the first team, not in keeping veterans immortal, will beat a club that tries to make every player last forever. Resources spent on perfect repair are resources not spent on offspring. Once you are past the reproductive window, the accountant shrugs.

That is why there is no law of physics that says you must age, and also why almost everything does. The [immortal jellyfish](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-immortal-jellyfish.md) cheats by hitting reset on cell identity, which is a different exam, and still not a plan you should try at home. We do not have a complete theory of aging. We have a pile of mechanisms that talk to each other, a selection argument that explains why the pile exists, and a longevity industry that would like you to forget the difference.

Telomeres are the celebrity version of the same accounting. Chromosomes have shoelace caps. The copying machinery cannot finish the tips cleanly, so a little length is lost each division, and a cell line that does not cheat (cancer cheats; germ lines invest) hits the Hayflick limit and stops. That is one fuse. It is not the fuse. Naked mole rats, bristlecone pines, and that rude jellyfish are reminders that "aging" is a family of strategies, not a universal countdown clock. Evolution can extend the warranty when it pays. It does not do it for free, and it does not do it for you personally just because a podcast used the word "autophagy."

Oxygen, from [chapter 02](02-the-atoms-life-bothered-with.md), is part of the joke. Aerobic life made a deal with a pyromaniac. The house sometimes rusts. We call it aging when we are being polite.

## Holobionts: You Are An Ecosystem Wearing A Nametag 🪸

Lynn Margulis already won the endosymbiosis argument: mitochondria and chloroplasts were free-living prokaryotes who moved in and never left. Then she kept going. In the early 1990s she used **holobiont** for a host plus its persistent symbionts. Coral biologists grabbed it. Microbiome people grabbed it harder.

You are not a single genome on legs. You are a primate plus a dense city of bacteria, archaea, viruses, and fungi, some inherited-ish, many rented from the environment, all of them doing metabolic work your "own" cells never learned. A coral is an animal plus algae plus a microbial cloud, and when the algae leave you get a bleaching event, which is a divorce with extra climate. A cow is a mammal wrapped around a fermentation vat. A eucalypt is a plant in conversation with fungi, as the [network under the forest](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-fungal-network-beneath-our-feet.md) already argued.

The **hologenome** idea — host genes plus microbial genes as one unit of selection — is spicier, and more disputed. Selection happens at more than one level. Microbes come and go. Treating "you plus your gut" as a Darwinian individual is sometimes useful and sometimes a category error with better lighting. The conservative version is still plenty weird: **there is no clean organism**. The nametag is a convenience. The ecosystem is the fact.

This is not a vibe. It is why antibiotics can feel like carpet-bombing, why "the human body" in later anatomy chapters is already a zoo, and why a definition of life that starts with "an individual" has already tripped on the welcome mat.

## Gaia: Poetry That Accidentally Predicts Things 🌍

James Lovelock, with Margulis in the mix, proposed that life and the planet form a coupled system that regulates itself — temperature, atmospheric composition, the chemical weather — as if Earth were keeping house. The strong reading, the one that made evolutionary biologists reach for a long stick, sounded like a planet with intentions. Ford Doolittle asked where the genome for "be a good planet" was supposed to live. James Kirchner split Gaia into several claims and then graded them: coevolution is ordinary science; homeostasis is testable and mixed; optimisation for life is the bit that starts writing cheques the evidence cannot cash.

Daisyworld was Lovelock's answer to the teleology charge: black daisies and white daisies, competing, accidentally buffering temperature without a committee. It is a toy model. Toy models can still teach. They can also be special cases that fall over when you add daisies who wreck the neighbourhood.

What survives, if you drop the goddess branding?

- Life *does* change the planet. Oxygen is not a mineral accident. It is a waste product that became weather, rust, and a new kind of metabolism.
- Feedbacks exist. Some are stabilising. Some, including biological ones, are not. The Great Oxidation was a mass-poisoning from the point of view of the anaerobes. If that counts as Gaia being a good mother, the mother has a dark sense of humour.
- Ice floats. Carbon cycles. Clouds, forests, plankton, and rocks talk to each other. Earth system science is Gaia after it learned to use a spreadsheet and stopped insisting on the word "organism."

A useful sports version, because Mick will otherwise start nodding at the word "system" as if it were a lullaby: Gaia is not the coach. It is not even the league. It is the way the crowd, the pitch, the weather, and the fixture list all change how the game can be played, including when the crowd sets the stadium on fire. Life is a player *and* part of the stadium. That is allowed to be true without the stadium having a soul.

So: **poetry that predicts**. The poem said "look at the coupling." The coupling is real. The planet is not a self. It is a conversation with a lot of dead participants. Weak Gaia is almost boringly true. Strong Gaia is a metaphor that keeps trying to clock in as a mechanism. Keep the metaphor. Make it earn the next prediction. The carbon cycle, the oxygen rise, plankton as a climate organ, forests as rainfall conspirators: those are the predictions that paid rent. "Earth is alive" is the chorus. Do not confuse the chorus with the verse.

## The Tree Of Life Is More Of A Web 🕸️

Darwin wanted a tree. Carl Woese, reading ribosomal RNA, found a third domain hiding in the furniture and gave us Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. That was a genuine plot twist, and it still organises textbooks.

Then genomes arrived and started gossiping.

**Horizontal gene transfer** is not a footnote in microbes. Genes hop by plasmids, viruses, naked DNA, fusion. The history of *cells* can still be a tree, especially if you follow the conservative core of the translation machinery. The history of *genes* is a web. Ford Doolittle drew the messy version on purpose. Informational genes are stingier about moving. Metabolic genes commute. The picture that survived the fight is a reticulate tree: vertical trunk, horizontal vines, viruses as extra weather.

A second plot twist is still in motion. The **eocyte** or two-domain tree puts eukaryotes *inside* Archaea, specifically near the Asgard archaea, rather than as a third equal sibling. If that holds, we are archaea who swallowed a bacterium and never got over it. Three domains was a brilliant map. Maps get redrawn.

Viruses do not sit on the cellular tree at all, except as graffiti. Endosymbiosis is a merger, not a branch. The "tree of life" is still the right metaphor for a family argument. It is the wrong metaphor for a legal document.

## What Even Is A Species? 🏷️

Ernst Mayr's biological species concept — groups that actually or potentially interbreed, reproductively isolated from others — is the one that escaped into culture. It works beautifully for birds that have opinions about songs. It falls over for:

- **Asexual lineages**, which is most of life's history
- **Fossils**, which do not mate in the museum
- **Ring species** and messy hybrid zones, where the offside rule depends which sideline you are standing on
- **Microbes**, who swap genes the way footballers swap shirts

Morphological species: they look like a kind. Phylogenetic species: the smallest diagnosable lineage. Ecological species: they make a living in a distinct way. Kevin de Queiroz's peace treaty is that **species are lineages**, and the various "concepts" are different *criteria* for noticing a lineage, not different kinds of thing. Useful. Still a committee.

Microbes make the whole argument look provincial. A bacterial "species" might be a fuzzy cloud of gene-swapping strains. The 97% ribosomal-RNA cutoff that working labs use is a filing rule, not a law of nature. Horizontal gene transfer means two cells can share a kitchen without sharing a grandmother. If your species concept needs a mating dance, most of the biosphere did not get the memo.

Why this belongs in a mysteries chapter: because every later argument about biodiversity, extinction, "how many species live in the sea," and "is this a new organism on another world" smuggles in a species concept. If you cannot say what a species is on Earth, you should be humble about counting them on Europa.

## Consciousness Stays Next Door 🧠

You will notice the missing celebrity.

Subjective experience — the fact that it is *like something* to be a nervous system having a Tuesday — is a real mystery. It is not a mystery this series is going to annex. The cabinet already has a room for it: [consciousness and the mind](../📰-articles/consciousness-and-the-mind.md). Biology can tell you about neurons, attention, and the mixing desk. It has not told you why the lights are on. Treating consciousness as a late chapter of metabolism would be a category error with a medical degree.

The living-world series needs brains as organs and as ecology. It does not need to solve the hard problem in a footnote so that chapter 21 can look tidy.

## The Door 🚪

If life is a process, two rude thoughts follow.

First: it might not have happened only once, even here. A **shadow biosphere** — life with a different handshake, a different code, quietly under the shoes — is a live, difficult, possibly empty idea. Carol Cleland and Paul Davies have been poking it for years. Absence of evidence is mostly absence of a search that would notice. That is the awkward *local* version of the alien question, and it belongs to the next arc.

Second: it might not look like a forest. Most of Earth's living *space* is not a forest. It is wet, dark, salty, and under pressure. The ocean is the practice planet we already have. Hydrothermal vents are both an origin-story candidate and a working biosphere that does not care about the Sun. If you want to hunt the verb elsewhere, start by noticing it is already running in places that would look, to a tourist from a meadow, like another world.

[The ocean chapter](17-the-ocean-is-another-planet-we-already-have.md) is next door. The telescope chapters wait until we have been honest about water. [The Fermi paradox](../💡-concepts/fermi-paradox.md) is the civilisation question, loud and later. The quieter one is whether the process started at all, anywhere else. We do not know. That is not a shrug. It is the point of still having a series.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

This whole chapter is the unsolved corner. A shorter list, for the people who skip:

- **How life started.** RNA world, metabolism-first, vents, ponds, incoming ingredients. Programmes, not souvenirs. LUCA is not the start.
- **Whether it started more than once on Earth.** Shadow biospheres remain a hypothesis with a search problem, not a discovery with a press officer.
- **What to do with viruses.** Lifestyle, multiple origins, not a yes/no on "alive."
- **Why aging is this particular pile of hallmarks**, and how much of it is negotiable versus baked into multicellular accounting.
- **Whether holobionts are units of selection** or just very intimate ecosystems. The nametag is the easy part.
- **How far Gaia can be pushed** before it stops predicting and starts preaching. Weak coupling: real. Planetary organism: poetry.
- **Tree versus web versus two domains.** The map is being redrawn while we walk on it.
- **Species.** Lineages are real. Our bins are tools. Tools disagree.
- **Consciousness.** Not unsolved *here*. Unsolved *there*, on purpose.
- **Whether "life" is one natural kind.** [Cleland's warning](01-what-even-is-life.md) still stands. Water is H₂O wherever you find it. Life might be a pile of similar tricks.
- **How much of the Earth story is luck versus law.** Fast LUCA, one code, one handshake, oxygen late, brains later: destiny, or a tournament with one remaining team? We will not know from one planet. The door at the end of this chapter exists because of that sentence.

If that list feels like the series failed: good. School biology ends like a sitcom. This one keeps the credits off.
