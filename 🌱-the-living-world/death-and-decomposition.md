# Death and Decomposition: Life After the Organism 🍂

A fallen tree has stopped living as a tree. As a habitat, it may be entering a particularly busy phase.

Fungi spread through its wood, beetle larvae feed beneath its bark, and the decaying trunk holds water and shelters other organisms. Its carbon and nutrients move into new bodies, soil and the atmosphere. Decomposition is a set of biological processes with consequences for entire ecosystems.

## 🧬 Cell death and organismal death are different

Cells die throughout the life of a multicellular organism. Controlled cell death helps shape developing tissues, remove damaged cells and regulate populations of immune cells.

In **apoptosis**, a cell activates mechanisms that dismantle it in a relatively contained way, allowing its remains to be cleared. Other forms of cell death can rupture membranes and provoke inflammation. The details matter to [development](embryonic-development.md) and [immunity](immunity.md).

Organismal death concerns the loss of the coordinated processes that sustain the organism. It does not require every cell or associated microbe to cease activity simultaneously. Tissues differ in their dependence on oxygen, nutrients and ongoing signals.

This biological distinction is useful without turning the note into a clinical definition of death. A tree, a bacterium and a mammal organise their living parts differently; the death of the whole cannot always be described by watching one component.

## ⏳ Why maintenance eventually fails

Organisms continuously repair damage, replace components and manage errors. Ageing involves changes across many systems, including DNA maintenance, protein quality control, metabolism, immune function and cellular communication.

Evolutionary explanations ask why natural selection has not produced unlimited maintenance. Selection against a harmful effect often weakens when that effect occurs late in life. Some variants beneficial earlier can impose costs later. Disposable soma theory emphasises trade-offs between investment in bodily maintenance and other demands, including reproduction.

These are related explanations, not evidence that bodies contain a universal scheduled-death switch. Ageing patterns differ greatly among species. [Open questions in biology](open-questions-in-biology.md) considers the mechanisms and remaining uncertainty; the [immortal jellyfish](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-immortal-jellyfish.md) is a particularly unusual example of life-cycle reversal, rather than invulnerability.

## 🪱 Decomposition is a food web

Dead organic matter is **detritus**. It includes leaves, wood, bodies, shed tissue and faecal material. Its consumers form a detrital food web linked to the rest of the ecosystem.

Scavengers remove pieces. Invertebrates fragment material and mix it with soil. Fungi and bacteria secrete enzymes that break large molecules into smaller ones they can absorb. Predators and grazers then consume the organisms doing that work.

Different resources favour different communities. A carcass provides concentrated protein and other readily available compounds. Wood contains cellulose embedded in a resistant matrix that includes lignin. Its decomposition often requires specialised chemistry.

White-rot fungi can substantially degrade lignin as well as other wood components. Brown-rot fungi preferentially remove structural carbohydrates while leaving chemically modified lignin-rich material. These processes produce different textures and residues. A crumbling log is a record of what its occupants have been able to digest.

## 🌡️ Why some things rot slowly

Decomposition depends on the material, the organisms present and the physical environment.

Water is essential for microbial activity, but saturation can restrict oxygen supply. Warm conditions often accelerate biological reactions within suitable ranges, while drought, cold or extreme heat can limit activity. The chemical composition and physical accessibility of the material matter too.

A standing dead trunk may remain relatively dry; a fallen trunk in contact with moist soil experiences different conditions. Bark, diameter, tree species and fungal colonisation alter the rate further. There is no universal expiry date for a log.

Fire is another route by which organic matter is transformed. It can rapidly release carbon, redistribute nutrients and leave charcoal and ash. It does not reproduce the same ecological effects as gradual decay. [Fire, water and succession](forests/fire-water-and-succession.md) looks at that difference in Australian landscapes.

## 🔁 Nutrients cycle; energy flows

As decomposers respire, some organic carbon returns to the environment as carbon dioxide. Some becomes microbial biomass or remains in partially processed material. Under oxygen-free conditions, further microbial transformations can also produce methane.

Organic nitrogen can be released as ammonium, a process called **mineralisation**. But decomposers also need nitrogen for their own growth. When feeding on carbon-rich, nitrogen-poor material, they may take up available nitrogen and temporarily reduce what nearby plants can access. That is **immobilisation**.

Phosphorus is released from organic compounds through enzymatic processes and may be taken up, retained on mineral surfaces or lost from the site.

The atoms are reused. The energy is not recycled in the same way: as organisms use it, usable energy is progressively dissipated as heat. Ecosystems require continuing energy inputs, mostly sunlight. Decomposition is recycling with a power bill.

## 🪵 Dead wood is also structure

A log holds moisture, creates shaded spaces and changes how water and sediment move. Standing dead trees can provide cavities and perches; fallen wood supports organisms specialised for particular decay stages.

A canopy gap also changes light and temperature. Seedlings may establish or accelerate growth while the fallen trunk decomposes nearby. The vegetation response and the decay process have different timescales.

This is why removing every fallen branch does more than change appearances. It removes habitat and alters the movement of carbon and nutrients. The effect depends on context, but a visually tidy woodland is not automatically an ecologically complete one.

[Soil ecology](soil-ecology.md) explains what happens to some of the resulting organic matter. Persistence often depends on association with minerals and protection inside soil structures, not simply on material being intrinsically indigestible.

## 🌊 The same processes feed the deep sea

**Marine snow** consists of sinking particles such as dead plankton, faecal pellets and aggregates of organic material. Microbes and animals consume much of it before it reaches the seabed. The fraction that sinks deeper transfers carbon and energy away from surface waters. See [NOAA's explanation](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/marinesnow.html) and the cabinet's [marine snow note](../🌙-bedtime-reading/marine-snow-the-oceans-slow-blizzard.md).

A whale fall is a much larger, concentrated resource. Mobile scavengers consume soft tissue. Other animals exploit enriched sediments and remaining material. Microbial breakdown of bone lipids can generate sulfide, supporting organisms that depend on sulfur-oxidising microbes.

These stages overlap and vary with carcass size, depth and local conditions. They illustrate succession driven by a changing resource: what can live there changes as the material is consumed.

A dead organism therefore has several ecological futures at once: food, physical shelter, a nutrient source and a changing patch of habitat. Watching a log decay is watching those futures unfold slowly enough to notice.
