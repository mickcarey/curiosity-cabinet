# Why Siblings Aren't Photocopies 👪🧬

Two puppies can have the same parents and look surprisingly different. So can two children. If both parents supplied the ingredients, why didn't every offspring come out the same?

Because inherited information gets shuffled. And growing up involves much more than receiving the starting ingredients.

## 🃏 What gets passed on?

Living things carry inherited information in **DNA**, short for **deoxyribonucleic acid**. This long molecule helps cells—the tiny living units of a body—make the working parts they need.

DNA is organised into packages called **chromosomes**. Most human body cells have two sets, one originally supplied by the egg and one by the sperm.

When eggs and sperm form, matching chromosomes exchange sections. The resulting cells receive different mixtures of the parents' DNA. Each fertilisation brings together a new combination.

It resembles shuffling two decks, except pieces of the cards can be exchanged too. No wonder a brother or sister isn't a repeat order.

## 🌸 What a pea plant can teach us

A **gene** is a section of DNA used to make a working molecule. Different versions are called **alleles**.

For a simplified example, imagine a pea flower with a version that helps make purple colour and a version that doesn't. One working copy may make enough colour for the flower to look purple. We call that version **dominant** for this feature; the hidden effect is **recessive**.

Two purple-flowered plants carrying both versions can produce an offspring that receives the no-purple version from each parent. That offspring can have white flowers.

Gregor Mendel's experiments with peas helped reveal these patterns. They also showed why a feature can disappear from view in one generation and reappear in another.

“Dominant” doesn't mean better, stronger or more common. It describes what shows up when particular versions occur together.

The familiar ratios describe chances across many offspring. Four offspring are under no obligation to line up like the four squares in a textbook.

## 🧩 Most features have many contributors

Human height and eye colour involve many genetic differences. They are much more complicated than one switch marked “tall” or “brown”.

A gene can affect more than one feature. Its effect can depend on other genes. Nearby DNA sections also tend to travel together unless shuffling separates them.

[Gene regulation](gene-expression-and-regulation.md)—how cells control which information they use—adds another layer. Having instructions isn't the same as using them everywhere, all the time.

This is why claims about “the gene for” a whole personality trait deserve careful questioning. There is no tiny chromosome drawer labelled “always loses their shoes”.

## 🌦️ The surroundings help shape the result

Give genetically similar plants different amounts of water and they can grow to different heights. The inherited information still matters, but it works inside particular conditions.

Human families share food, housing, habits and experiences as well as DNA. A family resemblance alone doesn't tell us which of those caused it.

Scientists sometimes measure **heritability**: how much of the variation within a studied group is associated with genetic differences. This describes differences between members of that group, in those conditions.

It does **not** tell you what percentage of one person's height came from genes. Nor does it mean a feature cannot change when conditions change.

## 🔍 A clue is the beginning of an explanation

Researchers compare DNA and measured features across many people to find patterns. A DNA version that appears alongside a feature may help cause it—or may sit near another version that does.

Finding the connection gives researchers somewhere to investigate. They still need to work out what happens inside cells and how surroundings affect the outcome.

Some inheritance follows other routes. Mitochondria, the cell compartments that help use food energy, have their own DNA, normally passed through the egg in humans.

Family resemblance is a mixture of shared history, shuffled information and individual lives. [Evolution](evolution.md) asks what happens when inherited differences spread or disappear over many generations.
