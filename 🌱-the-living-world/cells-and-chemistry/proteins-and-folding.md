# Proteins: Shape, Movement and Molecular Work 🧩

Crack an egg into a hot pan and watch its clear, slippery white turn firm and opaque. You are watching proteins change shape—and discovering why uncooking an egg is a terrible afternoon project.

A **protein** is a large molecule, a structure made of connected atoms. Each protein contains one or more chains of smaller chemical units called **amino acids**. Different sequences of those units produce different shapes and behaviours. Your body uses proteins to build tissues, move muscles, carry oxygen and speed up chemical reactions. Food labels only tell a small part of their story.

Inside a **cell**, the tiny living unit that makes up an organism, proteins move in a crowded, watery mixture. The colourful ribbon in a textbook is a still image of something considerably less cooperative.

## 🔤 A chain with chemical differences

Most proteins use a standard set of twenty amino acids. Each has a shared backbone and a variable **side chain**. Some side chains carry electrical charge; others have unevenly distributed charge, making them **polar**. Some interact poorly with water, making them **hydrophobic**. Others are bulky or especially flexible. Their arrangement determines which interactions the chain can make.

Amino acids are linked by **peptide bonds**, chemical connections between neighbouring units. The peptide bond is relatively rigid, while adjacent backbone bonds allow rotation within limits imposed by neighbouring atoms. A chain therefore has many possible shapes, but not unlimited freedom.

Proteins are described at several levels:

| Level | What it describes |
| --- | --- |
| Primary | Amino-acid sequence |
| Secondary | Local coils (alpha helices) and folded sheets (beta sheets) |
| Tertiary | The three-dimensional organisation of one chain |
| Quaternary | The assembly of several chains into a larger complex |

These are descriptions of the same molecule at different scales. A single protein may contain several independently folding **domains**, linked by flexible regions.

Cells also alter proteins after making them. Attaching a small phosphate group can switch activity; adding sugars can change how a protein is recognised; cutting part off can activate a previously inactive protein. The chain you build is only the beginning of what you can do with it.

## 💧 How folding happens

In water, many soluble proteins bury hydrophobic side chains and expose more water-compatible surfaces. Attractions between nearby parts help hold the folded structure together. Sometimes strong links between sulfur atoms, called **disulfide bonds**, add extra stability. Heat can disrupt the balance: egg-white proteins unfold and stick together, producing the familiar cooked tangle.

Christian Anfinsen's experiments showed that some purified proteins could regain their structure and activity after unfolding, under suitable conditions. The amino-acid sequence contained enough information for recovery in that environment.

But folding cannot involve trying every possible shape. Even a modest chain has too many possibilities. Instead, nearby parts interact in ways that favour some arrangements over others. Think of a landscape with many slopes and hollows: there are several routes downhill, and a chain can get stuck partway. This **energy-landscape** picture describes a chemical process, not a protein deciding where to go. Folding may begin while a **ribosome**, the cell's protein-building machinery, is still making the chain.

Inside cells, **chaperones** help prevent inappropriate sticking and give proteins opportunities to fold or refold. They do not usually supply a separate blueprint for the final structure. They manage the conditions under which the chain finds a useful state.

## ⚙️ Why enzymes change everything

An **enzyme** speeds up a chemical reaction. Most enzymes are proteins. Your saliva, for example, contains an enzyme called amylase that starts breaking starch into smaller sugars. It helps dismantle lunch without needing to set lunch on fire.

A reaction often needs an initial push before it can proceed: its **activation barrier**. An enzyme lowers that barrier by holding molecules in useful positions or helping them pass through an unstable intermediate arrangement. The enzyme is available to do the job again afterwards.

Speeding up a reaction does not change its eventual balance, or **equilibrium**, between starting materials and products. Nor does an enzyme supply missing energy. Cells can link an energy-demanding reaction to one that releases usable energy, often involving **ATP**, adenosine triphosphate, a molecule continually made and used to power cellular work.

An enzyme's **active site** is the region where the molecule it acts on, its **substrate**, binds and reacts. The amino acids contributing to that site can be far apart along the chain but close together after folding. Proteins can also change shape when molecules bind. In **allostery**, binding at one site alters behaviour at another, allowing feedback control.

These mechanisms explain why a small sequence change can have a large effect, while another change has almost none. Position and context matter more than the mere fact that the inherited instructions changed—a change called a **mutation**.

Enzymes connect this subject directly to [digestion and cellular energy](../bodies-and-energy/digestion-and-cellular-energy.md). Other proteins provide structure: collagen supports tissues, while actin and tubulin form dynamic cellular scaffolds.

## 🧶 When there is no single stable shape

Some proteins, and regions within proteins, remain **intrinsically disordered** under ordinary cellular conditions. They move between many shapes instead of holding one stable fold.

Flexibility can be useful. A region may connect two domains, interact with several partners or fold only when it binds a target. Accessible flexible regions can also provide sites for regulatory modifications.

“Disordered” therefore does not mean broken. Function can depend on a range of shapes. This is why a structure alone rarely gives a complete explanation of what a protein does.

## 🦢 Misfolding and prions

Exposed sticky regions can lead proteins to aggregate. Cells can refold proteins or break them down. A **proteasome** is a molecular machine that dismantles selected proteins; **lysosomes** are compartments that digest material for recycling. Keeping the supply, condition and removal of proteins in balance is called **proteostasis**.

A prion takes the problem further: one protein shape encourages other copies to adopt the same shape. The change spreads without rewriting DNA—deoxyribonucleic acid, the molecule carrying inherited information. Mammalian prion diseases are the best-known examples, but heritable prion states also occur in fungi.

Other proteins can undergo seeded aggregation without sharing every property of infectious prions. Similar molecular behaviour is not enough to infer ordinary person-to-person transmission. The distinction matters more than the memorable phrase “contagious folding”.

## 🔍 What structure prediction can tell us

The 2021 AlphaFold2 work demonstrated a major advance in predicting protein structures from their amino-acid sequences. Predictions can help researchers identify folds, propose functions and design experiments.

A predicted structure still leaves important questions: how the protein moves, which partners it binds, whether a mutation changes stability, what chemical modifications matter and how the molecule behaves inside a cell. Confidence varies across a prediction, especially in flexible regions.

Solving a shape is a powerful step towards understanding a protein. It is not automatically a complete account of its chemistry. The related topics are [gene expression](../genetics-and-evolution/gene-expression-and-regulation.md), which produces the chain, and [immunity](../bodies-and-energy/immunity.md), where molecular recognition becomes especially consequential.
