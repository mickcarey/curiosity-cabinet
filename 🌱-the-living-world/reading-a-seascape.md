# Why Does the Beach Change Around the Corner? 🧭🌊

On one side of a headland, waves crash against rocks covered in tightly clinging shells. Around the corner, quiet mud holds burrows and patches of seagrass.

Same coast. Very different homes.

A **seascape** is the connected setting of shore, water and seabed. To read it, ask what each place supplies—and what living things there have to survive.

## 🌊 Three kinds of movement

**Waves** usually begin with wind and can shove water back and forth against the shore. **Tides** are the regular rise and fall of sea level, driven mainly by the Moon's and Sun's gravity. **Currents** are flows of water, caused by tides, wind and other differences in the water.

An animal on an exposed rock needs to cope with wave force. One higher up the shore must survive longer out of water. A creature in a current may receive a steady delivery of floating food.

A calm afternoon doesn't prove the place is usually sheltered. A headland's shape and the plants and animals living there record more than today's weather.

## 🪨 The floor changes the possibilities

Rock offers something to cling to. Moving sand can bury or scour organisms, favouring creatures that burrow or tolerate shifting ground. Mud often settles in quieter places, though sediment supply matters too.

An apparently empty mudflat may be full of animals beneath the surface. Burrows and small piles of sediment are clues to activity below.

The inhabitants can change the place. Seagrass slows water and traps particles. Mussel clusters create little gaps for other creatures. Large seaweeds provide shade and shelter. The residents help build the neighbourhood.

## 🧂 Seawater isn't equally salty everywhere

**Salinity** means saltiness. Rain can freshen a pool; evaporation can make it saltier. An **estuary**, where river water meets the sea, changes with rainfall, tides and mixing.

Fresher water can sit above denser salty water. So the surface and bottom may offer different conditions even in one place.

Animals need to keep their inner chemistry within workable limits. A sudden change in saltiness may be difficult even for something that appears comfortably wet.

## ☀️ Underwater sunshine has limits

Light fades with depth. Suspended particles can block it too. Kelp and seagrass need enough light to make food, so cloudy water can change which depths they can occupy.

Shallow pools can heat up. Shaded cracks may remain cooler. Higher shore animals may face long stretches of drying air, while lower animals stay submerged longer.

These differences help create bands of life across a shore. But a band of barnacles isn't a precise tide ruler: competition, grazing, shade and waves affect its boundaries too.

## 🔍 A bare patch has more than one possible story

Suppose one rocky area has thick seaweed and another has bare rock with many sea urchins.

Urchins eat seaweed, so grazing is a plausible explanation. But perhaps a storm or unusually warm water removed the seaweed first. Perhaps the patches differ in depth or exposure.

Repeated photographs, nearby comparisons and local monitoring can help. A single visit tells you what is present; explaining how it got that way takes more evidence.

Water colour is similarly slippery. Brown water may carry sediment or dissolved plant material. Green water may contain many tiny photosynthetic organisms. Colour alone doesn't tell you whether the water is polluted.

## 🐚 Make a small shore notebook

Record the tide stage, recent weather, type of bottom and whether the spot faces open waves or sheltered water. Notice grazers, filter feeders and hiding places as well as the most colourful animal.

Photograph a wider view alongside close-ups. Return at another tide or season from safe access, leaving attached animals and their shelters in place.

[Rockpools](coasts/rockpool-ecology.md) let you compare these conditions over a few steps. The [coastal examples](coasts/coastal-case-studies.md) show bigger contrasts. Once you start noticing the clues, “just a beach” becomes a rather unconvincing description.
