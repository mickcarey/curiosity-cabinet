# Walkable Case Studies 🚶

Anyone can say "nice trees." Literacy is knowing the absences.

This chapter is not a rewrite of the [hike guide](../🏙️-melbourne/hikes.md). Those notes tell you where to stand. This one tells you what the standing is saying. Five envelopes, Victoria first, Sunshine Coast as the second dialect. Each one in the same three moves: **what you see → what it implies → what would not belong.**

These walks sit on Country: Gadubanud and Eastern Maar in the Otways, Wurundjeri and Bunurong in the Dandenongs and on the Nepean, Wadawurrung on the granite and some of the dry outliers, Dja Dja Wurrung and neighbours in box-ironbark, Kabi Kabi and Jinibara on the Sunshine Coast. The reading is ecological. The place is not empty of people.

A fern gully is not a dune with extra effort. That is the whole shelf in one sentence.

The global method sits in [how to read a landscape](../🌱-the-living-world/15-how-to-read-a-landscape.md). Use it. Then argue with these five scenes until your first draft gets less embarrassing.

## 1. Otways Fern Gully 🌿

**Stand here:** Hopetoun Falls, or any wet southern-fall gully in the Great Otway National Park that smells rinsed and makes you lower your voice. Cretaceous sandstone and mudstone underfoot. Rainfall that can run 1000–2000 mm. Cool. The ridge is a rain trap. The gully is the trap's pocket.

**What you see.** Soft tree-ferns (*Dicksonia*) with trunks like wet coats. Rough tree-ferns further up the bank. Ground ferns, maybe filmy ferns on bark if the humidity is a lifestyle. Myrtle beech in the real rainforest pockets — small, dark, toothed leaves, mossed trunks — sometimes with blackwood. Tall eucalypts on the slopes above: mountain ash where it is wet enough and sheltered, messmate and mixed species as you step out of the gully. The air feels washed. Light is green, not gold. Litter is deep, fungal, quiet. A waterfall may be showing off because a harder sandstone band is doing geology in public.

**What it implies.** Reliable moisture. Shelter. Acid, organic, moderately heavy soils on Otway Group sediments. Fire as a visitor, not a landlord — especially in the gully tongue. This is the wet end of the Victorian codebook: wet sclerophyll grading into cool temperate rainforest (EVC 31) in the most protected lines. Bradstock's switches: biomass always there, availability usually off, until a shocking summer. The plants are the margin comment on [climate and rock](01-the-climate-and-the-rock.md) plus a south-facing fold in the map.

**What would not belong.** Ironbark. Grey box as the canopy. Wallum banksia. Coastal tea-tree as the dominant scrub. A grass-tree hillside as the main act. A "nothing grows here" reading. A tropical-rainforest shopping list — this is Gondwanan cool, not a Queensland fig party. Maples can be kept alive in a town garden nearby and still be a subplot. A second severe fire before ash seedlings seed would not belong either, in the sense that the cathedral would not survive it. Could ≠ should: taking tree ferns, "opening the gully up," or planting a dry-country grevillea because it is native are all ways to fail the reading.

If the ferns stop and the canopy opens and the litter thins, you have walked out of the sentence. That is the test. The gully is local.

## 2. Dandenongs Wet Sclerophyll 🌲

**Stand here:** Sherbrooke Falls loop, or the 1000 Steps if you want the same forest with extra quads. [The hike note](../🏙️-melbourne/hikes.md) already sold you lyrebirds and memorial stairs. Under that: rhyodacite hills, orographic rain, Mount Dandenong's totals around a metre to 1200 mm, deep acid loams on the sheltered faces.

**What you see.** Tall eucalypts. Mountain ash in the wetter, deeper, more sheltered stands — pale, straight, stocking of rough bark, smooth above, a ceiling that makes you feel briefly mortal. Messmate and mountain grey gum as you move toward damp rather than wet. Tree ferns in the creek lines, not always a closed myrtle-beech room. Broad-leaf wet-forest shrubs: musk daisy-bush, hazel pomaderris, blanket-leaf, the usual damp-sclerophyll chorus. Lyrebirds turning the litter like they own the soil chapter. Exotic oaks and maples in the villages below, which is a climate clue and a cultural subplot, not remnant text.

**What it implies.** Wet to damp sclerophyll. Rainfall high enough, soil deep enough, fire rare enough at the wet end that ash can exist, frequent enough over decades-to-centuries that the forest is still eucalypt rather than a closed beech room. This is Viridans' wet sclerophyll: tallest Victorian forest, deep litter, fleshy-fruited understorey, trees that can be 75 m and still younger than a redwood of the same height. The Dandenongs are not "Melbourne but leafy." They are a different envelope, which is why Keilor and Belgrave are not interchangeable gardens.

**What would not belong.** Box-ironbark as the native canopy. Calcareous-sand moonah. A wallum heath. "This is rainforest, so tropical plants would love my backyard" — the wet understorey is not a licence to export the gully. Mountain ash in a small suburban yard is a could that collapses into a should-not, and often into a will-not. Frequent hot fire would not belong in the sense that the ash stand cannot take it. A fertiliser regime aimed at those village maples would not belong in a remnant understorey of P-sensitive shrubs.

The absences are dry-country trees, salt-pruned scrub, and the assumption that all tall wet forest is the same wet forest. Otway gully and Dandenongs slope rhyme. They do not copy. The Otways have more of the cool rainforest extreme. The Dandenongs have more of the people, the roads, and the exotic subplot.

## 3. Coastal Tea-Tree On Calcareous Sand 🌊

**Stand here:** Point Nepean, the Sorrento–Portsea dunes, or a quieter calcarenite patch on the Bellarine or west of Cape Otway. The hike guide's Fort Nepean circuit is a door. The vegetation is Coastal Alkaline Scrub / Coastal Moonah Woodland, not a short Otway.

**What you see.** Low, dense, wind-combed canopy. Coast tea-tree (*Leptospermum laevigatum*) in thick stands, bark shredding in strips, small hard leaves, white flowers in season. Moonah (*Melaleuca lanceolata*) with tighter, darker, more "melaleuca" bark, often co-dominant. Coast beard-heath. Drooping she-oak in places. Coast wirilda. Saltbush and climbers in the gaps. Light can be bright at the dune crest and dim under a closed tea-tree roof, where the floor goes quiet except for mosses, a few orchids, and whatever has given up. The soil is pale, sandy, sometimes shelly, dry a day after rain. No tree ferns. No mountain ash. No messmate cathedral. The wind tastes like the Bass Strait end of the argument.

**What it implies.** Calcareous sand, alkaline chemistry, free drainage, salt exposure, a nutrient world that is not wallum and not krasnozem. Fire happens, but the headline is lime and wind and drought-between-showers. Tea-tree can close a stand and lock others out; in some sites, especially off its natural range or on richer soils, it behaves like a weed. Here, on the Nepean, it is native text. Moxham and colleagues reconstructed a peninsula that was this community at landscape scale, now fragmented, weeded, and loved to death by feet.

**What would not belong.** Myrtle beech. Mountain ash. Wallum banksia as the default (wrong sand chemistry, even if "banksia, coast, sand" sounded close). A fern-gully planting. A phosphorus-happy "native mix." Maples. The inference "degraded eucalypt forest that needs trees." Eucalypts may sit behind the dunes or in swales; they are not the sentence on the calcarenite crest. Also not belonging: assuming every Victorian coastal scrub is this. Siliceous dunes further around the coast can read as heath and banksia, which is a different chapter 02.

Could ≠ should: planting coastal tea-tree because it is tough, in a place where it is *not* the remnant, is how a native becomes a weed. The codebook is local.

## 4. Dry Box-Ironbark ☀️

**Stand here:** not a Melbourne day-trip waterfall. Castlemaine Diggings, Heathcote, Rushworth, the Brisbane Ranges or Werribee Gorge outliers if you want it closer. Undulating, 150–600 m, rainfall often 500–800 mm. Stony, auriferous, thoroughly used ground. The gold story and the vegetation story are the same poor rock.

**What you see.** Light. You can walk between the trees without becoming a fern. Canopy to maybe 20–25 m, often less, often more open than a photograph from the 1850s would have been because the best timber and the best soil were taken. Ironbark on the high, hungry ridges — dark, hard, fissured. Red box on ridges too. Grey box on the better flats. Yellow box and yellow gum toward the kinder lower slopes and gullies. Red stringybark on some higher stony ground. Understorey: wattles, small-leaf shrubs, a lot of grass and herbs, not a closed wet scrub. In spring it can flower like it is showing off. In late summer it looks like a stripped bone and is still not dead. Charcoal in the bark, grass-trees in some of the heathy outliers, no tree-fern gully unless a creek is cheating hard.

**What it implies.** Dry, poor, fire as furniture, soil that was never a Dandenongs loam. Slope sorting is the reading: ironbark and red box up, grey box and yellow box down. This is the envelope people call "nothing grows here" when they mean "nothing grows *soft*." Box-ironbark once covered millions of hectares in northern Victoria; most of the good land was alienated by 1900, which is why the remnants sit on the worst soils. Mining, coppicing, firewood, and gold did the rest. What you see is a woodland that has been through a lot and is still a woodland.

**What would not belong.** Mountain ash. Myrtle beech. Soft tree-fern as a native. Coastal moonah. Wallum heath. A closed wet-forest understorey. High-phosphorus garden beds "to help the natives along." The inference "degraded wet forest." Also: a tidy European park with a lawn and a few ironbarks as ornaments is a garden, not this community. Could ≠ should includes dumping "wildflower mixes" and planting a rainforest corner because you like the contrast. The contrast is the evidence you have not read the site.

If you find a wet line of manna gums and a fern along a creek, do not generalise it upslope. The gully is a water cheat, same as everywhere.

## 5. Sunshine Coast Rainforest Edge / Wallum 🌞

**Stand here:** two scenes, twenty minutes apart, which is the pedagogical cruelty of this coast. Hinterland wet gully or Mapleton/Kondalilla-style wet sclerophyll and rainforest edge. Then the sandplain — Sippy Downs was the "remnant block" nobody wanted, open wallum, swampy, sandy, full of holes. [The origin story](../🕰️-origin-stories/nambour-sippy-downs-and-the-sunshine-coast.md) is the human version. This is the plant version.

### The wet edge

**What you see.** Tall eucalypts that are not Victorian surnames: flooded gum, blackbutt, tallowwood, brush box, maybe turpentine. Canopy 50–80%, not closed. Understorey trying to be vine forest: paler-leaved rainforest seedlings, vines, ferns, a green that has more humidity in it than a Dandenongs winter. Figs where the rainforest has actually taken the lease. Summer-wet air. The winter is mild compared with an Otway July. Fire scars on the sclerophyll, not on the true rainforest cores.

**What it implies.** High rainfall (Nambour-scale, on the order of 1700 mm), summer-dominant, sheltered, better or at least wetter soils than the sandplain, fire rare enough for rainforest taxa to recruit underneath. Wet sclerophyll as sliding door: keep fire out and the understorey closes; burn it and the eucalypts hold. This is not Otway myrtle beech. It is the subtropical dialect of the same job.

**What would not belong.** Wallum banksia as the canopy. Coastal tea-tree of the Nepean type as the remnant. Victorian mountain ash. Ironbark-box dry woodland. A "needs fertiliser" reading of the rainforest floor. Assuming this edge will be rainforest by Christmas if you pull a few wattles.

### The wallum

**What you see.** Pale sand. Light. Sky. Wallum banksia with warty trunks and stiff toothed leaves. Scribbly gum. Bloodwoods. Heath shrubs. Grass trees. Paperbarks in the wet tiles. Sedges in the swales. You can see distance. The ground can be wet enough to soak a boot and then, on the rise, dry enough to squeak. Coffee rock downstairs if you had a pit. No closed vine forest. No flooded-gum cathedral. The plants look hardy because they are specialised, not because they are waiting for compost.

**What it implies.** Siliceous sand, acid, low phosphorus, pan, seasonal water-table mosaic. Fire in the heath career plan. Kabi Kabi named the banksia; the word stretched to the ecosystem. Selectors judged it useless for agriculture. That judgement is the tell: if a 19th-century farmer did not want it, you are probably in a specialist poor-sand system, not a failed rainforest.

**What would not belong.** Rainforest planting as "restoration." Lime. Phosphorus. A Dandenongs wet-forest package. Nepean calcareous tea-tree as if all coastal sand were one sand. Mountain ash, obviously. The inference "empty, so develop it" — Sippy Downs already did the punchline, from remnant block to campus. Could ≠ should includes loving wallum so much you "enrich" it.

The test of this case is the **pair**. If you can stand in the wet edge and then in the wallum and not mix up the shopping lists, the shelf has done its job. Same region. Same method. Different first two cards, different soil, different fire, different absences.

## The Absences Are The Exam 📝

| Envelope | Belongs | Does not belong |
|---|---|---|
| Otway fern gully | Myrtle beech, *Dicksonia*, wet-end eucalypts | Ironbark canopy, wallum banksia, "tropical" shopping list |
| Dandenongs wet sclerophyll | Mountain ash, messmate, wet-forest shrubs, gully ferns | Box-ironbark, moonah dune, exporting the gully to Keilor |
| Calcareous tea-tree | Tea-tree, moonah, beard-heath, she-oak | Ash forest, wallum banksia-as-default, fern gully |
| Box-ironbark | Ironbark, boxes, yellow gum, light, grass | Tree fern as native, mountain ash, rainforest corner |
| Rainforest edge (SEQ) | Flooded gum / blackbutt, vine-forest understorey | Wallum canopy, Victorian ash, dry box |
| Wallum | *B. aemula*, scribbly gum, heath, paperbark swales | Rainforest restoration, lime, P, calcareous tea-tree |

If your first walk produced a list with no absences, you described a garden centre. Try again.

## Mini-Cases, Same Moves, Smaller Stage 🔍

Not every walk is a national park. The method still has to work on a creek behind a suburb.

**You Yangs granite, rain shadow.** Tors, shallow sandy soil, ~450 mm, open woodland, red gum cheating in the gullies. Implies: dry, hungry, evaporative, fire-aware. Would not belong: Otway fern package, mountain ash, a "this needs compost and maples" reading. [Chapter 01](01-the-climate-and-the-rock.md) used this as the other half of the Otway sentence. Here it is the exam: if you can feel the granite and still want a tree fern, start again.

**A Melbourne western-plains creek.** Basalt, maybe a red gum line, weeds, a bike path. Implies: volcanic clay, winter wet, summer hard, remnant grassland/woodland if you squint. Would not belong: Sherbrooke understorey as "native planting," unless you are installing a hose-fed lie and you know it.

**A Sunshine Coast suburban wallum leftover.** Pale sand, a few banksias, a drain, a shopping centre. Implies: the sandplain is still speaking under the car park. Would not belong: rainforest feature trees and a lime-happy lawn as "rehabilitation."

These are shabbier than Hopetoun Falls. They are also where most people actually live. Literacy that only works at a waterfall is a weekend hobby.

## What "Would Not Belong" Is For 🚫

The absences are not a purity test. They are how you catch a swap.

People plant the Dandenongs in the west because the Dandenongs were the pretty walk. People plant rainforest on wallum because rainforest is the prestige green. People plant ironbark in a gully because they liked the bark. Each swap is a failed inference wearing a native-plant tag.

"Would not belong" means: this plant's envelope is a different climate, rock, soil, fire, or water story. It might live with a hose. It will not tell the truth about the site. A garden is allowed to lie. A reading is not.

If you want the sports version: knowing the ground means knowing which match cannot be played here. You do not take a surfboard to a basketball court because both are "Australian sport." You do not take myrtle beech to a box-ironbark ridge because both are "trees."

Work the five big cases until the absences feel obvious. Then try a shabby creek. If the absences are still obvious, you are literate enough to be dangerous, which is when [chapter 06](06-therefore-you-could-grow-this.md)'s brake matters. Could ≠ should. The case studies are not planting plans. They are reading plans. The bush remains the author.

## One Composite Walk, If You Only Have A Weekend 🗺️

From Keilor Downs, a stubborn person could, in theory, stack the Victorian cases in two days and then fly north for the dialect. You do not have to. The point of a composite is the contrast, not the itinerary.

- **West, granite:** You Yangs. Light, tors, rain shadow. Dry woodland. Absences: ferns, ash, rainforest.
- **East, rhyodacite:** Dandenongs. Tall wet sclerophyll. Absences: ironbark canopy, moonah dune.
- **South-west, sandstone:** Otway gully. Ferns, maybe myrtle beech. Absences: box-ironbark, wallum.
- **South, calcarenite:** Point Nepean. Tea-tree, wind, lime sand. Absences: mountain ash, wallum banksia-as-default.
- **North of the Divide, another day:** box-ironbark. Light, iron, gold country. Absences: tree fern as native, wet-forest shopping list.

Then, when you are on the Sunshine Coast for the human story, add the pair: rainforest edge and wallum leftover. If those two feel as different as the Otways and the You Yangs, the method has travelled. If they feel like "Queensland, so lush," start at [chapter 01](01-the-climate-and-the-rock.md) again. Lush is not a diagnosis. It is a postcard.

Do not eat the mushrooms. Do not pocket the orchids. Do not "rescue" a grass tree. The case studies are for reading. The living-world series will keep teaching the science; this shelf was the walk. When you get it wrong — and you will — the correction is another walk, not a louder opinion.

Inference is a first draft. Check it. The bush is patient. Your certainty does not have to be.

The absences are the questions the journalists forgot to ask. If the press conference is a box-ironbark ridge, do not report it as a fern gully with low self-esteem.

## How To Use These Without Getting Cocky 🎯

Check. A plantation, a picnic ground, a weed wave after fire, and a council planting will all impersonate a case study. The method from the expedition map still stands: notice, name the kind, infer, ask what else would belong, then ask what would die here looking surprised.

Take the [hikes](../🏙️-melbourne/hikes.md). Take a Sunshine Coast track that actually has a remnant, not just a name. Write one sentence per stop. Argue with [chapter 03](03-eucalypts-as-a-reading-key.md) and [chapter 04](04-the-supporting-cast.md). Then look up the EVC or the Queensland regional ecosystem later, like an adult.

When the water starts looking like it is also trying to tell you something — rockpools, estuaries, the barnacle line — that is [Reading The Water](../🌊-reading-the-water/01-tides-salt-and-the-shape-of-the-shore.md)'s job. This shelf is the land. The land was enough for one walk.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Every site is a mosaic.** These five are types, not GPS pins that never fail.
- **Weeds, deer, drying, and fire-interval collapse** are rewriting the case studies while we use them as textbooks.
- **Pre-European structure** of box-ironbark and of coastal woodland is still argued. Openness, fire, and kangaroo grass percentages are not settled.
- **Wallum versus wet sclerophyll boundaries** on the Sunshine Coast are being suburbanised faster than they are being read.
- **The method will travel.** The surnames will not. If you take this to another state and do not restart at climate and rock, that is on you.
