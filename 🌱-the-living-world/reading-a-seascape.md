# Reading a Seascape 🧭

A sheltered muddy inlet and an exposed rocky headland can sit a few kilometres apart while supporting very different communities. Water movement, exposure to air, salinity, light and the seafloor all help determine who can live there.

Reading a seascape means using visible evidence to form a testable explanation. You can begin with the shore's shape, the sediment or an organism. The useful habit is to connect those observations rather than turn one species into a universal environmental diagnosis.

## 🌊 Distinguish waves, tides and currents

Waves repeatedly accelerate water near a shore. Breaking waves and surge impose forces that favour strong attachment, flexible bodies, low profiles or protected positions.

Tides change water level and generate currents. Their range and timing depend on the local coastline and basin as well as astronomical forcing. Currents can deliver food, oxygen and larvae even where the surface looks relatively calm.

A calm afternoon is weak evidence for a sheltered wave climate. Look at the headland's orientation, exposure to open water, sediment and attachment strategies of resident organisms. Conversely, rough-looking water is not itself evidence of pollution.

The mechanisms and Victorian examples are developed in [tides, salt and shorelines](coasts/tides-salt-and-shorelines.md).

## 🪨 The bottom controls the available footholds

Rock offers attachment surfaces and crevices. Mobile sand favours burrowing organisms and species able to cope with burial or abrasion. Mud often accumulates where finer particles can settle, although its presence also depends on sediment supply.

The sediment surface can be oxygenated while deeper layers are oxygen-poor. Burrowing animals alter that pattern by moving sediment and irrigating their burrows. An apparently bare flat can contain a substantial community below the visible surface.

Organisms can modify the habitat themselves. Seagrass slows flow and traps sediment; mussel beds create crevices; kelp provides shade and structure. The community is partly a response to conditions and partly a cause of them.

## 🧂 Salinity varies through time and depth

Open seawater is commonly near 35 parts per thousand salinity, but an estuary can range widely with rainfall, tides and evaporation. Fresher water may overlie denser salt water rather than instantly mixing into one intermediate value.

Pools isolated at low tide can become saltier through evaporation or fresher after rain. Temperature and oxygen can change at the same time. A species' presence may reflect tolerance of these fluctuations, not a preference for the conditions observed at noon.

Water colour is also ambiguous. Brown water can contain suspended sediment or dissolved organic compounds. Green water may contain abundant phytoplankton. Clear blue water can have low nutrient concentrations. Appearance alone cannot establish contamination or ecological condition.

## ☀️ Light and heat are local constraints

Depth matters because light diminishes underwater. Turbidity and shading can reduce available light without any change in depth. Kelp and seagrass therefore respond to the underwater light environment, not simply whether the day looks sunny.

Temperature includes seasonal conditions, marine heatwaves and short-term heating in shallow water. A pool that stays submerged can nevertheless become physiologically stressful. A shaded crevice nearby may provide refuge.

Exposure to air matters separately. An intertidal animal must tolerate some combination of drying, heating and interruption to feeding. That produces visible patterns across a shore.

## 🐚 Zonation is a biological pattern, not a tide gauge

Rocky shores often show bands of organisms associated with different exposure levels. But the boundaries reflect several interacting causes:

- Physical tolerance of drying, heat and wave force.
- Competition for space and light.
- Grazing and predation.
- Larval supply and successful recruitment.
- Local shade, crevices and wave splash.

A barnacle band is therefore not a precise mark of one tide height. Its upper and lower boundaries can arise for different reasons. A fixed photograph can reveal a pattern without explaining it.

[Rockpool ecology](coasts/rockpool-ecology.md) shows how to compare these conditions over a small area, while [coastal animals](coasts/coastal-animals.md) links body structures to feeding and survival.

## 🔍 Work from evidence to alternatives

Suppose one rocky patch has dense seaweed and a neighbouring patch has bare rock with many urchins.

**Observation:** different canopy cover and different visible grazer abundance.

**Possible explanation:** grazing helps maintain the bare patch.

**Other possibilities:** the patches differ in wave exposure, depth, recent heat stress or recruitment history. Urchins may have increased after canopy loss rather than initiated it.

**Useful checks:** repeat photographs, similar-depth comparisons, local monitoring records and surveys of remaining holdfasts or juvenile kelp. Manipulative experiments can establish mechanisms more strongly than a single walk can.

The same reasoning applies to a missing seagrass meadow or an unusually green pool. A plausible story is a starting point, not a completed diagnosis.

## 🥾 A useful shore visit

Record location, tide stage, recent weather, exposure and the kind of bottom. Note several organisms or functional groups rather than only the most photogenic animal. Photographs of the wider setting often help more than a close-up with no scale or context.

Repeat visits reveal changes that one low tide cannot: recruitment, seasonal vegetation, storm effects and freshwater pulses. Observe from secure access and leave organisms attached and shelters intact; disturbing the patch changes the evidence you came to see.
