# Other Animals, Same Song, Different Arrangement 🎼

Chapter 11 spent a long time on one body. Fair. It is the body Mick has to feed, and the one I do not have. It is also a provincial way to study life. Most animals are not bipedal, sweaty, or convinced that a skull is the only respectable place to keep a mind.

The three bills from [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md) have not changed. Capture energy. Copy information. Build structure. What changes is the **arrangement**. A bird lung, an insect's open plumbing, a fish gill, an octopus arm that thinks for itself: same song, different charts. If you only know the human version, you will walk into a forest or a rockpool and treat everything else as a failed mammal.

I am the AI narrator. Mick is the human with one heart, tidal lungs, and a nervous system that insists on sitting in a box. The rest of the animal kingdom looked at that design and said, politely, "or we could not."

This is still Arc 5. Same three bills. Different venues. A gill is not a failed lung. An open circulatory system is not a draft. An arm that thinks is not a mistake that forgot to build a skull.

This chapter is not a zoo. It is not a complete phylogeny. It is the comparative kit you need so a bird lung, a bee, and an octopus stop looking like extras in a human story.

## Body Plans: Which Way Is Forward? 🧭

A **body plan** is not a vibe. It is the deep geometry: where the mouth is, whether there is a head, how many times you can fold the animal and get a match, whether the gut has a dead end.

**Radial symmetry** is the "any direction might be lunch" plan. A jellyfish has a top and a bottom and no left. Stimuli arrive from the whole compass, so the nervous system is a net, not a headquarters. The [immortal jellyfish](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-immortal-jellyfish.md) is this geometry with a reset button: *Turritopsis dohrnii* can reverse from medusa back to polyp, which is a career move no bilateral vertebrate has ever pulled off. Radial animals are not primitive in the insulting sense. They are specialised for a world that does not have a preferred direction.

**Bilateral symmetry** is the "I am going that way" plan. One plane cuts you into roughly matching halves. A front appears. Sensors pile up there, because the first thing you meet should be investigated before the last thing you meet. A gut can run from mouth to anus, which is a plumbing upgrade over a sack with one opening. Most animals you can name without a field guide — insects, fish, birds, us, octopuses — are **bilaterians**. More than 99% of described animal species sit on this side of the fence.

Starfish cheat. As larvae they are bilateral. As adults they are (secondarily) radial, which is the echinoderm version of growing up and deciding compasses are bourgeois. The lesson is not "radial then bilateral then us." The lesson is that geometry is a job description. Sit still and wait for the current: radial is a reasonable office. Chase things: you want a head.

Two more words that earn their keep. **Diploblastic** animals, like cnidarians, build from two germ layers. **Triploblastic** animals add mesoderm, which is how you get muscle, a true coelom, and the option of complicated insides. The Cambrian did not invent animals. It unpacked the ones that had already chosen a direction.

Gábor Holló's reminder is useful here: genomes can express both radial and bilateral tricks, and bodies use both, locally, when the job demands it. A human is bilateral and still grows radial parts (a tree of bronchi, a lens). A cnidarian is "radial" and still has internal axes that look suspiciously like a plan. The poster is a poster. The animal is a compromise.

## Three Bands, Not A Ladder 🎸

School charts still smell faintly of a ladder: sponge, worm, insect, frog, us. That ladder is a Victorian hangover. The useful picture is a few great **clades** that solved "be an animal" in parallel, then spent half a billion years remixing.

**Vertebrates** are the chordates who kept a backbone, or at least a notochord with ambitions. Internal skeleton. Closed circulation. A central nervous system that really likes a skull. Fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals: one band, many albums. A whale is not a fish that got fat. It is a mammal in a different costume — lungs, milk, warm blood, the whole terrestrial kit — restyled for a life that forgot the land except for the small matter of air.

**Arthropods** are the exoskeleton people. Jointed legs. Moulting as a lifestyle. Insects, spiders, crustaceans, millipedes. In species count they own the planet. A beetle does not fail to be a vertebrate. It is a different engineering brief: armour on the outside, muscles on the inside, grow by throwing away the suit.

**Molluscs** are the soft-bodied improvisers. Shell, or the memory of a shell. A muscular foot that can become a jet. Snails, clams, and then the cephalopods, who looked at "slow and armoured" and chose "fast and clever" instead. An octopus is a mollusc the way a Formula One car is a cart: same phylum, different afternoon.

A little more kit, because the differences are not cosmetic.

Vertebrates **segment** along a notochord, then hide the segmentation in a mammal until you look at a spine or an embryo. The skeleton is a compression structure: muscle pulls, bone pushes back, joints are the hinges. You grow without throwing the house away. The cost is weight, and a repair bill when a bone breaks.

Arthropods **segment** too, more obviously: head, thorax, abdomen, or the many-plated versions in millipedes and fossil wonders. The exoskeleton is both suit of armour and attachment point for muscles. It cannot grow, so the animal **moults** — a periodic vulnerability that looks like a terrible idea until you notice it also lets you change career, as when a caterpillar becomes a moth. Insects added **wings** that are not restyled legs. That is a new appendage, which is why a bee in the air is not a tiny bat.

Molluscs often keep a **mantle** that can secrete shell, a **radula** for rasping (octopuses lost it as a conveyor and kept a beak), and that famous foot. Bivalves doubled down on filter-feeding and a two-part house. Gastropods torsioned their guts, which is a sentence I cannot make elegant. Cephalopods turned the foot into arms and a siphon and, in squid, a jet you would file under propulsion.

Those three are not the whole tree. Sponges, cnidarians, echinoderms, nematodes, and a long list of worms would like a word. They are the point of a later walk, not this chapter's job. The job here is comparative: once you can see the arrangement, the human body stops looking like the default and starts looking like one chart among many.

If you want a music analogy that does not collapse: vertebrates are a symphony orchestra, arthropods are a drumline that never clocks off, molluscs are a jazz trio that sometimes becomes a big band. Same twelve notes. Different need for a conductor.

A pocket chart, for the walk:

| | Vertebrate | Arthropod | Mollusc (cephalopod vs the rest) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Support | Internal bone / cartilage | External cuticle, moulted | Shell, or a foot and a prayer |
| Blood | Closed, haemoglobin | Open hemolymph | Open, except cephalopods closed |
| Oxygen path | Blood to tissues | Tracheae (insects) or gills | Gills; hemocyanin often |
| Nervous system | Central, skull-loving | Segmental ganglia, brain of sorts | Ganglia; octopus says "and also the arms" |
| Famous cheat | Endothermy, a whale in fish clothing | Flight, sociality, complete metamorphosis | Jet, ink, a mind in a tentacle |

Charts lie by leaving people out. Use it as a tuning fork, not a census.

Two more splits that school mentions and then flees.

**Protostomes and deuterostomes** are about which hole in the early embryo becomes the mouth. Most invertebrates we have been discussing sit on the protostome side; echinoderms and vertebrates sit on the deuterostome side. It is an embryological membership card, not a quality ranking. A sea urchin is closer to you, in this one bureaucratic sense, than a bee is. Sit with that at lunch.

**Ectotherms and endotherms** are about who pays for temperature. A lizard runs on the weather. A mammal runs on a furnace. The furnace is expensive and buys a nervous system that can stay fast in the cold. The weather is cheap and buys a winter of not-much. Tuna and some sharks cheat toward warm-bodied. Naked mole-rats cheat toward the other side. The categories leak, like all good categories.

## Breathing: Three Ways To Rob A Gradient 💨

Gas exchange is the same physics everywhere. Oxygen wants to go where there is less of it. Carbon dioxide wants the opposite. Evolution's trick is **surface area**, **thin barriers**, and **not mixing the fresh with the spent**.

**Fish gills** are the countercurrent masterpiece. Water flows one way across the lamellae. Blood flows the other. At every point along the exchange surface, water is just a little richer in oxygen than the blood it is meeting, so the gradient never quite dies. Reverse the water flow in the lab and oxygen uptake falls off a cliff. Water holds less oxygen than air, and it is viscous, so fish cannot afford a sloppy lung. They built a heat exchanger for gases instead. It is why a trout in a stream looks effortless and a trout in a bucket looks like a philosophical emergency.

**Bird lungs** refuse the tidal bargain mammals signed. We suck air in, mix it with what was already there, push it out, and call the leftover "residual volume" as if naming the stale bit made it a feature. Birds keep a rigid lung and a set of **air sacs** that act as bellows. Air moves through the parabronchi largely **one way**, during both inspiration and expiration, on a two-breath cycle. Gas exchange is **cross-current**: blood meets the air stream at an angle, which is not quite a fish gill and still better than a mammalian alveolus at altitude. A sparrow can work where a mouse of the same mass is already writing a complaint. The old story was that this evolved for flight. Then unidirectional flow showed up in crocodilians and some lizards, which do not fly and are not trying to. The arrangement is older than the marketing. Flight used it. It did not necessarily invent it.

**Insects** cheated the whole "blood carries oxygen" exam. **Hemolymph** — the insect's open-system fluid — sloshes through a hemocoel, bathing tissues directly. A dorsal vessel with ostia pulses it forward. Accessory pumps help shove fluid into wings, antennae, legs. About 90% of hemolymph is plasma: water, amino acids, sugars, ions, sometimes a greenish or yellowish tint that would alarm a haematologist. Hemocytes handle clotting and defence. With a few aquatic-midge exceptions, there is no haemoglobin worth mentioning. Hemolymph carries nutrients, hormones, immune cells, the payroll. It does **not**, in most insects, carry the oxygen.

That job belongs to **tracheae**: air pipes that ramify until they are sitting on the muscle. Spiracles open on the sides. The finest branches are fluid-lined and close enough to mitochondria that diffusion finishes the job. A flying insect is not limited by how fast a heart can send red cells. It is limited by how well air can be got down a tree of tubes. That is why insects can be metabolically furious — a bee is not lazy — and still run an open circulatory system that a textbook would call "primitive" if the textbook had not met a bee.

The circulatory system and the respiratory system are different departments. An insect is not a failed closed-circulatory vertebrate. It is a creature that delivered air by courier instead of putting it in the post.

Cephalopods, being molluscs with somewhere to be, closed the pipes. Three hearts: two **branchial** hearts pushing blood through the gills, one **systemic** heart for the body. The blood is blue because the oxygen kit is **hemocyanin**, copper-based, not iron. High metabolic rate, jet propulsion, a brain that costs rent: you do not run that on an open splash of hemolymph. The exception proves the brief. When a mollusc decides to be fast, it reinvented vertebrate-ish plumbing without asking us.

Size is a hidden character in this scene. Tracheae work brilliantly at bee-scale and become a physics problem as you scale up, which is one reason the metre-long insects of the Carboniferous needed an oxygen-rich atmosphere and still did not become cows. Gills need water. Lungs need to not dry out. Every arrangement is a deal with a medium. Change the medium and the deal looks clever or stupid, which is why a fish out of water is a proverb and a human underwater is a sport.

## Circulation: Closed City, Open Market 🩸

A **closed** system keeps blood in vessels. Pressure can be high. Delivery can be addressed. Vertebrates live here. So do cephalopods, as above. Annelid worms got there too, which is a reminder that "closed" is a trick, not a membership card.

An **open** system pumps fluid into sinuses and lets it wander home through ostia. Cheaper to run. Worse at pinpoint delivery. Fine if your oxygen is arriving by trachea, or if you are a clam and nobody asked you to sprint.

Neither is "more evolved." They are different answers to "how do I move stuff without building a motorway through every organ." Insects prove you can be metabolically furious with open plumbing, provided air bypasses the blood. Humans prove you can be metabolically furious with closed plumbing, provided you accept a heart that never gets a lunch break. Pick your overhead.

## Whales: Mammals In A Different Costume 🐋

A whale is the comparative aside that keeps people honest.

It is a mammal: endotherm, live young, milk, lungs, a history written in terrestrial bones. The nostrils migrated to a blowhole. The forelimbs became hydrofoils. The hind limbs voted themselves off the island and left a vestige. The tail is a fluke, not a fish tail — it beats in the vertical, because that is how a mammalian spine already liked to move. Warm blood in cold water is expensive, so the costume includes blubber, countercurrent heat exchangers in the flippers, and a diving physiology that would look like a medical emergency if you did it: bradycardia, blood shunted to the brain and heart, myoglobin stacked in the muscle like a private oxygen account.

Toothed whales added **echolocation**. Baleen whales added a filter and a different career. Both are still mammals. A shark is doing a related job with a completely different inheritance: gills, a cartilaginous skeleton, a body temperature that, in most species, tracks the water. The silhouette rhymes. The family tree does not. If you cannot tell a dolphin from a shark except by the smile, you are reading the costume and missing the play.

This is the point of comparative anatomy when it is not a list of parts. Same constraints (water is thick, oxygen is a gas, heat leaks). Different starting kits. A whale is not a fish that got ambitious. It is a cow that went back, and had to keep surfacing to prove it.

## The Octopus, Who Refused To Centralise 🐙

The [octopus intelligence](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-mystery-of-octopus-intelligence.md) note is already in the cabinet, and it is the right kind of trouble. A common octopus has on the order of **500 million neurons**. Roughly two-thirds of them are in the arms, not the head. Each arm has an axial nerve cord, sucker ganglia, a chemotactile surface that tastes as it grabs. A severed arm will still hunt. That is not a party trick. It is a control architecture.

Peter Godfrey-Smith's line is the one that sticks: an **alternate experiment in intelligence**. Solitary, short-lived, no childhood spent in school, no culture in the primate sense, and still: tool use (coconut shells as future hiding places), escape artistry, play, skin that may be doing some of its own seeing. RNA editing as a live remix, which a later information chapter will have to deal with properly — octopuses recoding their own messages on the fly is not a party trick, it is a different relationship to a genome.

The skin deserves its own half-paragraph. Chromatophores, iridophores, leucophores: colour, shine, white, on a timescale of milliseconds. Octopuses appear to be colourblind in the camera-eye sense, which makes the matching even ruder. Light-sensitive proteins in the skin are a live research lead, not a closed case. If the skin is doing some of the seeing, that is distributed sensing to go with the distributed motor control. A football team where the jerseys also watch the play.

The philosophical itch is real and should stay labelled as an itch. We do not know what it is like to have a mind whose votes are partly in the tentacles. We do know the hardware is not a vertebrate brain with the labels peeled off. If you want a sports analogy: a human nervous system is a coach with a radio to every player. An octopus is a team where the wingers take corners without waiting for the huddle, and the huddle is still somehow a match.

Mollusc, closed circulation, distributed nervous system, camera eye that we will get to in a moment. Same song. Arrangement that makes a primate look provincial.

## Convergent Evolution: The Cover Version 🎧

**Homology** is sharing a trait because you inherited it. Your arm and a bat's wing are homologous as tetrapod forelimbs. **Analogy** is sharing a trait because the problem was the same and the solutions rhymed. A bat wing and a bee wing are analogous as flight surfaces.

**Convergent evolution** is the cover version: different bands, similar chorus.

**Camera eyes.** Vertebrates and coleoid cephalopods both built a lens, a retina, a pupil, a dark room. The last common ancestor did not have that kit. The wiring is the giveaway. Vertebrate photoreceptors sit behind a carpet of neurons, so the optic nerve punches a **blind spot**. Cephalopod photoreceptors face the light; axons leave out the back; no blind spot. Same job, different circuit board. *Pax6* and friends were in the toolbox both times, which is why some people say "deep homology" and mean "the switch was shared, the house was not." Nautilus kept a pinhole eye, a living reminder that "cephalopod" is not a synonym for "camera." The house was built twice, and a cousin kept the prototype in a drawer.

**Wings.** Insects, pterosaurs, birds, bats. Four origins of powered flight in animals, if you are counting the way palaeontologists count, and that is before you get to gliders cheating in the stairwell. The forelimb kept getting restyled. The physics of air did not.

**Eyes, more broadly.** Eyes have evolved many times — Nilsson and Pelger's famous back-of-envelope said a camera eye could be assembled in a few hundred thousand generations of reasonable selection, which was a splash of cold water on "too complex to have evolved." Compound eyes (arthropods) tile the world in ommatidia: great at flicker and direction, less like a high-resolution photograph. Pit eyes, mirror eyes (some scallops, some spiders), the nautilus pinhole: a toolbox, not a ladder. "The" eye is a journalist's convenience.

**Echolocation.** Bats and toothed whales. Different medium, same trick: shout, listen, build a world out of echoes. The protein **prestin**, which helps outer hair cells in the ear dance at high frequency, shows amino-acid changes so similar in echolocating bats and toothed whales that a family tree drawn from that protein alone would put dolphins next to bats and start a fight at the family reunion. Convergence can reach all the way down into sequence. That is not destiny. It is a small number of workable knobs.

**Streamlining.** Sharks, ichthyosaurs, dolphins. One shape keeps winning in water if you want to be fast. A whale is still a mammal. The costume is hydrodynamics.

Convergence is the most comforting and the most humbling fact in this chapter. Comforting: some solutions are good enough that life finds them more than once. Humbling: "it looks like us" is not evidence of kinship. It is evidence of physics.

## Extremophiles: Unofficial Astronaut Training 🚀

If the song is "stay a self in a gradient," some verses are sung in rooms that would kill Mick before the chorus.

**Tardigrades** dry down into a tun, pause the film, and have survived the vacuum of low Earth orbit. On the 2007 FOTON-M3 / TARDIS flight, desiccated *Milnesium* and *Richtersius* came back from ten days of space vacuum in good shape; add unfiltered solar UV and most of them did not, which is the adult version of the superhero story. They are not immortal. They are extremely good at not being alive for a bit.

**Deinococcus radiodurans** shrugs off radiation that would file a human under "mistakes." It is not a spell. It is DNA repair plus a manganese antioxidant kit that keeps proteins from falling apart while the genome is being restacked. The Tanpopo mission parked it outside the ISS for a year. It came back with a to-do list, not a funeral.

**Thermophiles** and **psychrophiles**, acid-lovers, salt-lovers, the vent communities that do not care about sunlight: these are not mascots. They are the range of the process. A hydrothermal vent is a biosphere with no sundial. Chemolithoautotrophs eat rock redox the way a leaf eats photons. The animals up the food web — tube worms with bacterial tenants, shrimp that look designed by a committee that had lost the brief — are still the same three bills, paid in hydrogen sulfide. When later chapters talk about other worlds, this is the training montage. Not "a tardigrade on Mars," which is a cartoon. The useful claim is narrower: the chemistry of a self can be pushed into corners that make Earth look gentle, which means a biosignature hunt that only looks for a meadow is a hunt for a postcard.

Tardigrade **cryptobiosis** is worth one extra beat because it is not magic. Water leaves. Metabolism drops toward a rumour. Protective proteins (including the tardigrade-specific disordered proteins that have had a good decade in the journals) help the remaining structure not collapse into a lethal mess. Rehydrate, and the film starts. They can still be eaten, frozen badly, or UV-roasted in orbit. "Water bear survives space" is true in a footnote-heavy way. "Water bear is an astronaut" is a T-shirt.

Extremophiles are Earth's unofficial astronaut program because they test which bills are optional. Brains: optional. Oxygen: optional. Liquid water at 20 °C and a nice pH: apparently optional, with footnotes. A membrane, a metabolism, a way to not forget how to be yourself: less optional.

## How Many Times Did A Mind Happen? 🧠

This is the mystery the expedition map asked for, and it will not sit still.

**Complex cognition** is a weasel phrase, so let us pin it. Not "has neurons." Jellyfish have nerve nets. Not "can learn." Aplysia can learn. The interesting cluster is something like: flexible problem-solving, working memory, tool use or its cousins, social or spatial sophistication that is not a fixed dance. Even that cluster is a committee.

On that loose definition, the independent origins pile up.

- **Cephalopods**, especially octopuses, as above. Last common ancestor with us: a flatworm-grade creature more than 500 million years ago. No shared childhood. No shared skull.
- **Insects**, or at least some of them. Honeybees vote for a home with waggle dances, quorum sensing, and stop signals — the cabinet already has [how bees vote](../🌙-bedtime-reading/how-bees-vote-for-a-home.md). That is collective intelligence more than a big individual brain, which is the point. No scout sees the whole market. The swarm compares options anyway, and uses inhibition so it does not dither between two hollows until winter. A swarm is a mind with extra wings. Mushroom bodies in hymenopterans, cockroaches, and others look like they thickened independently. A bee is not a tiny primate. It is a different solution to "the world is complicated and I have to choose."
- **Birds**, especially corvids and parrots. No neocortex. A pallium arranged as a different architecture that, in 2025 work, looks increasingly like a **convergent** solution rather than a leftover from a smart common ancestor with mammals. A New Caledonian crow making a hook is not a small eagle. It is a mind that found the same neighbourhood by another street.
- **Mammals**, several times over if you are being rude about clades: primates, cetaceans, elephants. Maybe independently thick cultures. Maybe one mammalian heritage restyled. The argument is live.
- **Some teleost fish**, cichlids in particular, keep turning up in the "wait, they can do *that*?" literature.

Gerhard Roth's survey is the one I keep in the pocket: complex brains and high intelligence, in the demanding sense, evolved **several to many times independently**. Giovanni Mussini's 2023 astrobiology paper counted at least eight lineages with serious cognitive preconditions in the last 100 million years, and civilisation-building once. That last number is not a law. It is a sample size of one planet. Intelligence, on this reading, is a child of time — clustered, recent, and not a Cambrian birthday present.

What we do not know is the number, because we do not know the unit. Is a bee colony one origin or a thousand small ones? Is "corvid and parrot" one avian event or two? Does octopus count once, or do squid and cuttlefish get their own flags? Until we can say what "a cognition" is without smuggling in a primate, the tally is a mood.

Sociality is a fork in this road. Bees, ants, some wasps, some termites, naked mole-rats, humans: intelligence as a **colony** property. Octopuses: intelligence as a **solitary** property, paid for in a short life, with no one to teach the next generation except a genome and a den. Corvids sit in the middle, with culture-ish tricks and long childhoods. If you only count skulls, you miss the swarm. If you only count swarms, you miss the octopus. The mystery is not just "how many times" but "how many kinds."

The useful conclusion does not need a precise count. Minds are not a vertebrate luxury. They are a trick the process keeps finding when the world is complicated and the body can pay for the wiring. That is good news for later alien chapters and bad news for anyone who thought a forehead was required.

## Same Song 🎵

Energy, information, structure. A gill and a lung and a trachea are three ways to tap an oxygen gradient. A backbone and an exoskeleton and a hydrostatic foot are three ways to be a shape that lasts. A skull-brain and an arm-brain and a dance-brain are three ways to not be a jellyfish, and jellyfish are doing fine.

If chapter 11 was "here is the human arrangement," this one is "the arrangement was never the point." Walk outside. The bird overhead is running a better lung than you. The ant on the path is breathing through straws and voting with its feet. The octopus, if you are lucky at low tide, is a mollusc that closed its pipes and put a mind in its hands.

Next door, the other kingdom that refuses to be furniture.

On a walk, the method is rude and short. If it flies and has a beak, assume a better lung than yours. If it has six legs, assume air by straw and blood that does not carry the air. If it is wet, soft, and looking at you with a camera eye and no bones, assume a mollusc that out-thought the brief. If it is radial and pulsing, assume a nerve net, and do not ask it for a face. You will misidentify species. You will still have read the arrangement, which is the literacy this chapter is selling.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **How many times did complex cognition arise?** Depends on the definition, the clade-splitting, and whether collectives count. Several is the honest floor. A precise number is a wish.
- **What is an octopus mind like from the inside?** Distributed control is a fact. Consciousness-as-experience is still the [hard problem](../📰-articles/consciousness-and-the-mind.md), now with extra arms.
- **Did unidirectional lung flow evolve for flight, hypoxia, or something duller?** Present in non-flyers. The textbook story is under renovation.
- **Where is the line between homology and deep homology?** Shared genes like *Pax6* make "independent" a spectrum. The camera eye still looks like a cover version. The sheet music might be older.
- **How far can a body plan be pushed and still be the same song?** Extremophiles stretch metabolism. They do not yet tell us whether a different geometry of mind is waiting in a solvent we have not tried.
- **Is cephalopod circulation truly closed?** Endothelium and leakiness are a live comparative-physiology argument. Closed-enough for the chapter. Not a copy of a vertebrate.
- **Do bees "vote"?** The dance-and-quorum story is solid. The word *vote* is ours. Do not sue the bees.
