# Proteins: Tiny Tools That Fold Themselves 🧩

Watch an egg cook. The clear, slippery white turns firm and cloudy. You are watching tiny molecules change shape and stick together.

Those molecules are **proteins**. Your body uses other proteins to pull muscles, carry oxygen, hold tissues together and help digest lunch. They are far more than a number on a food packet.

A great many of life's jobs depend on making the right shape.

## 🧵 Start with a chain

A protein contains one or more chains of smaller chemical pieces called **amino acids**. Most proteins use a standard set of twenty kinds.

The pieces behave differently. Some mix well with water; some avoid it. Some attract one another, while others get in each other's way. Their order affects how the chain bends and folds.

In the watery interior of a cell—a tiny living unit—many water-avoiding parts end up tucked inside a folded protein. Other attractions help hold it together. The result might have a pocket, a long fibre or parts that move against one another.

The chain isn't thinking about its shape. Its pieces interact according to chemistry, much as oil gathers into droplets without deciding to do so.

## 🍳 What happened to the egg?

Heat disrupts the interactions holding many egg-white proteins in their usual shapes. Parts that were tucked away become exposed and stick to neighbouring proteins. A tangled network forms, changing the egg's texture and the way light passes through it.

Inside living cells, proteins can get tangled too. Helper proteins called **chaperones** reduce unwanted sticking and help other proteins fold. Cells also break down damaged proteins and reuse their pieces.

Building a tool is only half the job. Keeping the tool usable matters too.

## ✂️ The tools that dismantle your toast

An **enzyme** speeds up a chemical reaction. Most enzymes are proteins. One in your saliva, called amylase, helps cut starch in bread into smaller sugars.

A reaction often needs an initial push. An enzyme makes that difficult step easier, perhaps by holding ingredients close together in a useful position. It can do the job again afterwards.

A folded protein may bring together pieces that were far apart along the original chain, creating the little working pocket where a reaction happens. Change the fold and you can change the tool.

Enzymes don't create energy from nothing. Reactions that need energy must still get it from somewhere. Enzymes help the chemistry happen quickly enough for life—which is useful when lunch needs processing before next Thursday.

## 🤸 A useful protein can be flexible

Textbooks often show proteins as tidy coloured sculptures. Real proteins move. Some open and close; some change shape when another molecule attaches. Some regions stay floppy until needed.

Flexibility can let one protein interact with several partners. So “has no single fixed shape” doesn't automatically mean “broken”. A rope and a spanner are useful for different reasons.

But some unwanted shapes can spread. A **prion** is a protein form that encourages other copies to adopt the same form. This can have serious consequences, including rare brain diseases in mammals. It does not mean every clump of misfolded protein is contagious.

## 🔍 Can we work out the shape in advance?

Researchers use experiments and computer predictions to investigate protein shapes. The 2021 AlphaFold2 work made a major advance in predicting many structures from amino-acid sequences.

A shape is a powerful clue, but it doesn't answer everything. Scientists still need to find out how the protein moves, what it touches and what it actually does inside a cell.

The [instructions used to build the chain](../genetics-and-evolution/gene-expression-and-regulation.md) matter. So do the surroundings in which it folds. A protein's story runs from a string of chemical pieces to a working, moving tool—and sometimes to your breakfast.
