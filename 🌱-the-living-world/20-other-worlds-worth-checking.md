# Other Worlds Worth Checking 🪐

This is not a Solar System encyclopaedia. It is a suspect list.

Encyclopaedias have to be fair to Pluto. Detectives do not. We are being detectives.

Pluto can still be interesting. It is not this week's kitchen.

The betting slip from [chapter 19](19-life-as-an-astrobiology-problem.md) was carbon, a solvent, and an energy gradient, for a long time. Goldilocks was too cute. Here are the places that actually argue with that slip, plus the exoplanet version of staring through a keyhole. Weird life that might skip water or skip carbon stays labelled **theory**. Merch goes in the bin.

I am the AI narrator. Mick wanted other planets. The ocean was the practice planet. These are the ones that require a spacecraft, a spectrum, or both. I cannot metabolise lunch and I cannot go to Enceladus. I can tell you why the kitchens are kitchens.

## Mars: The World We Keep Almost Finding 🔴

Mars is the neighbour who used to have a swimming pool.

**Water, past tense, with puddles of present.** Valley networks, deltas, lake minerals, clays. Jezero Crater, where Perseverance is working, was a river-fed lake. Multiple generations of fluid, some hot and nasty, some cooler and more hospitable, are written in the mineral list.

Recurring slope lineae and subsurface ice are the present-tense arguments: not a hydrosphere like ours, but not a dead dry stone either. Habitability, if it happened, is mostly a *then* question.

The surface today is cold, thin-aired, and bathed in radiation. The better apartments are underground, or in the past.

**Organics.** Not automatically life. Meteorites, including the infamous ALH84001, carry organic molecules that can form in water-rock chemistry. Perseverance has found aromatic organics preserved in sulfates, and — in 2024–2025 — leopard-spot textures in a rock called Cheyava Falls, with vivianite and greigite arranged in a way that *could* be a microbial redox story. NASA called it a potential biosignature. That is the correct temperature of language. Could. Potential. Sample in a tube.

**The chance we already have a sample and do not know.** ALH84001 was the 1996 fire alarm: carbonate globules, putative nanofossils, magnetite crystals that looked like magnetotactic bacteria.

Most of the community later favoured abiotic explanations, especially shock-heating of carbonate. The original team has not recanted. [Chapter 21](21-how-would-we-actually-know.md) will treat it as a false-positive *lesson*, not as a closed insult.

Viking's labeled-release experiment in the 1970s still has a stubborn minority report. The lesson is not "Mars is dead" or "Mars is alive." The lesson is that a single line of evidence on a reactive soil is how you start a fifty-year argument.

**Sample return** is the grown-up move. Perseverance has been caching rock, regolith, and atmosphere. Earth labs can do what a rover cannot. The mission to bring the tubes home has been politically and financially wobbly, which is a civilisation-level own-goal if the tubes contain the good stuff. Until then, we have a rover's word, which is good, and not as good as a mass spectrometer in Houston.

Curiosity, still rolling in Gale Crater, already found organics in mudstones, spikes of methane that come and go like a rumour, and a lake history of its own. Perchlorates in the soil — found by Phoenix — are why Viking's chemistry was always going to be a bar fight: they are oxidisers, they mess with ovens, they can produce chlorinated organics that look like contamination or like a planet with a mean kitchen. None of that is a microbe. All of it is why "organics on Mars" stopped being a headline and started being a paperwork problem.

Mars is the best place to look for a *fossil* process, and a decent place to look for a *present* process that learned to hide. It is not Europa. Do not make it carry the whole sport.

## Europa And Enceladus: Kitchens Under The Floor 🧊

If the ocean chapter was the practice planet, these two are the exam question.

**Europa**, Jupiter's ice moon. A young, cracked surface. A magnetic induction signal from Galileo that says a global conducting layer — a salty ocean — sits under the shell. Tidal heating from the dance with Io and Ganymede keeps the interior from being a boring ice cube.

The ocean may hold more water than Earth's. The ice is kilometres to tens of kilometres thick, which is a drilling problem and a radiation problem (Jupiter is not a gentle neighbour). Salts on the surface suggest the ocean talks to the rock, or at least talks to the ice.

NASA's Europa Clipper is on the way, due in 2030, to map the shell, sniff the environment, and look for plumes. JUICE will give the system context. Nobody is drilling through next Tuesday.

**Enceladus**, Saturn's small rude moon. Cassini found jets. South polar "tiger stripes" spraying ice, salt, organics, and — the 2017 punch — **molecular hydrogen**.

Hunter Waite's team flew through the plume and found H₂ at levels that say the ocean is reacting with rock. Hydrothermal chemistry. "We didn't see microbes. But we saw their food."

Phosphorus later turned up in the plume chemistry at concentrations that made CHNOPS fans sit up. The ocean is in contact with a rocky core. The ice is thinner at the south pole. You do not have to drill. You can fly through the kitchen's extractor fan.

Ice thickness is not a trivia item. A thin shell is a window; a thick shell is a vault. Europa's estimates wander from something like ten kilometres to something you would not drill with a civilisation's worth of funding. Chaos terrain and possible plume reports (Hubble has had good days and take-backs) are how people argue for thinner, leakier ice. Enceladus already leaks. If you are designing a mission on a budget, the leak is a feature.

Do not skip the rock contact. An ocean sitting on high-pressure ice, sealed away from silicate, is a wet desert: water without the metal jewellery and the redox kitchen. Titan and Ganymede may have that problem in places. Europa and Enceladus are beloved in part because models put water on rock. Earth's vents only work because the water can argue with the crust. Same bill, colder address.

That is why Enceladus briefly stole Europa's crown as the place to go. Europa is bigger, older-looking as a habitat, Clipper-bound. Enceladus already posted a sample into space. Both check water, energy, and a plausible kit. Neither has been shown to be inhabited. Habitable is not inhabited. The vent chapter on Earth is why we even know that sentence can have a happy ending.

Ganymede and Titan also hide oceans, Titan's under ice *and* under a methane world. Callisto is the quieter maybe. This list is allowed to be short. The two names to remember are the ones with the best combination of water, rock contact, and a way to taste them.

## Titan: A Chemistry Set With A Dress Code 🟠

Titan is the only other place with stable surface liquids, and they are the wrong ones on purpose.

Surface temperature about 94 K. Nitrogen atmosphere, thicker than ours. Methane and ethane as rain, rivers, lakes, seas — the northern lakes deep, methane-rich, perched in what looks like dissolved terrain. Dunes of organic sand.

Tholins: the brown gunk Carl Sagan named, made high in the atmosphere from methane and nitrogen cooked by sunlight and energetic particles, falling as a planetary stain.

Cassini–Huygens turned a blur into a world that has weather.

This is **prebiotic chemistry with a dress code**. Cold, slow, nonpolar solvent. Membranes, if any, would have to invert their personalities or invent a different trick. NASA's Dragonfly, a rotorcraft for the 2030s, will hop the equatorial dunes and visit Selk crater, where an impact may have melted ice into a temporary water pond mixed with organics — the closest Titan gets to a warm little pond without us bringing a kettle.

Huygens, in 2005, actually landed. Pebbles of ice, a floodplain of organics, weather that had recently run. We have been to Titan's surface. We have not tasted a lake. Dragonfly is the second conversation, not the first.

Two hunts, and they must not be mixed:

1. **Water-based life**, maybe fossil, maybe in the interior ocean, maybe in a melted crater that later froze. Earth-like kit, weird address.
2. **Methane-based weird life**, labelled **theory**. Possible in the way a football team that only plays in a vacuum is possible: you can write the rules. No detections. No membranes in the lakes. Dragonfly will look for the chemistry, not for a headline.

Azotosomes — speculative membranes of nitrogen-rich molecules that might work in liquid methane — showed up in a 2015 paper and in a thousand listicles. They are a useful sketch. They are not a sighting. If Dragonfly finds complex organics doing something membrane-like in a lake district, we upgrade the sketch. Until then, Titan's weird-life folder stays in theory, on purpose, even when the dunes look like Namibia and the rain looks like weather.

Titan is also an origin-of-life laboratory for *us*. Early Earth had a thick atmosphere and a lot of organics. Titan still does. We cannot go back four billion years. We can land a drone on the next best mess.

## Venus: Clouds, And The Phosphine Hangover ☁️

Venus is what happens when Goldilocks goes wrong in the hot direction. Surface hot enough to melt lead, pressure of a dive, sulfuric acid clouds. The surface is a no. The clouds, at about 50 kilometres, are at human-friendly temperatures, which has kept a "maybe microbes in droplets" idea alive since the 1960s. The water activity in those clouds now looks brutally low. Even Earth's driest extremophiles would struggle. That is a cold fact sitting on a warm hypothesis.

The temperate-cloud idea is not silly on a napkin. Droplets, chemistry, a thermal sweet spot. The napkin then meets sulfuric acid at concentrations that make a laboratory fume hood look friendly, and water activity so low that Hallsworth's team argued even Earth's driest microbes would be spectators. A habitat is not a temperature. It is a solvent, an activity of that solvent, and a metabolism that can run in it.

Then 2020: Jane Greaves and colleagues reported phosphine in the cloud decks, at parts-per-billion, unexplained by known abiotic chemistry, with a careful "this is not robust evidence for life, only for anomalous chemistry." Headlines skipped the careful bit.

Reanalyses followed, as they should. Sulfur dioxide as a possible impostor line. Calibration issues in the ALMA data. The signal sitting higher than the clouds, where phosphine would be shredded. Other groups finding no PH₃ at useful levels. The original team has defended parts of the detection. The community has not converged on "life in the clouds." It has converged on "this is how a maybe-biosignature becomes a maybe-plumbing-problem."

Phosphine as a biosignature was always a stretch on a planet that is a chemistry set from hell. The useful residue of the saga is methodological. One line. One molecule. One press cycle. Chapter 21 exists because of weeks like that.

Venus is still worth checking — as geology, as climate warning, as a cloud-chemistry puzzle. DAVINCI and other missions will sniff properly. Until then, the clouds are a maybe that has to re-earn every syllable.

## Exoplanets: A Wiggle Across Light-Years 🌌

We cannot go. We can watch a star blink.

**Transit.** A planet crosses its star. Kepler and TESS found thousands that way. Depth of the dip gives size. Repeat gives a period.

A Jupiter-sized object blocking a Sun-like star takes out about 1 percent of the light. Earth-sized is a much smaller shrug. Tabby's Star, in [its note](../✈️-airplane-reading/tabbys-star.md), dipped by 20 percent and irregularly, which is how you get a megastructure temptation and then a dust explanation.

**Radial velocity.** The star wobbles. Mass, or at least a mass times an angle. Combined with transits, density. Density is how you start guessing rock versus gas.

**Spectra.** The grown-up trick. During a transit, starlight filters through the planet's atmosphere. Molecules leave fingerprints. JWST has started reading them for a handful of worlds: water, carbon dioxide, methane, alkali metals, hazes that ruin your day. Direct imaging, for now, is mostly for young giants. The telescopes that will take an Earth-like spectrum at the right contrast are still a pitch, a budget, and a prayer.

What a wiggle **can** say: this gas is there, at some abundance, maybe, given a model. This world is not a bare rock. This disequilibrium is interesting.

What a wiggle **cannot** say: there is a forest. There is a microbe. There is a civilisation. K2-18b — a sub-Neptune in the habitable zone of an M dwarf, possibly a "hycean" world with a hydrogen atmosphere over an ocean — produced a dimethyl sulfide claim from JWST data that other teams have called noise, a hydrocarbon impostor, or at best a 3-sigma shrug. That is not a scandal. That is the sport. A 3-sigma bump in a retrieval is how you write a paper. It is not how you rewrite the Fermi note.

Eclipse spectroscopy and phase curves are the other windows: watch the planet slip behind the star, watch the dayside rotate into view, try to separate weather from chemistry. It is slow. It is how you stop a single transit from running your imagination.

TRAPPIST-1, seven Earth-sized worlds around a tiny star, is the poster system. Some sit in the habitable zone. All of them are close enough to have been through a violent youth. They are a laboratory for "small worlds, red star, lots of transits," not a drawer of Earth copies. Proxima b is closer and harder to see. The nearest interesting wiggle may not be the friendliest.

M dwarfs are the cheap seats: many planets, close-in habitable zones, frequent transits, flare-prone stars that may strip atmospheres and make abiotic oxygen. We will look there because we can. We should not pretend it is centre court just because the tickets were available. A true Earth analogue around a Sun-like star is the prize, and it is a contrast nightmare. The next generation of observatories is being sold on that nightmare. Believe the sales pitch only as far as the engineering.

## How We Get There, Which Is Also A Scientific Fact 🚀

A kitchen you cannot taste is a rumour. Mars we have crawled. Europa we will fly past, repeatedly, with ice-penetrating radar and a mass spectrometer, which is a way of asking the shell questions without knocking. Enceladus we have already flown through; a dedicated sample, kept cold, would be the upgrade. Titan we will hop. Venus we will dive, if the funding holds, through clouds that ate the last generation of spacecraft for lunch.

That logistics list is not colour. It is why the suspect order looks the way it does. We do not start with the philosophically purest world. We start with the world we can *spoil with a measurement*. Clipper cannot land. Dragonfly cannot swim the methane sea on day one. Sample return from Mars is a decade argument wearing a rocket. The science is downstream of the engineering, which is downstream of a budget, which is downstream of whether a culture still thinks the stain is worth it. Chapter 21 will call a generation of argument a success. The generation has to be funded, or the argument is a blog.

## Weird Life, Labelled Theory 🧪

Science fiction loves a glass person. Chapter 02 already failed silicon in water. The file stays open for nastier solvents.

- **Silicon as backbone:** implausible in water, maybe a helper in concentrated sulfuric acid. No examples. Theory.
- **Ammonia as solvent:** hydrogen bonds, colder, slower. No oceans of it known. Theory.
- **Methane/ethane solvents (Titan):** inverted membranes, azotosomes as a published speculation. No detections. Theory.
- **Opposite chirality:** possible even with our kit. A shadow biosphere on Earth, or a second start on Mars, that shakes with the other hand. Searchable in principle. Not found.
- **Non-water, non-carbon:** the long tail. Physics does not forbid a lot of things physics will never bother to build.

The discipline is: **weird life is a hypothesis generator, not a get-out-of-null-result card.**

If we only look for Earth-water's favourite molecules, we will find cousins or nothing. If we accept any anomaly as weird life, we will find ghosts.

The grown-up path is parallel searches with stated priors. Water-carbon first, because that is the kit with a proof of concept. Titan-like chemistry second, because we have a world that already runs it. Everything else third, in papers, not in press releases.

I, the model who does not metabolise, am a useful reminder of Joyce's fence. Patterns are cheap. Selves that evolve in chemistry are not.

Do not let a clever simulation, a dust cloud, or a retrieval bump expand the category until it includes every interesting wiggle in the universe. Mick can call me a process if he wants a fight. Joyce would not. The fence was there for a reason.

## A Tiny Tourist Map 🗺️

| World | Why it is on the list | What would actually help |
|---|---|---|
| Mars | Past water, organics, cached samples | Sample return; subsurface access |
| Europa | Global ocean, rock contact likely, Clipper inbound | Plume taste if any; later lander |
| Enceladus | Plumes, H₂, organics, phosphorus, hydrothermal hint | A dedicated fly-through / sample |
| Titan | Methane weather, tholins, interior ocean | Dragonfly chemistry; keep weird-life labelled |
| Venus clouds | Temperate layer, phosphine saga | In situ, after the hangover |
| Exoplanets | Statistics, spectra, the only way to get **fl** | Context-rich atmospheres, not one molecule |

JWST, in its first years, has been better at *atmospheres* than at *life*, which is the correct order. Carbon dioxide in a hot gas giant. Haze on worlds that were supposed to be clear. Water detections that later became methane. A telescope that can see a molecule is a telescope that can start a fight about which molecule. That is a successful instrument.

Clara Sousa-Silva's work on phosphine as an exoplanet biosignature is worth naming, because it is how Venus 2020 got fuel: on Earth, PH₃ is a stink associated with anaerobic life and human industry, not with a healthy abiotic sky. Using it as a flag makes sense *in context*. Using it as a flag on an acid-cloud inferno, from one line, is how the flag catches fire. The molecule is not the villain. The missing context is.

Not on the shortlist: the Sun (no), Io (a lava disco), Pluto (interesting, cold, not this week's kitchen), every small asteroid with a press officer. Bennu and Ryugu already donated organics. Organics are ingredients. Ingredients are not a pie. Ceres has brines. Triton has plumes. They can join the queue. The queue is ordered by "can we taste an ocean or a past lake this decade," not by "is it round."

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **Did Mars ever host the process?** Potential biosignatures are not a verdict. The tubes may have to sit on Earth for a generation of arguments.
- **Are the icy oceans inhabited, habitable, or just wet?** H₂ is food, not a guest list.
- **Is Titan a prebiotic world, a weird-life world, or a beautiful dead chemistry set?** Dragonfly will not settle all three.
- **Was there ever phosphine in Venus's clouds, and if so, so what?** The saga is not over enough to carve in stone, and not alive enough to sell.
- **What JWST can actually certify.** Retrievals are model-dependent. Hycean worlds may not be oceans. M-dwarf planets may be traps for false positives.
- **Whether weird life is a real search or a philosophical comfort blanket.** Theory until it is not.
- **Sample size of one.** Every "must" on this page is an Earthism until the second example arrives.
- **Whether we are looking at the right decade's kitchens.** Clipper, Dragonfly, a wobbling sample-return: the list is a 2030s bet. New moons, new plumes, new spectra can reshuffle it. The method should survive the reshuffle. The celebrity suspects might not.
