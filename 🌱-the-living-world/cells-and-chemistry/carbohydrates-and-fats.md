# Why You Can Eat Toast but Not the Table 🍞

Bread and wood share a surprising ingredient. The starch in bread and much of the structure in wood are built from the same little sugar, **glucose**.

Yet toast can fuel your morning, while your body cannot get the same nourishment from a wooden table. Even with jam.

The difference is how the pieces join together.

## 🔗 Same pieces, different connections

**Carbohydrates** include sugars and chains made by joining sugar units. Plants store many of these chains as **starch**. They also build **cellulose**, whose strong bundles help support their cells—the tiny living units plants are made from.

Your digestive system contains **enzymes**, molecules that speed up particular chemical reactions. Some cut starch into smaller pieces you can absorb. But the connections in cellulose have a different arrangement, and your body doesn't make the enzymes needed to cut them.

Think of scissors and a tin opener. Both separate things, but having one doesn't mean you can tackle the other's job.

Cows get help from microbes, microscopic organisms that break down plant material. Some of the fuel released by those partners feeds the cow. Eating grass successfully is a team achievement.

Sugar-based materials also help make insect shells and fungal walls. The carbohydrate family has ventured well beyond the biscuit aisle.

## 🌰 How to store sugar without becoming syrup

Plants store starch in places such as seeds and potatoes. Animals store glucose in branching chains called **glycogen**, especially in the liver and muscles.

The branches provide lots of ends where enzymes can remove sugar units quickly. Linking the units also helps with water balance. Lots of separate dissolved sugar molecules draw water into a cell; joining them into fewer, bigger chains reduces that pull. The cell can keep fuel without drawing in as much extra water.

Liver stores help supply glucose to the blood between meals. Muscle stores mainly fuel the muscle that holds them. A shared pantry and a packed lunch have different jobs.

## 🛢️ What makes fat different?

Fats belong to a broader group called **lipids**, substances that generally mix poorly with water.

Stored fat packs a lot of usable energy into a small mass. Its chemical arrangement releases more energy per gram than carbohydrate when the body breaks it down using oxygen. Fat stores also carry less water than glycogen stores do.

That makes fat useful for longer-term energy storage. But oily molecules do construction work too.

Some have a water-friendly head and oily tails. In water, they gather into double layers with the tails tucked inside. These layers form the basis of [cell membranes](cells-and-organelles.md), the thin boundaries around cells.

Proteins fitted into the membrane control traffic. Changing the oily tails changes how tightly the membrane packs. The wrapper around a cell is carefully maintained equipment.

## 🏷️ Sugars can be name tags

Short sugar chains also decorate cell surfaces. Other molecules can recognise their shapes, helping cells interact.

The familiar A and B blood groups involve different sugars displayed on red blood cells. A tiny difference on the surface matters greatly to how the immune system recognises those cells.

So sugar can be fuel, a building material or part of a name tag. Its job depends on where it is and how it is connected.

## 🍳 Cooking changes what we can reach

Heating starch with water can open up its packed structure, making it easier for digestive enzymes to reach. Grinding food can expose material that was hidden behind plant cell walls. Preparation changes access as well as taste.

This helps explain why [food energy](../bodies-and-energy/calories-and-food-energy.md) is more interesting than simply burning a sample and measuring the heat.

None of these molecules comes with a moral label saying “good” or “bad”. Bodies need fuels and building materials in workable combinations. The fascinating question here is what each structure lets life do.
