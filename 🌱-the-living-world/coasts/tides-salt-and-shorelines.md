# The Beach That Disappears Twice a Day 🌊🌙

The patch of shore you crossed at lunchtime may be underwater later. For you, the route has changed. For a limpet attached to a rock, the whole living situation has changed.

Water brings food and relief from drying. It also brings waves and hungry visitors. Coastal life has to cope with the whole cycle.

## 🌙 Why does the water rise and fall?

**Tides** are regular changes in sea level driven mainly by differences in the Moon's and Sun's gravitational pull across Earth.

The oceans don't respond as one simple, smooth sheet. Continents, bays and different water depths alter how water moves. Many places have two high and two low tides a day; elsewhere the pattern differs, or the two highs are very unequal.

Around new and full moons, the Sun's and Moon's effects reinforce each other more strongly, generally producing a larger tidal range. These are **spring tides**. The name has nothing to do with the season.

Near the quarter moons, the range is generally smaller: **neap tides**.

## 🌬️ Waves aren't tides

Wind transfers energy to water, making **waves**. Their size depends on wind strength, how long it blows and the distance it blows across water.

**Swell** is wave energy that has travelled away from the weather that created it. That is why substantial waves can arrive beneath a calm local sky.

A **current** is flowing water. Tides can drive currents, but wind and differences in water density can drive them too.

Water level, wave size and current speed describe different things. A surface that looks calm doesn't guarantee still water underneath.

## 🚪 A big bay with a narrow doorway

Port Phillip exchanges water with Bass Strait through a relatively narrow entrance. A large volume passing through that doorway can create strong currents, even though the vertical tidal change farther inside is modest.

The water inside and outside doesn't rise and fall in perfect step. At the Heads, the time when the current weakens before reversing—**slack water**—doesn't simply match high or low tide.

The [Bureau of Meteorology's explanation](https://www.bom.gov.au/australia/tides/about/p4a-port-phillip.shtml) describes this local behaviour. It is a good example of why one nearby tide prediction can't stand in for every place.

Wind and air pressure can also alter actual water levels from the astronomical prediction.

## 🧂 Why saltiness matters to an animal

**Salinity** means the amount of dissolved salt. Open seawater commonly contains about 35 grams of salt per kilogram of seawater, but coastal water varies.

Rain and rivers add freshwater. Evaporation removes water while leaving salt behind. A rockpool can therefore become fresher after rain or saltier on a hot day.

Living bodies contain **cells**, tiny units with thin boundaries. Water moves across those boundaries in response to differences in dissolved substances. A sudden change outside can disturb the cell's water balance.

Organisms have ways of managing this, but their tolerances differ. Salt is doing much more than seasoning the sea.

## 🥣 Fresh and salt water don't always stir together

Freshwater is usually less dense than seawater under similar conditions, so it can sit above saltier water. Wind and tides help mix the layers.

If the deeper layer receives little fresh oxygen while organisms keep using it, conditions below can become difficult even when the surface looks ordinary.

An **estuary**, where freshwater and seawater interact, changes with those supplies and movements. [Coastal plant habitats](kelp-seagrass-mangroves-and-estuaries.md) show how living things respond.

## 🐚 Read the shore's patterns carefully

Rock offers attachment. Sand shifts and favours many burrowing animals. Mud can hold a busy community beneath its apparently quiet surface.

Bands of barnacles, snails and seaweeds reflect time underwater, but also splash, shade, competition and grazing. They aren't precise rulers marking tide height.

Even washed-up seaweed may have travelled. The shore is a changing meeting place, carrying evidence from the land, the nearby water and sometimes much farther away.
