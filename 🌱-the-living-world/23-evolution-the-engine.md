# Evolution: The Engine ⚙️

Chapter 01 already used the word and then walked off.

Gerald Joyce's working definition of life — the one everyone quotes and nobody is supposed to treat as scripture — said **Darwinian**. Variation, heredity, selection. A chemistry that can remember *and* change. We spent a whole climb on the chemistry, the memory, the bodies, the forests, the other 70 percent of the planet, and we never started the motor. That was rude of us. This is the motor.

I am the AI narrator. Mick asked for the engine, not a marble statue of Darwin, and not a ladder with humans on the penthouse. Fair. Evolution is not a moral. It is not progress in a suit. It is what happens when you have a remix that can be copied, a world that cares about the copies, and time. [Chapter 22](22-how-traits-run-in-families.md) was the remix. This one is why remixes, given long enough, become beetles, kelp, and us.

## Four Moving Parts, Not A Slogan 🎸

The engine has four parts. Miss one and you do not get a lineage that can change. You get a crystal, a fire, or a very convincing chatbot.

1. **Variation.** Individuals in a population are not identical. Typos in the library, shuffled chromosomes, the odd stolen gene. [Chapter 04](04-how-cells-read-and-rewrite-the-library.md) already said mutation is damage *and* fuel. Without differences, there is nothing to pick.
2. **Heredity.** Some of those differences copy. Alleles ride gametes. Chapter 22 was this part with family photographs. A trait that dies with the body is a rumour, not an evolutionary fact.
3. **Selection.** The world is not a fair referee. Some variants leave more copies — more offspring, more spores, more viral particles — in *this* environment. That is not a prize. It is an accounting identity with teeth.
4. **Time.** Generations. Lots of them. A filter that runs once is weather. A filter that runs for a billion years is a biosphere.

That is the whole trick. It does not require a plan. It does not require a brain. It does not require anyone to be trying. It requires a population of things that copy with differences, and a world in which those differences are not all equal.

**"Survival of the fittest" is not the engine.** It is a slogan Herbert Spencer coined after reading Darwin, and Darwin later borrowed it, nervously, because Wallace thought "natural selection" sounded like a chooser with hands. The slogan has a tautology problem if you let it: who survives? The fittest. Who are the fittest? The ones who survive. The engine is not that loop. Fitness, used carefully, is a rate — expected contribution to the next generation's gene pool — in a specified environment. A variant can be fit in a drought and a disaster in a flood. The fittest peacock in a cage is still in a cage. Selection is a relationship between a variant and a world, not a medal.

If you want a sports picture: variation is the squad having different shots. Heredity is the academy producing more of the same kind of player. Selection is the league, the pitch, the weather, and the other team. Time is seasons. Nobody is "progressing toward football." The table just keeps getting rewritten by who actually played.

A music picture, because the sports one will get tired: mutation writes wrong notes. Heredity copies the chart. Selection is the crowd, the room, and whether the song still works on this instrument. Time is the discography. You do not get Beethoven from one bad rehearsal. You get him from a lot of copying, a lot of wrong notes, and a world that kept some of them.

Three things selection is not, because the slogan keeps lying:

- **Not random.** Mutation is the random-ish part (random with respect to need, not random as in "any nucleotide equally likely in any genome"). Selection is the opposite of a lottery. It is a bias.
- **Not directed toward us.** There is no finish line. A parasite getting simpler is as evolutionary as a peacock getting louder.
- **Not "for the good of the species,"** as a default. Selection usually sees individuals, or genes, or sometimes other units. A trait can spread because it helps *itself* copy, even if it is rude to the group. Group selection is a real research programme and a real fight. It is not the Year 10 default.

Sexual selection is the other Darwin, the one people skip so they can keep saying "survival." Peacocks, songs, antlers, a lot of the biosphere's jewellery: variants that win mates, not winters. It is still selection. The environment just includes other choosers. Coevolution — hosts and parasites, flowers and bees — is the same engine with two tables updating each other. Van Valen's **Red Queen**: you have to keep running just to stay in the same place. The pitch is other lineages.

## Darwin And Wallace, Without The Stained Glass 📜

Charles Darwin spent twenty years sitting on the idea after the *Beagle*, breeding pigeons, reading Malthus, and being ill on a sofa in Kent. Alfred Russel Wallace, younger, poorer, and actually in the field, caught fever in the Malay Archipelago in 1858 and wrote the same engine down in an essay from Ternate. He posted it to Darwin. Darwin wrote to Lyell, in so many words: I am about to be forestalled.

Lyell and Joseph Hooker arranged a joint reading at the Linnean Society on 1 July 1858: extracts from Darwin's unpublished work, plus Wallace's Ternate essay, *On the Tendency of Varieties to depart indefinitely from the Original Type*. The room did not explode. The 1859 book did: *On the Origin of Species*. Wallace, to his credit and to later historians' fascination, did not spend the rest of his life suing. He called himself a Darwinian. Darwin called the engine natural selection. Both men deserve the joint billing. Neither man invented "change over time" — that rumour had been around. They named a *mechanism* that could make a tree.

What they did not have: genes. Mendel was counting peas and the letters still had not met. Darwin's own heredity (pangenesis, little gemmules) was a miss. The engine still ran as an argument because the four parts do not require you to know DNA. They require variation that is heritable. The molecule, when it showed up, was almost rude in how well it fitted.

They also did not have a monopoly on "species change." Lamarck had a mechanism (use and disuse, acquired characters handed down) that was wrong as a general heredity and is still dragged out whenever someone wants a ghost. The fossil people already knew the furniture had moved. Darwin and Wallace's contribution was not "change happened." It was "this is a filter that can *make* a tree from ordinary variation," without a designer and without a ladder. That was the bit that got people out of their chairs.

What they also did not have: a clean account of chance at the molecular level, of drift, of the fact that most DNA-level change might not be "for" anything. That comes later in this chapter. Do not make them saints of a complete theory. Make them the people who got the filter right, in public, under their own names, in a culture that was about to have a very long argument.

The argument is not over. It just moved from "did it happen" to "how much of the genome is the filter versus the noise, and what counts as a species, and why complexity sometimes piles up." Those are better arguments. They are still arguments.

## Common Descent: A Tree, Nested 🌳

The other half of Darwin, the half the slogan forgets, is **common descent**. Life, as we can poke it, is a family. Not a chain. A tree.

The evidence is the nested pattern. You can group organisms by shared traits, and the groups nest: vertebrates inside animals inside eukaryotes inside cellular life. A trait shared by a tight group (feathers, more or less) sits inside a trait shared by a bigger group (amniote eggs, backbones, nuclei).

That is what a branching process *looks like* after the fact. It is not what a separate-creation catalogue looks like. A catalogue can have any combination. A family tree has nested leftovers. Fossils sit on the same tree, which is why a *Tiktaalik* is interesting as a cousin with a job, not as a missing rung.

Molecular sequences made the argument louder and ruder. The same nested pattern shows up in DNA, in proteins, in the ribosomal RNA Carl Woese read until a third domain fell out of the furniture. Independent inventions would not keep aligning the same silent spellings in the same order. Homology at the sequence level is the family resemblance chapter 22 was doing for faces, now doing for the biosphere.

Here is the sentence that still makes people put down a mushroom: **fungi are closer to animals than they are to plants.**

Not as a vibe. As a clade. Animals and fungi sit together in **Opisthokonta** — the "rear-pole" people, named for a single posterior flagellum on the motile cells of the group. Thomas Cavalier-Smith floated the name in 1987; Baldauf, Palmer, Wainright and the rRNA and protein trees of the 1990s nailed it. Plants are on a different eukaryotic branch, the archaeplastid story, photosynthesis as a tenant from [chapter 09](09-the-cell-as-a-city.md). Fungi do not photosynthesise. They eat. They make chitin. Their swimming ancestors, like ours, pushed from the back. A mushroom is not a strange plant. It is a cousin who sat down and learned to digest the world from the outside. The [fungal network](../✈️-airplane-reading/the-fungal-network-beneath-our-feet.md) note in the cabinet just got a family-tree caption.

That is why "looks like a plant" is not kinship. Nested descent is kinship. A whale looks like a fish and is a mammal. A fungus looks like a plant and is sitting on our side of the eukaryotic hall. Chapter 12 already said a whale is a mammal in a different costume. This is the same lesson at kingdom scale.

LUCA, from [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md), is the last node we can all still see. Not the first living thing. The last grandparent before the lineages we have went their ways. Common descent is the claim that the tree has a trunk we can point at, even if the first act is missing.

## Adaptation Versus Just-So Stories 🦒

An **adaptation** is a trait shaped by selection for a current function. That sentence is doing a lot of work, and people skip the work.

Current utility is not origin. A trait can be useful now and have shown up for another reason, or for no reason the filter cared about, and then been hired. Darwin knew this.

Gould and Lewontin, in 1979, had to shout it: the **spandrels** of San Marco are the leftover triangles where a dome meets the arches. You can paint saints on them. That does not mean the triangles were built to hold saints. They are a geometric byproduct. Biology is full of spandrels, of developmental leftovers, of traits that come as a package because the embryo is not a buffet. Feathers for display, or insulation, before they were for flight: the usual cautionary tale. The tale has to earn the next fossil, not just the next sentence.

The failure mode is the **just-so story**, Kipling's phrase, Gould's weapon: a plausible tale about why a trait exists, accepted because it sounds neat. "Giraffes have long necks to reach high leaves." Maybe. Sexual selection, neck-as-weapon, a developmental constraint that elongated several things at once: also in the literature. The test is not plausibility. The test is evidence — phylogeny, experiments, genetics, the comparative method — and a willingness to say "we do not know."

Adaptation is real. Eyes, roughly, are adaptations for detecting light, built more than once. Chapter 12 already did camera eyes, wings, echolocation, and the prestin protein that converged in bats and whales. We are not redoing bird lungs. The point here is methodological: **selection can shape. It is not obliged to have shaped everything you notice.** A good evolutionary explanation names the alternatives: selection, drift, constraint, byproduct, hitchhiking, chance. Then it tries to kill some of them.

If a paragraph about a trait could be swapped for Kipling without anyone noticing, it has failed. If it could be swapped for a first-year lecture that never mentions constraint, it has also failed. The engine is a filter. The body is not a list of independently optimised gadgets. It is a compromise that has to be born.

## Drift, Bottlenecks, Founders 🎲

Selection is not the only thing that changes allele frequencies.

**Genetic drift** is sampling error with a long career. Each generation is a draw from the last. In a small population the draw is noisy. Alleles wander, get lucky, go extinct, all without being "for" anything. In an infinite population, drift would be a rumour. No population is infinite.

The **effective population size** (N<sub>e</sub>) — the size of an idealised population that would wander as much as this real one — is usually much smaller than the headcount, because breeding is not a fair roster. A few individuals can contribute most of the next generation. Drift, then, is stronger than the census made you think. A breeding system that lets a handful of males win the season is a bottleneck you run every year.

**Bottlenecks** squeeze the bottle. A population crashes; the survivors are a non-random, too-small sample; diversity dies. Northern elephant seals were hunted to something on the order of **20 to 100** animals by the late nineteenth century. A 2024 genomic reconstruction put the effective size during the squeeze around **six**. They recovered to hundreds of thousands. The diversity did not. Low heterozygosity is the scar. The species is a conservation success story with a genetically quiet soundtrack.

**Founder effects** are a bottleneck you take with you. A few individuals colonise an island, a valley, a new host. Their allele frequencies *are* the new population, however eccentric. Pingelap in Micronesia is the classroom case: a typhoon, a handful of survivors, a colour-vision allele that rode along and became common because the founders were few, not because the atoll wanted it. Islands, immigrant communities, isolated valleys: evolution by moving van.

Drift is not "the opposite of evolution." It is evolution. Evolution is change in the genetic composition of a population. Selection is one cause. Drift is another. Bottlenecks and founders are drift with a dramatic lighting cue. A lot of what a population *is*, genetically, is leftover luck.

## Neutral Theory, In One Clear Paragraph 🧪

Motoo Kimura, in 1968, looked at the rate of amino-acid change and the amount of hidden variation electrophoresis was finding, and said the unsayable: **most of the change at the molecular level is not selection picking winners. It is neutral (or nearly neutral) mutants drifting to fixation.** King and Jukes, a year later, called it non-Darwinian evolution, which was a fight-starting title on purpose.

The claim is not "selection does not happen." Kimura was explicit: form and function can still be Darwinian. The claim is that *at the level of DNA and many protein sites*, the clock you see is mostly mutation plus drift.

Silent (synonymous) changes should accumulate faster than ones that rewrite a protein, if the protein is doing a job. They do, mostly. Pseudogenes — dead genes — should tick even faster. They do. The rate of neutral substitution equals the mutation rate, independent of population size: a rare elegant equation. In a big population, more mutants appear, but each is less likely to drift to fixation. The two cancel. That is why molecular clocks are even a thing.

Tomoko Ohta's **nearly-neutral** refinement matters: slightly deleterious alleles behave as neutral in small populations (drift drowns their faint badness) and as selected in large ones. Population size, then, changes *which* sites look free. The fight that followed — how much of the genome is constrained, how much of amino-acid change is adaptive — is not over. It is the adult version of "is it all selection." Neutral theory is the null. Selection has to beat it. A lot of sequence cannot.

If chapter 22's heritability lecture was "the percentage is not about *you*," this paragraph is "the substitution is not always *for* something." Most of the library's typos are not adaptations. They are weather in the archive.

## Speciation, Briefly, Because Chapter 16 Owns The Noun 🏷️

Selection and drift change populations. **Speciation** is when a lineage splits so the parts stop being one exchanging pool.

The textbook start is **allopatric**: a barrier, a mountain, a current, a founder on an island; the two sides drift and adapt apart until they would not, or could not, merge if they met. Darwin's finches are the postcard, and the postcard is allowed to be more complicated than the gift-shop version. **Sympatric** and the messier in-betweens exist — same place, different hosts, different songs, different chromosomes — and they are argued over because they are harder to catch in the act. Polyploidy in plants can split a lineage in a generation. Cichlids in a lake can split on colour and habitat without a mountain. Reproductive isolation is a process, not a stamp.

Ring species — a chain of populations that can breed with neighbours and not with the far end — are the offside rule of speciation, and chapter 16 already filed them under "Mayr's concept leaks." We point. We do not annex.

What is a species? [Chapter 16](16-mysteries-theories-and-the-unsolved.md) already held that fight: Mayr's biological species concept (they could breed, they don't, with outsiders), morphological, phylogenetic, ecological, and de Queiroz's peace treaty that species are lineages and the "concepts" are criteria. Microbes laugh at the mating dance. We are not rewriting that chapter. We are noting that the engine produces splits, and that our bins are tools. Tools disagree. The tree is still a useful drawing of the splits we can see.

## Not A Ladder, Not A Promotion 🪜

School charts still smell of a staircase: bacteria, then protists, then plants, then animals, then us, taking the trophy. That staircase is a Victorian hangover and a failure to read a tree.

A tree has living tips at every height. Bacteria did not stop being bacteria when eukaryotes showed up. They are the majority of life's metabolism, the majority of the tree's branch-length if you count honestly, and the reason you have nitrogen in your proteins.

"Primitive" as an insult is a category error. A bacterium is a contemporary. It has had as much time as you have. It solved different problems. If you want a majority-of-life chapter, look at microbes; this series still owes that one a proper room. Here the point is narrower: the engine did not promote anyone out of existence by inventing you.

Evolution is not **progress**. There is no theoretical reason lineages should get more complex, and plenty of lineages get simpler: parasites throwing away kits, cave fish throwing away eyes, our own mitochondria throwing away most of a bacterial genome. Maynard Smith and Szathmáry, who thought harder about complexity than most, opened their major-transitions paper by saying exactly that: no reason to expect an increase, and yet here we are with cells inside cells inside bodies inside societies. The increase, when it happens, is a series of rare reorganisations, not a law. We will get to those as the mystery.

Humans are not the point of the story. We are one tip, with language, which is a big deal for us and not a destination the Cambrian was aiming at. A seagull is not a failed human. An oak is not a stalled animal. Chapter 12's whole job was "same song, different arrangement." This chapter's job is "the song was never a staircase."

If you catch a sentence ranking lineages by how much they resemble Mick, walk it back out of the room. That sentence is not biology. It is a mirror.

## The Tree Is Also A Web 🕸️

Vertical inheritance — parent to child — draws a tree. Life keeps smudging the ink.

**Horizontal gene transfer**, from [chapter 04](04-how-cells-read-and-rewrite-the-library.md): bacteria eat DNA, catch viruses that mis-package a previous host, conjugate plasmids down a pilus. Antibiotic resistance travels faster than a textbook phylogeny. The history of *cells* can still be a tree if you follow the conservative core of the translation machinery. The history of *genes* is a web. Chapter 16 already enjoyed that sentence. Here it is load-bearing: the engine is not only "descend with modification along a lineage." It is also "sometimes a neighbour slides you a page, and the page is now yours."

**Endosymbiosis**, from [chapter 09](09-the-cell-as-a-city.md): mitochondria and chloroplasts are not branches. They are mergers. An archaeal host and a bacterium, a eukaryotic cell and a cyanobacterium, and then the secondary thefts where someone ate a cell that had already eaten a photographer. You cannot draw that as a clean fork. You draw a joining. The eukaryotic cell is a nested community. Selection still acts. The unit just got weirder.

So: common descent is still the right large-scale claim. The metaphor is a tree with vines, grafts, and the occasional fusion. Viruses are graffiti. Informational genes are stingy about moving. Metabolic genes commute.

Anyone who shows you a perfectly tidy tree of all genes is selling a filing system, not a history. The engine still runs on the vines. A stolen gene is still a variant that can be selected, or drift, or sit there like a spare cable. Horizontal is not a loophole. It is another way the four parts get paid.

## Convergence, With A Point At Chapter 12 🎧

The engine, running in different lineages, sometimes finds the same chorus.

That is **convergent evolution**, and [chapter 12](12-other-animals-same-song-different-arrangement.md) already did the concert: camera eyes in vertebrates and coleoid cephalopods, with the wiring as the giveaway; wings four times; echolocation in bats and toothed whales, down to similar tweaks in prestin; a shark, an ichthyosaur, and a dolphin agreeing about streamlining. Bird lungs got their own heading there. We are not stealing them.

The theoretical point for this chapter: convergence is evidence that **selection plus physics** can be strong enough to rhyme. It is also evidence that "it looks like us" is not kinship. Homology is shared ancestry. Analogy is shared problem. The engine produces both. A good naturalist asks which one they are holding before they write the family tree.

Constraint is the quieter twin of convergence. There are only so many ways to build a stable protein, a waterproof egg, a wing that lifts. Some rhymes are the world being narrow, not the filter being clever. Distinguishing those is part of the unsolved pile.

## The Mystery: Major Transitions, Evolvability, Filter Versus Frame 🕳️

Here is the bit that should keep you up, now that the engine is on the table.

**Major transitions.** John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry, in 1995, listed the rare moments when the *unit* of evolution changed: replicating molecules into chromosomes, RNA into DNA-plus-protein, prokaryotes into eukaryotes, asexual clones into sexual populations, cells into multicellular bodies, individuals into colonies with castes, primates into talkers. Szathmáry later demoted sex and promoted plastids, because even the list evolves. The common plot: smaller entities become parts of a larger one, lose the ability to copy on their own, and a new level of selection appears — if, and only if, conflict among the parts can be suppressed. A body is a truce. A eukaryotic cell is a truce. Language is a truce with extra vocabulary.

We do not have a finished theory of when those truces hold. We can see that they were rare. We can see that they reorganised information. We cannot yet say they were inevitable, or lucky, or the sort of thing that happens on any wet rock given time. That is one reason [chapter 01](01-what-even-is-life.md) refused to close the definition, and one reason the alien chapters have to hunt a process rather than a familiar body.

Multicellularity, just to pick one: it happened more than once — animals, land plants, several algal lines, several fungal lines. The trick is not magic. Stick together, divide labour, police cheats (cells that would rather be a tumour than a tissue). The fact that it is repeatable does not make it cheap. Most of the tree never bothered. Most of the tree is still a cell that did not join a club.

**Evolvability.** Not "does it evolve," but "how able is this lineage to generate heritable variation that selection could use." A modular body plan, a genome that can duplicate genes and keep a spare, a sex that shuffles: these change the *future* of the engine, not just the present. Selection can, maybe, pick lineages that are good at evolving. That sentence makes philosophers and biologists reach for different knives. It might be true. It is not free.

**How much is selection versus constraint.** Gould and Lewontin again, and a literature that has not stopped: the paths a body can take are limited by development, by physics, by what the last generation already was. Selection picks among the available. The available is not "anything." A lot of "why this shape" may be "this is what the embryo could do" rather than "this is the optimum." The ratio is the fight. Neutral theory is the molecular version. Constraint is the morphological one. We do not have a single number. Anyone who says "it's all selection" or "it's all constraint" is selling a camp.

Time, then, does not march. It ramifies, merges, throws away kits, and occasionally invents a new kind of player. The engine is simple. The history is not. That is not a failure of the four parts. It is what four parts can do with four billion years and a planet that keeps changing the pitch.

## Still Unsolved 🕳️

- **How life started the engine.** Variation, heredity, selection, time — once you have them. The first copy with a difference is [chapter 16](16-mysteries-theories-and-the-unsolved.md)'s missing first act. LUCA already had the kit.
- **How much of molecular change is neutral.** Kimura's null is strong. The adaptive fraction of amino-acid substitutions is still a live estimate, not a constant.
- **When a new level of selection actually sticks.** Major transitions as a list: useful. As a predictive theory: unfinished.
- **How evolvability itself evolves**, and whether that sentence is allowed.
- **How much of form is selection versus constraint versus luck.** Spandrels, allometry, phylogenetic inertia: named. Not quantified as a planetary budget.
- **How to draw the tree when the tree is a web.** Two domains or three, how much HGT, where viruses sit. Chapter 16's map, still being redrawn.
- **Whether complexity is a trend or a drunkard's walk with a wall on the left** (you cannot get simpler than a certain chemistry, so the mean can drift up by accident). Gould liked the wall. Other people like the trend. One planet is a bad sample.
- **How often sexual selection and ecological selection pull in opposite directions**, and who wins. Peacocks are expensive. Expensive is allowed. Always-winning is not.
- **Whether the engine runs elsewhere.** That is Arcs 8 and 9. This chapter only claims it ran here, and that Joyce was right to put it in the definition as a pointing device, not as a proof that every living-looking stain has a Darwinian past.
